## 0.1 The Safeguarding Mindset and Mandatory Reporting

> [!tip] Consider it routinely — the way you consider sepsis
> **Safeguarding is not an accusation you level when something feels wrong. It is a CONSIDERATION you hold in every relevant presentation, in the same way you consider sepsis or malignancy — most of the time it is not the answer, and the discipline is in asking the question at all.**
> **The mindset that works: does the history explain the findings? Does it fit the developmental stage? Is anything about this interaction unusual? Who else is affected?**
> **The mindset that fails: waiting until you are certain.**

> [!danger] The threshold is reasonable suspicion — not proof, and you do not investigate
> **DOCTORS ARE MANDATED NOTIFIERS FOR CHILD ABUSE AND NEGLECT IN EVERY AUSTRALIAN JURISDICTION.**
> **THE THRESHOLD FOR REPORTING IS A SUSPICION ON REASONABLE GROUNDS — NOT CERTAINTY, NOT PROOF, AND NOT A COMPLETED INVESTIGATION.**
> **IT IS NOT YOUR JOB TO ESTABLISH WHETHER ABUSE OCCURRED. That is the statutory agency's role.** **Your job is to recognise, to report, to provide medical care and to document.**
> **Trying to be sure before reporting is the commonest reason reports are not made, and it is a misunderstanding of the obligation.**
> **THE PROTECTIONS: reporting in good faith attracts legal protection from liability and from breach-of-confidentiality claims, and the reporter's identity is protected.** **The obligation OVERRIDES confidentiality.**
> **FAILURE TO REPORT is an offence in most jurisdictions and is a professional conduct matter.**
> **In South Australia, reports are made to the Child Abuse Report Line.** `UNVERIFIED — the SA legislation, the reporting threshold as legally defined, the contact pathway, and the categories of reportable concern. Look these up now, and know where your health network's policy is kept.`

> [!warning] Four practical rules that apply across all of safeguarding
> **1. DO NOT MANAGE IT ALONE.** **Involve a senior clinician, social work, and your local child protection or safeguarding service early.** This is not a decision for a junior doctor at 3am on their own.
> **2. DO NOT PROMISE CONFIDENTIALITY YOU CANNOT KEEP.** **Say at the outset — particularly to an adolescent — that what is discussed is private unless you are worried someone is at serious risk of harm, and that if that happens you will tell them first and involve them in how it is done.** Cross-refer [[M7]] 0.3.
> **3. DOCUMENT AS IF IT WILL BE READ IN COURT — because it may be.** **Contemporaneous · factual · VERBATIM QUOTES in quotation marks for what was said · body maps and diagrams for injuries · measurements rather than comparisons to fruit · photographs only in accordance with local policy and consent · and a clear distinction between what you OBSERVED and what you were TOLD.**
> **4. THINK ABOUT THE OTHERS.** **Siblings and other children in the household. Children of an adult patient you are treating for substance use, mental illness or injury from violence. Other residents in a facility. The risk rarely attaches to one person alone.**

## 0.2 Recognising Child Abuse and Neglect

> [!danger] The injuries that should stop you
> **· BRUISING IN A NON-MOBILE INFANT.** **"If they don't cruise, they don't bruise."** **A baby who cannot yet roll, crawl or pull to stand does not acquire bruises accidentally. Any bruise in a pre-mobile infant requires explanation and, in most guidance, mandates referral.**
> **· BRUISES IN PROTECTED SITES — the EARS, NECK, TORSO, BUTTOCKS, INNER THIGHS and GENITALIA.** Accidental bruising in mobile children occurs over BONY PROMINENCES on the FRONT of the body — shins, knees, elbows, forehead. Protected, soft, concealed sites are different.
> **· PATTERNED INJURIES — the shape of an implement, a hand, a grip mark on the upper arms or chest, a ligature mark, or a BITE MARK.**
> **· BURNS with a sharp demarcation, a glove-and-stocking or immersion distribution, symmetrical burns of the buttocks or feet, or discrete circular burns.**
> **· A TORN FRENULUM in a non-mobile infant.**
> **· FRACTURES with high specificity: METAPHYSEAL CORNER or "bucket-handle" fractures · POSTERIOR RIB fractures · scapular, sternal and spinous process fractures · MULTIPLE fractures of DIFFERENT AGES · and any long bone fracture in a NON-AMBULANT child.** Cross-refer [[L7]] 0.5.
> **· ABUSIVE HEAD TRAUMA — the combination of subdural haemorrhages, retinal haemorrhages and encephalopathy.** **It presents as a seizure, apnoea, vomiting, irritability, poor feeding or collapse — frequently with NO history of trauma offered at all.** **It is a leading cause of death from child abuse, and the presenting complaint is often something else entirely.** Cross-refer [[M1]] 0.4 and [[M3]] 0.5.

> [!danger] The history matters as much as the injury
> **· A history that is INCONSISTENT with the injury, or that CHANGES between tellings or between carers.**
> **· A history INCONSISTENT WITH THE CHILD'S DEVELOPMENTAL STAGE — an injury attributed to an action the child cannot yet perform. This is one of the most objective and useful discriminators available.**
> **· UNEXPLAINED injury, or an absent, vague or evasive explanation.**
> **· DELAY in seeking care without adequate reason.**
> **· Injury attributed to a sibling, a pet or a third party.**
> **· Repeated presentations with injuries.**
> **· A child's demeanour that does not fit — excessive wariness, frozen watchfulness, or indiscriminate affection toward strangers.**

> [!warning] Neglect is the commonest form and the most under-recognised
> **It is under-recognised because it is CUMULATIVE and undramatic — there is no single event, so no single moment triggers concern.**
> **Features: FALTERING GROWTH not explained by illness · poor hygiene · inadequate or inappropriate clothing · untreated MEDICAL AND DENTAL problems · repeatedly missed appointments and immunisations · developmental delay from lack of stimulation · poor school attendance · supervisory neglect (a young child repeatedly presenting with preventable injuries or ingestions) · and emotional unavailability.**
> **THE CAUTION THAT MATTERS: POVERTY IS NOT NEGLECT.** **Families experiencing severe material hardship, homelessness or food insecurity may present with features that overlap, and the response to that is SUPPORT — social work, financial and housing referral, practical help — not a child protection report on its own.** **The distinction is whether the child's needs are being met and whether the parents are able and willing to use the help available. Where genuine doubt exists, discuss it with a senior clinician and with child protection services rather than deciding alone in either direction.**

> [!danger] Most sexually abused children have a normal examination
> **A NORMAL GENITAL AND ANAL EXAMINATION DOES NOT EXCLUDE SEXUAL ABUSE — and this is one of the most important facts in the area.** **Most abuse leaves no physical trace, and tissue heals rapidly.**
> **So a normal examination must never be reported to a family or documented as evidence that nothing happened.**
> **THE PRESENTATIONS: a DISCLOSURE by the child — which is the single most important piece of evidence there is · a sexually transmitted infection in a child · pregnancy in a young adolescent · anogenital injury · sexualised behaviour inappropriate for the developmental stage · new-onset enuresis or encopaosis · recurrent unexplained abdominal pain · self-harm, eating disturbance or sudden behavioural or school change.**
> **A CHILD'S DISCLOSURE MUST BE TAKEN SERIOUSLY AND ACTED ON.** Children rarely fabricate abuse, and retraction is common and does not indicate falsehood.
> **FABRICATED OR INDUCED ILLNESS is a form of abuse and is covered in [[N6]] 0.6.**

> [!tip] Consider the medical differentials — and document the one that causes most trouble
> **Before concluding, consider: BLEEDING DISORDERS (including von Willebrand disease and vitamin K deficiency bleeding in an infant — cross-refer [[J3]] 0.2 and [[M3]] 0.3) · OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA and metabolic bone disease including rickets · birthmarks and vascular malformations · coagulopathy from liver disease · and certain traditional or cultural practices such as cupping and coining, which produce characteristic marks and are not abuse.**
> **AND DOCUMENT CONGENITAL DERMAL MELANOCYTOSIS ("Mongolian spots") EXPLICITLY — site, size and appearance — in the newborn or early infant record.** **These blue-grey macules over the sacrum and buttocks are common in babies with darker skin, are entirely benign, and are REPEATEDLY AND DISTRESSINGLY MISTAKEN FOR BRUISING, leading to unnecessary and traumatic child protection referrals.** **A clear early note prevents that.** Cross-refer [[M3]] 0.6.
> **Considering these differentials is part of good practice, not a reason to delay a report where suspicion remains — both can and should happen in parallel.**

## 0.3 Responding to a Child Protection Concern

> [!danger] What to do — in order
> **1. THE CHILD'S IMMEDIATE SAFETY AND MEDICAL NEEDS COME FIRST.** Treat the injury, resuscitate, admit if needed. **Admission is a legitimate way of ensuring safety while things are sorted out.**
> **2. INVOLVE SENIORS EARLY** — the consultant, the paediatric team, social work, and the child protection service.
> **3. REPORT, on reasonable suspicion.**
> **4. DOCUMENT contemporaneously and factually, with verbatim quotes and a body map.**
> **5. INVESTIGATE MEDICALLY as directed by the paediatric team — which may include a SKELETAL SURVEY (to find occult and healing fractures of different ages), NEUROIMAGING, OPHTHALMOLOGICAL examination for retinal haemorrhages, coagulation studies, and bone biochemistry.**
> **6. CONSIDER THE SIBLINGS and other children in the household — they need assessment too.**

> [!danger] Three things not to do
> **1. DO NOT CONDUCT A FORENSIC INTERVIEW.** **Detailed questioning of a child about abuse is a specialist skill performed in a specific setting for a reason: repeated questioning CONTAMINATES the evidence, exposes the child to suggestion, and RE-TRAUMATISES them.**
> **If a child begins to disclose, LISTEN. Use open, non-leading prompts only — "tell me more about that", "what happened next" — do not ask leading or closed questions, do not press for detail, do not express shock, and RECORD THEIR WORDS VERBATIM.** **Then stop and hand over to the specialists.**
> **2. DO NOT CONFRONT OR ACCUSE THE CARER.** **It risks the child's immediate safety, it may prompt the family to disengage or remove the child from care, and it can compromise the statutory investigation.** **You are not conducting an investigation.**
> **3. DO NOT DELAY IN ORDER TO BE CERTAIN.** See 0.1.

> [!tip] What to say to the family
> **Be honest, neutral and non-accusatory. Concealment damages trust and is usually discovered anyway.**
> **A usable form of words: "I've found an injury that I can't fully explain. When that happens, I'm required by law to talk to child protection services — that's a legal requirement for all doctors and it isn't an accusation. I want to be upfront with you that I'm doing it."**
> **Framing it as a legal obligation applying to everyone, rather than as a judgement about this family, allows the relationship to survive it — and the family will need medical care afterwards regardless of the outcome.**
> **Exception: do not disclose your intention to report where doing so would place the child at increased immediate risk — take senior advice.**
> **AND LOOK AFTER YOURSELF AND YOUR TEAM.** **These presentations are distressing, they generate doubt and guilt in both directions, and debriefing with a senior colleague is appropriate rather than weak.** Cross-refer [[EBM1]] 0.6.

## 0.4 Family and Domestic Violence

> [!info] It is a pattern of control, not a series of assaults
> **Physical violence is one component and often not the dominant one. The defining feature is COERCIVE CONTROL — a pattern of behaviour that dominates and restricts another person.**
> **It includes: EMOTIONAL abuse and degradation · FINANCIAL control (restricting access to money, sabotaging employment, accumulating debt in the person's name) · SOCIAL ISOLATION from family and friends · monitoring and TECHNOLOGY-FACILITATED abuse (tracking, accessing accounts and devices) · REPRODUCTIVE COERCION (interfering with contraception, forcing or preventing pregnancy — worth asking about specifically in a sexual health consultation, cross-refer [[O6]] 0.3) · threats to children, pets or immigration status · sexual violence · and stalking.**
> **It is COMMON, it occurs across all socioeconomic, cultural and educational groups, it affects people of all genders, and it is a leading contributor to the burden of disease in women of reproductive age in Australia.**
> **VIOLENCE FREQUENTLY BEGINS OR ESCALATES DURING PREGNANCY AND AFTER THE BIRTH — which makes antenatal and postnatal contacts important opportunities to ask.** Cross-refer [[O2]] and [[O3]].

> [!warning] How it presents in a clinic
> **Rarely as a disclosure. Usually as something else:**
> **· Injuries that do not fit the explanation, or repeated injuries.**
> **· CHRONIC PAIN, headaches, gastrointestinal symptoms and other persistent somatic complaints.**
> **· Anxiety, depression, self-harm, substance use.**
> **· Sexual and reproductive health problems, repeated terminations, poor antenatal attendance.**
> **· Frequent attendance, or repeatedly missed appointments (which may reflect control rather than disengagement).**
> **· A PARTNER WHO ANSWERS FOR THE PATIENT, WILL NOT LEAVE THE ROOM, INSISTS ON INTERPRETING, OR CONTROLS THE CONVERSATION.** **That behaviour is itself information.**

> [!danger] Ask alone — and never with the partner in the room
> **YOU CANNOT ASK ABOUT VIOLENCE WITH THE PERSON'S PARTNER PRESENT.** **Asking in front of them can escalate the risk after they leave, and it will not produce a truthful answer.**
> **Create a plausible, routine reason to separate them: "I see everyone on their own for part of the consultation", taking them for a test or to be weighed, or asking the accompanying person to complete paperwork.**
> **USE A PROFESSIONAL INTERPRETER, NEVER A FAMILY MEMBER, FRIEND OR THE PARTNER.**
> **HOW TO ASK: normalise it, ask directly, and do not use euphemism.**
> **"Because violence at home is common and affects people's health, I ask everyone about it. Is anyone at home hurting you, frightening you, or controlling what you do?"**
> **Framing it as routine removes the implication that you have singled them out because of how they look or who they are with.**

> [!danger] Responding — and you are not required to fix it
> **· BELIEVE THEM, AND SAY SO.** The most important things you can say are simple: **"I believe you." "This is not your fault." "You don't deserve this." "I'm concerned about your safety." "Help is available."**
> **· ASSESS IMMEDIATE SAFETY AND RISK.** The recognised high-risk indicators include: **escalating frequency or severity · THREATS TO KILL · a HISTORY OF NON-FATAL STRANGULATION (which is one of the strongest single predictors of subsequent homicide and must be asked about specifically and taken extremely seriously, both as a risk marker and because it causes delayed neurological and airway injury) · access to WEAPONS · stalking and obsessive behaviour · SEPARATION OR PLANNED SEPARATION · pregnancy · sexual violence · threats to children or pets · and the person's own assessment that they are in danger, which is a good predictor.**
> **· THE PERIOD AROUND LEAVING IS THE HIGHEST-RISK PERIOD.** **This is why "why doesn't she just leave" is both a naive and a dangerous question — leaving is when people are killed.**
> **· REFER — 1800RESPECT and local specialist family violence services, police where there is immediate danger, and legal services for intervention orders.** `UNVERIFIED — current services and SA pathways.`
> **· RESPECT THEIR AUTONOMY.** **Leaving is a PROCESS, not an event, and most people make several attempts before it is permanent.** **Someone who chooses to return is not failing, and expressing frustration or issuing ultimatums guarantees they will not disclose again — to you or to anyone.** **Your role is to keep the door open: document, offer, and be there next time.**
> **· DOCUMENT CAREFULLY** — factually, with verbatim quotes and body maps, because the record may be used in legal proceedings. **And be aware that in some circumstances the perpetrator may gain access to records, which is a reason for care about what is recorded where.**
> **· MANDATORY REPORTING APPLIES WHERE CHILDREN ARE INVOLVED.** **Children EXPOSED to family violence are children at risk, whether or not they are physically harmed, and this is a reportable concern.**
> **· DO NOT recommend couple counselling or mediation where there is violence — it is unsafe. DO NOT attempt to confront or counsel the perpetrator.**

> [!tip] The groups facing additional barriers
> **· ABORIGINAL AND TORRES STRAIT ISLANDER women experience family violence at markedly higher rates, and face specific barriers — including a well-founded FEAR OF CHILD REMOVAL grounded in the history of the Stolen Generations, and distrust of police and statutory services.** **Involve Aboriginal Health Workers and Aboriginal-controlled services, and be explicit about what will and will not be reported.** Cross-refer [[AU1]] 0.2–0.3.
> **· People on temporary or dependent VISAS, where the perpetrator controls their immigration status.**
> **· People with DISABILITY, who may be dependent on the perpetrator for care.**
> **· LGBTIQA+ people, who may fear being outed or not believed.**
> **· OLDER people — see 0.6. · Men, who are under-identified and under-served. · Rural and remote communities, where anonymity is impossible and services are distant.**

## 0.5 Sexual Assault

> [!danger] The person's choices govern everything
> **The single organising principle: an assault is an experience of having control taken away, so THE RESPONSE MUST RETURN CONTROL WHEREVER POSSIBLE.** **Every step is offered and explained, and the person decides.**
> **THE IMMEDIATE PRIORITIES: safety · urgent medical needs · privacy and a supportive environment · and being believed.**
> **WHAT NOT TO DO: do not pressure them to report to police · do not ask "why" questions ("why did you go there", "why didn't you tell anyone") · do not express doubt, surprise or judgement · and do not attempt a forensic examination unless you are specifically trained and it is appropriate.**

> [!warning] Refer to a specialist service — and care is not conditional on reporting
> **SPECIALIST SEXUAL ASSAULT SERVICES provide integrated forensic examination, medical care and counselling by clinicians trained for it.** **In South Australia this is Yarrow Place.** `UNVERIFIED — the service, its scope and the referral pathway.`
> **TWO THINGS THAT MUST BE SAID CLEARLY TO THE PERSON:**
> **1. FORENSIC EVIDENCE IS TIME-LIMITED — so there is a reason not to delay if they might want that option — and evidence can often be collected and STORED without a decision about police being made immediately.**
> **2. MEDICAL CARE IS NOT CONDITIONAL ON REPORTING TO POLICE.** **A person can receive full medical care, treatment and follow-up without making a report, and can change their mind later.** **Many people believe they must report in order to be treated, and telling them otherwise removes a significant barrier to seeking care.**

> [!tip] The medical care
> **· INJURIES — assessed and treated.**
> **· EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION, and pregnancy testing.** Cross-refer [[O6]] 0.3.
> **· HIV POST-EXPOSURE PROPHYLAXIS — TIME-CRITICAL, and the window is short. It must be considered immediately rather than deferred to a follow-up appointment.** Cross-refer [[K3]] 0.5.
> **· STI testing and presumptive treatment, hepatitis B vaccination and immunoglobulin where indicated.**
> **· FOLLOW-UP — repeat serology at the appropriate intervals, and mental health support.**
> **· Consider DRUG-FACILITATED assault, where the person has amnesia or an unexplained level of impairment — relevant to both the medical assessment and the time-limited toxicology.**
> **· MANDATORY REPORTING obligations apply if the person is a child or a young person below the relevant age, or where other statutory criteria are met.** `UNVERIFIED — the SA provisions, including in relation to age and to power imbalance.`

> [!warning] Responding to a historic disclosure
> **Disclosures of past sexual abuse or assault — sometimes decades old — are COMMON in clinical settings, and are often prompted by a related consultation: a cervical screening test, an examination, a pregnancy, a child reaching the age the person was.**
> **HOW TO RESPOND WELL, in a few sentences:**
> **· THANK THEM for telling you, and acknowledge that it was hard to say.**
> **· BELIEVE THEM explicitly.**
> **· DO NOT PRESS FOR DETAIL — you do not need the narrative, and asking for it can be harmful.**
> **· ASK WHAT THEY NEED FROM YOU, and what would make the current consultation easier.**
> **· OFFER support and referral, without insisting.**
> **· RECORD IT (with their agreement) so that they do not have to disclose again to the next clinician, and so that future examinations can be approached appropriately.**
> **The consultation may well be able to continue — the point is that it continues on their terms.** Cross-refer [[N5]] 0.5 and [[O5]] 0.4.

## 0.6 Elder Abuse and Adults at Risk

> [!danger] The key difference from child protection is capacity
> **An adult WITH CAPACITY has the right to make decisions others consider unwise — including remaining in a situation you regard as abusive.**
> **This is the fundamental distinction from child protection, and it is uncomfortable.**
> **Your role with a capable adult is to: ensure they know what is happening is not acceptable · provide information and offer help · address any coercion or control limiting their real choice · maintain the relationship so the offer stands · and document.**
> **Where CAPACITY IS IMPAIRED, different frameworks apply — guardianship and administration, substitute decision-making, and adult safeguarding legislation.** Cross-refer [[A10]] 0.1–0.3.
> **SOUTH AUSTRALIA HAS ADULT SAFEGUARDING LEGISLATION AND AN ADULT SAFEGUARDING UNIT, with provisions for reporting concerns about adults at risk.** `UNVERIFIED — the scope, the reporting obligations (which may be mandatory for some groups), the age and eligibility criteria, and the referral pathway. This differs between states and must be checked.`

> [!warning] The types, and the one that is commonest
> **· FINANCIAL ABUSE — the most common form, and the most under-recognised.** **Misuse of an enduring power of attorney · pressure to change a will or transfer property · "inheritance impatience" · taking pension money · accumulating debt in the person's name · selling possessions · and the "granny flat" arrangement where an older person transfers assets in exchange for care that does not materialise.**
> **· PSYCHOLOGICAL — threats, humiliation, isolation from family and friends, threats of residential placement.**
> **· NEGLECT — withholding food, medication, aids, continence care or medical attention.**
> **· PHYSICAL and SEXUAL abuse.**
> **THE PERPETRATOR IS MOST OFTEN A FAMILY MEMBER, and frequently the primary carer** — which is what makes recognition and response so difficult, and why the person may be reluctant to disclose.

> [!tip] What to look for
> **· Unexplained injuries, patterned bruising, injuries in protected sites.**
> **· Poor hygiene, pressure injuries, malnutrition, dehydration.**
> **· Medication problems — unfilled prescriptions, or conversely OVER-SEDATION.**
> **· Missed appointments, delayed presentation, and repeated attendance with vague complaints.**
> **· FINANCIAL indicators: unpaid bills despite adequate income · a new signatory on accounts · sudden changes to a will or power of attorney · missing possessions · a carer with unexplained financial dependence on the older person.**
> **· BEHAVIOURAL indicators: fearfulness, withdrawal, deference to the carer, reluctance to speak in their presence.**
> **· AND THE CARER'S BEHAVIOUR: answering for the person, refusing to leave the room, appearing hostile or indifferent, or showing signs of severe strain.**
> **SEE THE PERSON ALONE. As with family violence, this is not optional, and a plausible routine reason should be created.**

> [!warning] Responding — and the carer dimension
> **· ASSESS CAPACITY, and be specific about which decision.**
> **· ASSESS RISK and immediate safety.**
> **· INVOLVE THE PERSON in decisions to the greatest extent their capacity allows — being "protected" against one's wishes is itself a harm.**
> **· Escalate appropriately: senior clinician, social work, the ADULT SAFEGUARDING pathway, guardianship and administration frameworks (in South Australia through SACAT), police for criminal matters, and the aged care complaints mechanism for concerns in residential or home care.** `UNVERIFIED — the SA pathways.`
> **· DOCUMENT factually.**
> **AND THE NUANCE THAT MATTERS CLINICALLY: SOME ELDER ABUSE ARISES FROM CARER BURNOUT rather than malice — an exhausted, unsupported, untrained carer managing dementia-related behaviours with no respite and no help.**
> **This does not excuse harm, and safety comes first — but it changes the response.** **Providing carer support, respite, education, home care packages and treatment for the carer's own health can be genuinely protective, and is sometimes the most effective intervention available.**
> **ASK THE CARER HOW THEY ARE, separately and meaning it.** Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.3 and [[D2]] 0.3.
> **The same principles extend to other ADULTS AT RISK — people with intellectual disability, acquired brain injury or severe mental illness, and people in institutional care — who face elevated rates of abuse and reduced ability to report it.**

**Ix:** **SEE THE PERSON ALONE — in every case of suspected child, family or elder abuse** (*why:* disclosure will not occur with the alleged perpetrator present, and asking in front of them may increase the risk afterwards; *what:* the disclosure). **A PROFESSIONAL INTERPRETER, never a family member and never a child** (*why:* family members filter, omit and may be implicated; *what:* an accurate account). **CONTEMPORANEOUS, FACTUAL DOCUMENTATION with verbatim quotes, body maps and measurements** (*why:* it becomes evidence, and it must distinguish observation from report; *what:* the record). **SKELETAL SURVEY, neuroimaging and OPHTHALMOLOGICAL examination for retinal haemorrhages, as directed by the paediatric team** (*why:* identifies occult fractures of different ages and abusive head trauma; *what:* the injury pattern). **Coagulation studies and bone biochemistry** (*why:* excludes the medical differentials, which should be considered in parallel with — not instead of — the safeguarding response; *what:* bleeding disorder, metabolic bone disease). **Assessment for NON-FATAL STRANGULATION where family violence is disclosed** (*why:* it is a major predictor of subsequent homicide AND causes delayed airway and neurological injury; *what:* the history, and the physical findings). **HIV POST-EXPOSURE PROPHYLAXIS assessment immediately after sexual assault** (*why:* the window is short and deferring it to follow-up loses the opportunity; *what:* eligibility). **CAPACITY ASSESSMENT, decision-specific, in suspected elder abuse** (*why:* it determines whether the person's own choice governs or a substitute framework applies; *what:* capacity for the specific decision). **REVIEW OF THE OTHER PEOPLE IN THE HOUSEHOLD** (*why:* siblings, other children and other dependent adults are at risk and are routinely not considered; *what:* who else is exposed).
