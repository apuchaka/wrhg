## 0.1 Assessing the Red Eye

> [!danger] Measure the visual acuity — it is the vital sign of the eye
> **THE MOST COMMONLY OMITTED STEP IN THE ASSESSMENT OF AN EYE, AND THE MOST IMPORTANT ONE.**
> **Use a Snellen chart at the correct distance, WITH the patient's glasses on, testing each eye separately with the other properly covered.**
> **If the acuity is reduced, RETEST WITH A PINHOLE** — a pinhole corrects refractive error, so **improvement with a pinhole means the problem is refractive (glasses), and NO improvement means the problem is in the eye itself.** That single manoeuvre separates a large proportion of presentations.
> **Document it as a number. "Vision seems OK" is not an acuity.**
> **In a child or a non-verbal patient, use age-appropriate tests, or at minimum assess fixation and following, and compare the two eyes.**

> [!danger] The red flags — any of these means same-day ophthalmology
> **· REDUCED VISUAL ACUITY.**
> **· TRUE PAIN — a deep, aching, boring pain, as opposed to grittiness or a foreign body sensation.**
> **· PHOTOPHOBIA.**
> **· AN ABNORMAL PUPIL — fixed, irregular, poorly reactive, or a relative afferent pupillary defect.**
> **· CILIARY FLUSH — injection concentrated in a ring around the cornea.**
> **· A HAZY OR OPAQUE CORNEA, or a visible corneal lesion.**
> **· NAUSEA AND VOMITING with a red eye.**
> **· CONTACT LENS WEAR.**
> **· TRAUMA, particularly high-velocity, or any CHEMICAL exposure.**
> **· Recent intraocular surgery or injection.**
> **· Immunosuppression.**
> **A red eye WITHOUT any of these — good acuity, no true pain, no photophobia, normal pupil, diffuse injection — is very unlikely to be sight-threatening.**

> [!tip] The injection pattern tells you where the problem is
> **· DIFFUSE conjunctival injection, most marked in the fornices and fading towards the cornea → CONJUNCTIVITIS.**
> **· CILIARY FLUSH (circumcorneal injection) — a violaceous ring of injection concentrated AT THE LIMBUS → KERATITIS, ANTERIOR UVEITIS or ACUTE GLAUCOMA. All three are sight-threatening.**
> **· SECTORAL injection in one segment → EPISCLERITIS or SCLERITIS.**
> **Learning to see the difference between diffuse and circumcorneal injection is one of the more useful skills in general practice.**

> [!warning] Fluorescein, and two prescribing prohibitions
> **PUT FLUORESCEIN IN EVERY RED PAINFUL EYE AND LOOK WITH A BLUE LIGHT.**
> **It reveals: corneal ABRASIONS · ULCERS · the DENDRITIC pattern of herpes simplex keratitis · foreign bodies · and vertical linear abrasions indicating a subtarsal foreign body.** **It takes seconds and it finds the diagnoses that matter.**
> **PROHIBITION 1: DO NOT PRESCRIBE TOPICAL CORTICOSTEROIDS FOR A RED EYE WITHOUT SLIT LAMP ASSESSMENT.**
> **In HERPES SIMPLEX KERATITIS, topical steroids cause the dendritic ulcer to enlarge into a geographic ulcer, with corneal melting, perforation and permanent scarring.** They also cause steroid-induced glaucoma and cataract with prolonged use, and worsen bacterial and fungal keratitis.
> **PROHIBITION 2: NEVER GIVE A PATIENT TOPICAL ANAESTHETIC TO TAKE HOME.**
> **It is enormously tempting because it abolishes the pain instantly — but repeated use impairs corneal epithelial healing, masks worsening damage, and causes corneal melting and permanent visual loss.** Use it once to permit examination, and no more.

## 0.2 The Sight-Threatening Causes

> [!danger] A contact lens wearer with a red painful eye is an emergency
> **MICROBIAL KERATITIS in a contact lens wearer can destroy the cornea within days.**
> **· *PSEUDOMONAS* — aggressive, rapidly progressive, associated with soft lenses, overnight wear and poor hygiene.**
> **· *ACANTHAMOEBA* — associated with WATER EXPOSURE (showering, swimming or rinsing lenses in tap water), and characterised by PAIN THAT IS SEVERE AND OUT OF PROPORTION to the signs.** It is frequently misdiagnosed as herpes simplex for weeks, and it causes devastating visual loss.
> **THE RULES: REMOVE THE LENS · DO NOT PATCH THE EYE (patching a contaminated cornea accelerates infection) · DO NOT prescribe steroids · KEEP THE LENS AND THE CASE for culture · and refer for SAME-DAY ophthalmology assessment.**
> **A white or hazy spot on the cornea in a lens wearer is an ulcer until proven otherwise.**

> [!warning] Herpetic eye disease
> **· HERPES SIMPLEX KERATITIS — the DENDRITIC ULCER on fluorescein staining is characteristic: a branching, linear ulcer with terminal bulbs.** **Reduced corneal sensation is a supporting sign.** **Treated with topical antivirals; STEROIDS ARE CONTRAINDICATED unless given by an ophthalmologist alongside antiviral cover.** It recurs, and recurrent episodes cause scarring and visual loss.
> **· HERPES ZOSTER OPHTHALMICUS — shingles in the V1 dermatome.** **HUTCHINSON SIGN — vesicles on the TIP, SIDE OR ROOT OF THE NOSE — indicates nasociliary nerve involvement and predicts ocular involvement, because the same nerve supplies the globe.** **It warrants ophthalmology assessment even before eye symptoms appear.** Systemic antivirals, started early. Cross-refer [[G1]] 0.5.

> [!danger] Anterior uveitis
> **Deep, aching pain · PHOTOPHOBIA · redness with CILIARY FLUSH · blurred vision · and a small, poorly reactive or irregular pupil (from posterior synechiae).**
> **CONSENSUAL PHOTOPHOBIA IS THE USEFUL BEDSIDE SIGN: shining light into the UNAFFECTED eye causes pain in the AFFECTED one, because the consensual pupillary constriction moves the inflamed iris.** **It distinguishes uveitis from conjunctivitis at the bedside.**
> **On slit lamp: cells and flare in the anterior chamber, keratic precipitates, and in severe cases a HYPOPYON.**
> **THE ASSOCIATIONS MATTER, because uveitis may be the presenting feature of systemic disease: HLA-B27-associated conditions (ANKYLOSING SPONDYLITIS, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease) · SARCOIDOSIS · TUBERCULOSIS · syphilis · herpes viruses · Behçet disease · and JUVENILE IDIOPATHIC ARTHRITIS.**
> **In JIA the uveitis is CHRONIC AND ASYMPTOMATIC — a white, painless eye — which is why children with oligoarticular ANA-positive JIA require scheduled slit lamp SCREENING regardless of symptoms.** Cross-refer [[M5]] 0.6 and [[L2]] 0.3.
> **Treated with topical steroid and a CYCLOPLEGIC (which relieves the ciliary spasm causing the pain and prevents synechiae) — under ophthalmology.**

> [!danger] Scleritis versus episcleritis — the phenylephrine test
> **· EPISCLERITIS — mild discomfort or none, SECTORAL redness, no visual loss, and the vessels BLANCH with topical PHENYLEPHRINE (a vasoconstrictor) because they are superficial and mobile. Benign and self-limiting.**
> **· SCLERITIS — SEVERE, BORING, DEEP pain that WAKES THE PATIENT AT NIGHT and radiates to the brow, jaw or temple · a globe that is TENDER to palpation through the closed lid · a VIOLACEOUS or bluish hue best seen in daylight · and vessels that DO NOT BLANCH with phenylephrine.**
> **Scleritis can cause scleral thinning, perforation and visual loss, requires SYSTEMIC immunosuppression rather than topical treatment, and is ASSOCIATED WITH SYSTEMIC DISEASE in a large proportion — rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis and other vasculitides, and inflammatory bowel disease.**
> **A NEW SCLERITIS WARRANTS A SEARCH FOR SYSTEMIC VASCULITIS, and necrotising scleritis in rheumatoid arthritis is a marker of severe systemic disease.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.4–0.5.
> **The pain is the discriminator patients describe best: episcleritis is uncomfortable, scleritis hurts enough to stop them sleeping.**

> [!warning] Endophthalmitis and the post-operative eye
> **Infection within the eye — following cataract surgery, INTRAVITREAL INJECTION (now a very common procedure for macular disease, so these patients are numerous), penetrating trauma, or haematogenous seeding in an unwell patient.**
> **Features: increasing PAIN and REDUCED VISION in the days after a procedure · hypopyon · lid swelling · and a poor red reflex.**
> **ANY PATIENT WITH INCREASING PAIN OR FALLING VISION AFTER INTRAOCULAR SURGERY OR INJECTION NEEDS SAME-DAY OPHTHALMOLOGY — do not attribute it to normal post-operative discomfort.** Vision is lost within hours to days.
> **ENDOGENOUS endophthalmitis occurs in septic patients, people who inject drugs, and those with indwelling lines or candidaemia — so a red painful eye in a septic patient is not conjunctivitis until proven otherwise.**

## 0.3 Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma

> [!danger] It presents with vomiting and gets diagnosed as gastroenteritis
> **THE PRESENTATION: severe UNILATERAL eye and periorbital PAIN · HEADACHE · blurred vision with HALOES AROUND LIGHTS · and prominent NAUSEA AND VOMITING.**
> **THE VOMITING IS WHAT CAUSES THE MISDIAGNOSIS.** **Patients are worked up for gastroenteritis, migraine, or an acute abdomen — while the eye goes blind over hours.**
> **THE SIGNS: a red eye with CILIARY FLUSH · a HAZY, CLOUDY CORNEA (from oedema — the iris details look blurred) · a MID-DILATED, OVAL, FIXED, UNREACTIVE PUPIL · markedly reduced acuity · and an eye that feels HARD compared with the other on gentle palpation through the closed lid.**
> **THE RULE: ANY OLDER PATIENT WITH A RED PAINFUL EYE AND VOMITING HAS ANGLE CLOSURE UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE — and any patient with a headache should have their eyes looked at.**

> [!warning] Risk factors and the drugs that precipitate it
> **PREDISPOSING: HYPERMETROPIA (long-sightedness, giving a shallow anterior chamber) · increasing AGE · FEMALE sex · East and Southeast ASIAN ancestry · Inuit ancestry · a family history · and a previous episode in the other eye.**
> **PRECIPITANTS — anything that dilates the pupil to a mid-dilated position: DIM LIGHT (classically presenting in the evening or at the cinema) · emotional stress · and DRUGS.**
> **THE DRUG LIST IS CLINICALLY IMPORTANT: topical MYDRIATICS · ANTICHOLINERGICS (including oxybutynin, benztropine, hyoscine and many others) · TRICYCLIC ANTIDEPRESSANTS · some ANTIHISTAMINES · SYMPATHOMIMETICS including decongestants · SSRIs and SNRIs · and TOPIRAMATE (which causes a distinct idiosyncratic bilateral angle closure through ciliary body swelling, usually within the first weeks of starting).**
> **A patient with a shallow anterior chamber prescribed one of these can present with acute closure — which is why "eye pain after starting a new medication" deserves attention.**

> [!tip] Management
> **URGENT ophthalmology referral — this is measured in hours.**
> **While arranging it: LOWER THE INTRAOCULAR PRESSURE with topical agents and systemic acetazolamide · PILOCARPINE to constrict the pupil and pull the iris out of the angle (though it works poorly at very high pressures) · lie the patient SUPINE (which may allow the lens to fall back) · and give ANALGESIA and ANTIEMETICS, because the pain and vomiting are severe and vomiting raises the pressure further.**
> **DEFINITIVE treatment is LASER PERIPHERAL IRIDOTOMY — and it is performed on BOTH eyes, because the fellow eye has the same anatomy and a high risk of subsequent closure.** `UNVERIFIED — all agents and doses.`
> **Distinguish this from the far commoner CHRONIC OPEN-ANGLE GLAUCOMA, which is painless, insidious, causes peripheral field loss the patient does not notice, and is detected on screening.** Cross-refer [[E2]].

## 0.4 Conjunctivitis

> [!tip] The three common types, and the symptom that separates them
> **· BACTERIAL — PURULENT, sticky discharge, lids stuck together in the morning, often starting unilaterally then becoming bilateral. Mostly SELF-LIMITING; topical antibiotics shorten it modestly and are frequently unnecessary.**
> **· VIRAL (usually adenovirus) — WATERY discharge, follicular conjunctival reaction, a TENDER PREAURICULAR LYMPH NODE (a useful and specific sign), often with a recent upper respiratory infection.** **HIGHLY CONTAGIOUS — hand hygiene, separate towels, and exclusion from work or school matter, and outbreaks occur in clinics and schools.** It may last weeks and can be complicated by keratitis.
> **· ALLERGIC — ITCH IS THE DOMINANT SYMPTOM, and itch is the discriminator. Bilateral, with chemosis, watery discharge, papillae, and usually a history of atopy or a seasonal pattern.** Treated with antihistamine or mast cell stabiliser drops and allergen avoidance.
> **"Sticky" suggests bacterial, "watery with a node" suggests viral, and "itchy" suggests allergic.**

> [!danger] Two conjunctivitis presentations that are emergencies
> **1. GONOCOCCAL CONJUNCTIVITIS — HYPERACUTE onset over 12 to 24 hours, with COPIOUS purulent discharge that reaccumulates immediately after wiping, marked lid swelling and chemosis.**
> **IT CAN PENETRATE AN INTACT CORNEA AND PERFORATE IT WITHIN DAYS.**
> **It requires SYSTEMIC antibiotics (not topical alone), urgent ophthalmology, and full STI screening and partner notification.** Cross-refer [[O6]] 0.2.
> **2. OPHTHALMIA NEONATORUM — conjunctivitis in the first month of life.** **Purulent discharge in the FIRST FEW DAYS suggests GONOCOCCAL infection and is sight-threatening; onset around days 5 to 14 suggests CHLAMYDIA, which also causes a pneumonitis at 4 to 12 weeks.** **Both need SYSTEMIC treatment, notification, and treatment of the mother and her partner.** Cross-refer [[M3]] 0.6.
> **CHLAMYDIAL conjunctivitis in adults — chronic, follicular, unilateral or bilateral, in sexually active young people, unresponsive to standard drops. Needs systemic treatment and STI screening.**

> [!warning] When "conjunctivitis" is the wrong diagnosis
> **Conjunctivitis should be a diagnosis of comfort: normal acuity, no true pain, no photophobia, a normal pupil, and diffuse injection.**
> **RECONSIDER IF: the acuity is reduced · there is real PAIN rather than grittiness · there is PHOTOPHOBIA · there is CILIARY FLUSH · the cornea is hazy or fluorescein-positive · the pupil is abnormal · it is strictly unilateral and severe · the patient wears CONTACT LENSES · or it is not settling as expected.**
> **"Conjunctivitis not responding to chloramphenicol" is a phrase that should prompt re-examination rather than a stronger antibiotic** — it is frequently keratitis, uveitis, or something worse.

## 0.5 Chemical Injury, Trauma and Foreign Bodies

> [!danger] Chemical injury — irrigate before you do anything else
> **THIS IS THE ONE OPHTHALMIC EMERGENCY WHERE TREATMENT PRECEDES ASSESSMENT.**
> **DO NOT take a history first. DO NOT measure the acuity first. DO NOT wait for ophthalmology. IRRIGATE IMMEDIATELY.**
> **· Topical anaesthetic to allow irrigation · copious irrigation with saline (or water if saline is not immediately available) for a PROLONGED period · EVERT THE LIDS and sweep the fornices to remove particulate matter, which continues to release chemical if left · and CHECK THE pH, continuing until it normalises and rechecking after a delay.**
> **ALKALI IS WORSE THAN ACID.** **Alkali (cement, lime, plaster, oven cleaner, drain cleaner, ammonia) causes LIQUEFACTIVE necrosis and penetrates DEEPLY into the eye, continuing to damage after the surface exposure ends.** **Acid causes coagulative necrosis, which forms a barrier limiting penetration.**
> **PARADOXICALLY, A WHITE, QUIET-LOOKING EYE AFTER ALKALI EXPOSURE IS OMINOUS — it indicates LIMBAL ISCHAEMIA from destroyed vessels, not an eye that has escaped injury.** **The red eye is doing better than the white one.**
> **Then: acuity, examination, ophthalmology.**

> [!warning] Corneal abrasion and foreign bodies
> **· ABRASION — diagnosed with fluorescein. Treated with topical antibiotic ointment and analgesia. DO NOT PATCH (it does not speed healing and is contraindicated in contact lens-related abrasions), and DO NOT give take-home topical anaesthetic.**
> **· EVERT THE UPPER LID in any patient with a foreign body sensation.** **VERTICAL LINEAR ABRASIONS on fluorescein staining are the signature of a SUBTARSAL FOREIGN BODY scraping the cornea with each blink — and the abrasions will not heal until it is removed.** **This finding is diagnostic and the lid eversion takes seconds.**
> **· Corneal foreign bodies may leave a RUST RING requiring removal.**
> **· RECURRENT EROSION SYNDROME — recurrent episodes of sudden severe pain, typically ON WAKING, after a previous abrasion, because the regenerated epithelium adheres poorly and is stripped by the eyelid. Treated with lubricants and ointment at night.**

> [!danger] High-velocity injury means intraocular foreign body until proven otherwise
> **HAMMERING, GRINDING, CHISELLING OR METAL-ON-METAL WORK WITHOUT EYE PROTECTION.**
> **A small metallic fragment can penetrate the globe with MINIMAL PAIN, a nearly NORMAL-LOOKING EYE, and preserved vision — so the patient presents late, or is reassured and sent home.**
> **THE HISTORY IS THE DIAGNOSIS. Ask specifically what they were doing.**
> **Look for: a small entry wound · a peaked or irregular pupil · shallow anterior chamber · hyphaema · or a subconjunctival haemorrhage with no visible posterior limit.**
> **IMAGE WITH CT ORBITS — NOT MRI.** **MRI is CONTRAINDICATED where a ferromagnetic intraocular foreign body is possible, because the magnetic field can move it and destroy the eye.**
> **This is a specific, examinable and genuinely dangerous point.**

> [!warning] Globe rupture and the other trauma
> **SUSPECTED GLOBE RUPTURE OR PENETRATING INJURY: apply a RIGID SHIELD, NOT A PAD · DO NOT PRESS ON THE EYE · do not instil drops or ointment · do not attempt to measure pressure · keep the patient upright and nil by mouth · give ANTIEMETICS (vomiting raises intraocular pressure and can expel intraocular contents) · give analgesia and tetanus prophylaxis · and refer immediately.** Cross-refer [[L8]] 0.2.
> **· HYPHAEMA — blood in the anterior chamber, with a fluid level. Upright posture, avoid NSAIDs and antiplatelets, and urgent ophthalmology; the risk is rebleeding and raised pressure, and it is higher in sickle cell disease.**
> **· RETROBULBAR HAEMORRHAGE — proptosis, a tense eye, severe pain, reduced acuity and a relative afferent pupillary defect. This is an ORBITAL COMPARTMENT SYNDROME, vision is lost within hours, and it requires immediate LATERAL CANTHOTOMY AND CANTHOLYSIS — a bedside procedure that may need to be done before ophthalmology arrives.** Cross-refer [[L8]] 0.2.
> **· ORBITAL BLOWOUT FRACTURE — including the paediatric "WHITE-EYED BLOWOUT", where a trapdoor fracture entraps muscle with minimal external signs, and the child has restricted upgaze with VOMITING AND BRADYCARDIA from the oculocardiac reflex.** **It is a surgical emergency and the vomiting gets attributed to head injury.** Cross-refer [[L8]] 0.2.
> **· ULTRAVIOLET KERATITIS (welder's flash, snow blindness) — severe BILATERAL pain, photophobia and blepharospasm developing SEVERAL HOURS AFTER the exposure, with punctate fluorescein staining.** **The delay is characteristic and explains why patients present in the middle of the night.** It heals within a day or two with analgesia, lubricants and cycloplegia.

## 0.6 Chronic and Recurrent Red Eye

> [!tip] Blepharitis — the commonest chronic red eye, and the treatment is not a drug
> **Chronic inflammation of the lid margins: anterior (staphylococcal or seborrhoeic) or POSTERIOR (meibomian gland dysfunction).**
> **Symptoms: gritty, burning, itchy eyes that are WORSE IN THE MORNING, with crusting on the lashes, redness of the lid margins, and recurrent styes and chalazia.**
> **STRONGLY ASSOCIATED WITH ROSACEA and with seborrhoeic dermatitis — so look at the face, and conversely, ask about the eyes in every rosacea patient.** Cross-refer [[G4]] 0.3.
> **THE TREATMENT IS LID HYGIENE, and it must be explained properly or it fails: WARM COMPRESSES for several minutes to soften the meibomian secretions · then LID MASSAGE toward the lid margin · then CLEANING the lash line.** Done daily, indefinitely.
> **IT IS A MANAGEMENT, NOT A CURE — the condition is chronic and relapses when the routine stops.** **Telling patients this at the outset prevents the cycle of brief improvement, abandonment and re-presentation.** Oral tetracyclines are used for meibomian dysfunction and ocular rosacea.

> [!warning] Dry eye — and it presents as watering
> **Very common, and increasing with screen use (because blink rate falls markedly during concentrated visual tasks).**
> **Symptoms: grittiness, burning, fluctuating blurred vision that clears on blinking, and — PARADOXICALLY — WATERING, because reflex tearing is triggered by surface irritation.** **A patient complaining of watery eyes may well have dry eye, which is counterintuitive and leads to the wrong treatment.**
> **Causes and contributors: age and female sex · SCREEN USE and air conditioning · blepharitis and meibomian dysfunction · DRUGS (anticholinergics, antihistamines, diuretics, ISOTRETINOIN, beta-blockers) · autoimmune disease, particularly SJÖGREN SYNDROME (ask about DRY MOUTH — cross-refer [[L2]] 0.4) · and previous refractive surgery.**
> **Management: lubricants used regularly rather than as required · lid hygiene · addressing the drug list · screen breaks and blink awareness · humidity · and punctal plugs or topical anti-inflammatories in refractory cases.**

> [!tip] Two benign presentations that alarm people
> **· SUBCONJUNCTIVAL HAEMORRHAGE — a dramatic, uniformly red patch with a normal pupil, NORMAL VISION and NO PAIN.** Benign, and it resolves over one to two weeks, changing colour like a bruise.
> **BUT: check the BLOOD PRESSURE · ask about ANTICOAGULANTS and consider a bleeding disorder if RECURRENT · and if it follows TRAUMA and has NO VISIBLE POSTERIOR LIMIT, consider an orbital fracture or occult globe injury and image it.**
> **· PTERYGIUM and PINGUECULA — sun and wind-related degenerative growths on the conjunctiva.** **Extremely common in Australia given the ultraviolet exposure.** **Managed with sun protection (wrap-around sunglasses and a hat) and lubricants, with surgery reserved for encroachment on the visual axis, restriction of movement, or recurrent inflammation.** Cross-refer [[G4]] 0.6.

> [!danger] The recurrent or non-resolving red eye
> **A red eye that keeps coming back in the same eye, or that fails to settle, deserves rethinking rather than another prescription:**
> **· RECURRENT ANTERIOR UVEITIS — and therefore a search for HLA-B27-associated disease, sarcoidosis or another systemic cause.**
> **· HERPES SIMPLEX keratitis, which characteristically recurs.**
> **· A retained FOREIGN BODY or recurrent erosion syndrome.**
> **· BLEPHARITIS and dry eye, which are chronic by nature.**
> **· OCULAR SURFACE NEOPLASIA — a persistent unilateral red or thickened area on the conjunctiva, particularly at the limbus in a sun-exposed patient, or unilateral chronic "conjunctivitis" that will not settle.** **It is uncommon but it is diagnosed late because it looks like inflammation, and Australia's ultraviolet exposure makes it more relevant here.**
> **· And in an older patient with unilateral chronic lid margin change, loss of lashes, or a recurrent "chalazion" that keeps returning in the same spot — consider SEBACEOUS CARCINOMA of the lid, which masquerades as chronic blepharitis or a chalazion and requires biopsy.**

**Ix:** **VISUAL ACUITY with glasses and with a PINHOLE, documented as a number, in every eye presentation** (*why:* it is the vital sign of the eye, it stratifies urgency, and the pinhole separates refractive from ocular causes; *what:* the acuity and whether it improves). **FLUORESCEIN with a blue light** (*why:* reveals abrasions, ulcers, dendrites and the vertical lines of a subtarsal foreign body; *what:* the staining pattern). **PUPIL examination including a swinging light test for a relative afferent pupillary defect** (*why:* an RAPD indicates optic nerve or extensive retinal pathology and changes the differential entirely; *what:* RAPD, shape, reactivity). **LID EVERSION** (*why:* subtarsal foreign bodies are invisible otherwise and the abrasion will not heal until removed; *what:* the foreign body). **Intraocular pressure measurement** where angle closure is suspected (*why:* confirms the diagnosis; *what:* markedly raised pressure — and a hard eye on gentle palpation is the bedside surrogate). **TOPICAL PHENYLEPHRINE to distinguish episcleritis from scleritis** (*why:* blanching indicates the benign one, and the distinction determines whether systemic disease is sought; *what:* blanching or not). **CT ORBITS — NOT MRI — for suspected intraocular foreign body** (*why:* MRI can move a ferromagnetic fragment and destroy the eye; *what:* the foreign body). **Conjunctival swabs including for gonorrhoea and chlamydia in hyperacute or chronic conjunctivitis, and in any neonate** (*why:* both need systemic treatment, notification and partner treatment; *what:* the organism). **Systemic workup in uveitis or scleritis — HLA-B27, ANA, ANCA, ACE, chest radiograph, syphilis and TB screening as directed** (*why:* the eye may be the presenting feature of systemic disease; *what:* the underlying condition — cross-refer [[L2]] 0.6). **pH testing after chemical injury, repeated** (*why:* confirms adequate irrigation, and it must be rechecked after a delay because it drifts back; *what:* neutral pH).
