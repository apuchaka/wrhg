## 0.1 Assessing a Pigmented Lesion

> [!danger] The single most important feature is CHANGE
> **Australia has the highest incidence of melanoma in the world, and melanoma is a leading cause of cancer death in young Australian adults** — which sets the threshold for how seriously a changing lesion should be taken.
> **ABCDE: ASYMMETRY · BORDER irregularity · COLOUR variation within the lesion · DIAMETER (conventionally above about 6 mm) · EVOLVING.**
> **"E" IS THE MOST IMPORTANT. A lesion that is CHANGING — in size, shape, colour, elevation, or by bleeding, itching or ulcerating — warrants assessment regardless of how it scores on the other letters.**
> **A patient who says "this one has changed" is providing better information than any single morphological feature.**

> [!tip] The ugly duckling sign — often more useful than ABCDE
> **Most people's naevi RESEMBLE EACH OTHER — they share a family likeness.**
> **THE UGLY DUCKLING is the lesion that looks DIFFERENT from the patient's others.**
> **This works because it uses the patient as their own control, and it detects the lesion that ABCDE misses in someone with many atypical naevi (where every lesion scores badly on ABCDE) or someone with very few (where a small early melanoma may not yet meet the criteria).**
> **Stand back and look at the whole back at once, rather than examining lesions one at a time through a lens.**

> [!warning] The history that stratifies risk
> **· PERSONAL history of melanoma or keratinocyte cancer — the strongest single risk factor for another.**
> **· FAMILY history of melanoma, particularly in a first-degree relative or at a young age.**
> **· SKIN TYPE — fair skin, red or blond hair, blue eyes, freckling, poor tanning.**
> **· SUN EXPOSURE HISTORY, and specifically the number of episodes of BLISTERING SUNBURN, particularly in CHILDHOOD.** Intermittent intense exposure relates to melanoma; cumulative chronic exposure relates to SCC.
> **· Number of naevi, and the presence of ATYPICAL naevi.**
> **· IMMUNOSUPPRESSION — see 0.3, where the transplant population is discussed. This dramatically changes risk.**
> **· Previous solaria use, outdoor occupation, and prior phototherapy.**

> [!danger] Examine the whole skin, and if in doubt, excise
> **A "check this mole" consultation should include a FULL SKIN EXAMINATION — including the SCALP (part the hair), BEHIND THE EARS, BETWEEN THE TOES, the SOLES, the NAILS, and the genital and perianal skin where appropriate and consented.**
> **Melanomas occur at sites that never see the sun, and acral and subungual melanomas are diagnosed late precisely because nobody looks.**
> **AND THE OPERATING RULE: IF YOU ARE NOT SURE, EXCISE IT OR REFER IT. DO NOT "WATCH" A SUSPICIOUS LESION.**
> **"Review in three months" applied to a lesion that concerns you is how thin, curable melanomas become thick ones — and the patient frequently does not return.**
> **Serial photography has a role for monitoring multiple naevi in high-risk patients under specialist follow-up; it is not a substitute for excising a lesion you are worried about.**

## 0.2 Melanoma

> [!danger] Nodular melanoma does not follow the ABCDE rules
> **Superficial spreading melanoma — the commonest — grows radially first and does follow ABCDE.**
> **NODULAR MELANOMA grows VERTICALLY from the outset. It is often SYMMETRICAL, uniformly coloured, with a REGULAR border and a SMALL diameter — so it scores WELL on ABCDE while being the most lethal subtype.**
> **USE "EFG" INSTEAD: ELEVATED · FIRM · GROWING (progressively, over weeks to months).**
> **A firm, growing nodule — of any colour — is melanoma until proven otherwise, and it needs excision now, not observation.**
> **AMELANOTIC MELANOMA has little or no pigment and appears PINK or RED. It is regularly diagnosed as a pyogenic granuloma, a basal cell carcinoma, dermatitis or a wart, and it is one of the commonest reasons for delayed diagnosis.**

> [!warning] The subtypes worth distinguishing
> **· SUPERFICIAL SPREADING — commonest; intermittently sun-exposed sites; back in men, legs in women.**
> **· NODULAR — as above.**
> **· LENTIGO MALIGNA — a slowly enlarging irregular pigmented macule on chronically sun-damaged skin, typically the face of an older person, over years, before becoming invasive (lentigo maligna melanoma).**
> **· ACRAL LENTIGINOUS — on PALMS, SOLES and NAIL UNITS. It is NOT related to sun exposure, and it represents a much higher PROPORTION of melanomas in people with darker skin and in Asian populations.** **It is diagnosed LATE — because it occurs where nobody looks, in populations not perceived as at risk, and it is mistaken for a wart, a callus, a bruise or a fungal nail.** **This is a genuine equity issue: the absolute risk is lower in darker skin but the outcomes are worse.** **LOOK AT THE SOLES AND THE NAILS.**
> **· DESMOPLASTIC and mucosal melanoma — rare, often amelanotic, frequently late.**

> [!danger] Subungual melanoma and Hutchinson's sign
> **LONGITUDINAL MELANONYCHIA — a pigmented band running the length of the nail.**
> **Multiple bands in multiple nails, particularly in people with darker skin, are usually PHYSIOLOGICAL.**
> **CONCERNING FEATURES: a SINGLE digit · a NEW band in an ADULT · a band that is WIDENING or has variable pigmentation · a band wider than about 3 mm · involvement of the THUMB, index finger or great toe · nail dystrophy or destruction · and HUTCHINSON SIGN — PIGMENT EXTENDING ONTO THE PROXIMAL NAIL FOLD or the surrounding skin.**
> **Hutchinson sign is the key finding and should trigger referral for nail matrix biopsy.**
> **The commonest error is attributing a pigmented band or a nail change to trauma — and subungual melanoma is repeatedly diagnosed as a subungual haematoma or onychomycosis for months to years.** **A subungual haematoma GROWS OUT with the nail; pigment that does not migrate distally over weeks is not a haematoma.**

> [!danger] Excision biopsy — never a partial biopsy of a suspected melanoma
> **BRESLOW THICKNESS — the depth of invasion in millimetres — IS THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT PROGNOSTIC FACTOR and determines the excision margin, the need for sentinel node biopsy, and staging.**
> **THEREFORE THE DIAGNOSTIC PROCEDURE IS COMPLETE EXCISION BIOPSY WITH A NARROW MARGIN, taking the full depth.**
> **DO NOT SHAVE BIOPSY, PUNCH BIOPSY OR PARTIALLY SAMPLE A LESION SUSPECTED OF BEING MELANOMA** — a partial biopsy may miss the deepest part and UNDER-STAGE the tumour, leading to inadequate definitive surgery and a worse outcome.
> **Partial biopsy is acceptable only in specific circumstances — a very large lesion, or a facial or acral lesion where complete excision would be disfiguring — and ideally under specialist direction.**
> **Other prognostic factors: ULCERATION and MITOTIC RATE.**
> **Definitive management: WIDE LOCAL EXCISION with margins determined by Breslow thickness · SENTINEL LYMPH NODE BIOPSY offered above a threshold thickness · and staging imaging in higher-stage disease.** `UNVERIFIED — all thresholds and margins.`

> [!tip] Advanced disease, and follow-up
> **THE TREATMENT OF METASTATIC MELANOMA HAS BEEN TRANSFORMED.** **Immune CHECKPOINT INHIBITORS and BRAF/MEK targeted therapy have changed survival in advanced melanoma from months to a substantial proportion achieving long-term disease control** — one of the most striking changes in oncology in recent decades.
> **This matters for two reasons: patients and families may hold outdated expectations · and interns will encounter the IMMUNE-RELATED ADVERSE EVENTS of checkpoint inhibitors, which can affect any organ and occur months after the last dose.** Cross-refer [[J5_Oncological_Emergencies_and_Palliative_Care]] 0.2 and [[A5_Toxicology_II_-_Poisoned_Patient__ADRs_and_Immunotherapy]] 0.7.
> **FOLLOW-UP after melanoma: risk of a SECOND PRIMARY melanoma is substantially increased, so surveillance is lifelong · teach and check SELF-EXAMINATION, including of the scalp and the back with a partner or mirror · rigorous SUN PROTECTION · and consider family screening where there is a family history.** Cross-refer [[G4_Chronic_Rash__Pruritus_and_Pigment]] 0.6.

## 0.3 Basal Cell and Squamous Cell Carcinoma

> [!tip] Basal cell carcinoma
> **The commonest cancer in humans, and extremely common in Australia.**
> **Appearance: a PEARLY, translucent papule or nodule with a ROLLED EDGE, surface TELANGIECTASIA, and often central ulceration and crusting ("rodent ulcer") — on chronically sun-exposed skin, particularly the head and neck.**
> **It grows SLOWLY, is LOCALLY INVASIVE, and essentially NEVER METASTASISES — but it can be LOCALLY DESTRUCTIVE, invading cartilage, bone and orbit if neglected.**
> **THE "H-ZONE" of the face — around the eyes, nose, nasolabial folds and ears — carries a higher recurrence risk and worse functional consequences, and lesions there warrant specialist management, often MOHS micrographic surgery.**
> **Subtypes matter: NODULAR (classic) · SUPERFICIAL (a scaly erythematous patch on the trunk, frequently mistaken for eczema or psoriasis and treated with topical steroid for months) · and MORPHOEIC/INFILTRATIVE (a scar-like, ill-defined, indurated plaque with margins extending well beyond what is visible — the highest recurrence risk).**
> **Treatment: excision · Mohs surgery for high-risk sites and infiltrative subtypes · and topical therapy, curettage or photodynamic therapy for selected superficial lesions.**

> [!danger] Squamous cell carcinoma — and it does metastasise
> **This is the key distinction from BCC.** SCC can metastasise, most often to regional lymph nodes.
> **It arises on chronically sun-damaged skin, frequently from pre-existing ACTINIC KERATOSES, and presents as a firm, keratotic, tender or ulcerated nodule that grows over weeks to months.**
> **HIGH-RISK FEATURES that change management: location on the EAR, LIP, temple or scalp · larger size and greater depth · POOR DIFFERENTIATION · PERINEURAL INVASION (with pain, paraesthesia or nerve palsy) · RECURRENT lesions · arising in a SCAR, chronic ulcer or sinus (MARJOLIN ULCER) · and an IMMUNOSUPPRESSED patient.** `UNVERIFIED — the size and depth thresholds.`
> **KERATOACANTHOMA — a rapidly growing, dome-shaped, crateriform nodule with a central keratin plug, which may involute spontaneously.** **It cannot be reliably distinguished from well-differentiated SCC clinically or even histologically, so it is EXCISED AND TREATED AS SCC.**

> [!danger] Organ transplant recipients need a different threshold entirely
> **Long-term immunosuppression after solid organ transplantation increases the risk of SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA MANY TIMES OVER — with a reversal of the usual SCC:BCC ratio, so SCC becomes the dominant cancer.**
> **These SCCs are MORE NUMEROUS, more AGGRESSIVE, more likely to RECUR and more likely to METASTASISE than in immunocompetent patients.**
> **Skin cancer is a leading cause of morbidity and a significant cause of mortality in long-term transplant survivors.**
> **What this means practically: transplant recipients require REGULAR DERMATOLOGICAL SURVEILLANCE from the time of transplantation · rigorous sun protection education · a LOW threshold for biopsy of any new or changing lesion · prompt treatment · and consideration of chemoprevention and immunosuppression modification by the transplant team.**
> **A "small skin lesion" in a transplant patient is not a small problem, and the same applies to patients on long-term immunosuppression for autoimmune disease and to those with chronic lymphocytic leukaemia.**

## 0.4 Premalignant and Benign Lesions

> [!tip] Actinic keratoses and field change
> **Rough, scaly, erythematous macules and papules on chronically sun-exposed skin — and they are frequently BETTER FELT THAN SEEN.** **Run your hand over the scalp, forehead, ears and dorsal hands.**
> **The risk of any individual lesion becoming an SCC is small — but the concept that matters is FIELD CHANGE: the surrounding skin has widespread ultraviolet damage and subclinical dysplasia, which is why patients develop lesions repeatedly across a region.**
> **This drives treatment strategy: CRYOTHERAPY for isolated lesions, but FIELD TREATMENT (topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, or photodynamic therapy) where lesions are numerous.** **Warn patients that field treatments produce a marked, alarming inflammatory reaction — and that this is expected, not a complication.** Otherwise they stop halfway.
> **BOWEN DISEASE (SCC in situ) — a persistent, well-demarcated, scaly erythematous patch, classically on the lower leg of an older woman.** **It is regularly treated as eczema or psoriasis for months because it is a solitary, fixed, non-itchy plaque that does not respond to topical steroid** — and a solitary scaly patch that does not respond to appropriate treatment should be biopsied.

> [!warning] The benign lesions, and the ones that mimic cancer
> **· SEBORRHOEIC KERATOSIS — "STUCK ON", warty, greasy, with a well-defined edge and visible follicular plugging, in colours from tan to black.** **They are very common with age, entirely benign — and they are confused with melanoma in both directions.** **The sudden ERUPTION OF MANY seborrhoeic keratoses (the LESER-TRÉLAT sign) has been described as a paraneoplastic marker of internal malignancy, though the association is debated.**
> **· DERMATOFIBROMA — a firm dermal nodule, often on the leg, with the DIMPLE SIGN (pinching laterally causes central dimpling).**
> **· PYOGENIC GRANULOMA — a rapidly growing, friable, bleeding red nodule, often after minor trauma or in pregnancy.** **ALWAYS SEND IT FOR HISTOLOGY — AMELANOTIC MELANOMA AND SCC MIMIC IT EXACTLY, and a lesion destroyed by cautery without histology is a missed diagnosis.** **This is a specific and important rule.**
> **· Epidermoid cyst, lipoma, skin tags, cherry angiomas, and benign naevi.**
> **THE OVERARCHING RULE: BIOPSY ANY LESION THAT IS NOT HEALING, IS CHANGING, OR THAT YOU CANNOT CONFIDENTLY NAME.** Histology is cheap; a delayed melanoma is not.

## 0.5 Nails

> [!tip] Nail signs of systemic disease
> **· CLUBBING — cross-refer [[A4_Dyspnoea__Cough_and_the_Solitary_Pulmonary_Nodule]] 0.1.**
> **· KOILONYCHIA (spoon-shaped) — IRON DEFICIENCY.**
> **· BEAU LINES — transverse grooves across all nails, caused by temporary arrest of nail growth during a severe systemic illness.** **They are useful because they DATE THE INSULT: knowing that nails grow at a roughly predictable rate, the distance from the cuticle indicates how long ago the illness occurred.**
> **· LEUKONYCHIA (white nails) — hypoalbuminaemia, from liver disease or nephrotic syndrome.**
> **· TERRY NAILS (proximal white nail with a distal band) — cirrhosis. HALF-AND-HALF nails — chronic kidney disease.**
> **· SPLINTER HAEMORRHAGES — TRAUMA is by far the commonest cause, but they are a recognised sign of INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS and vasculitis.** Cross-refer [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.1.
> **· PERIUNGUAL TELANGIECTASIA AND ABNORMAL NAILFOLD CAPILLARIES — a genuinely valuable bedside sign of CONNECTIVE TISSUE DISEASE, particularly systemic sclerosis and dermatomyositis.** **Look at the nailfolds with a dermatoscope or even an ophthalmoscope in anyone with Raynaud phenomenon — dilated, dropped-out capillaries indicate a secondary rather than primary Raynaud and change the workup entirely.** Cross-refer [[L2_Polyarthralgia_and_Inflammatory_Arthritis]] 0.4 and [[L3_Muscle_Symptoms_and_Widespread_Pain]] 0.6.
> **· Nail PITTING, ONYCHOLYSIS and oil-drop change — PSORIASIS.** Cross-refer [[G4_Chronic_Rash__Pruritus_and_Pigment]] 0.2.
> **· Yellow nail syndrome — with lymphoedema and pleural effusions.**

> [!danger] Confirm onychomycosis before treating it
> **A substantial proportion of dystrophic nails that look fungal are NOT — the differential includes PSORIASIS, TRAUMA (particularly the great toenails in runners and in people with foot deformity), lichen planus, and — critically — SUBUNGUAL MELANOMA.**
> **SYSTEMIC ANTIFUNGALS ARE NOT TRIVIAL: they require months of treatment, carry HEPATOTOXICITY, and have significant DRUG INTERACTIONS (terbinafine and the azoles).**
> **TAKE NAIL CLIPPINGS AND SUBUNGUAL DEBRIS FOR MICROSCOPY AND CULTURE BEFORE PRESCRIBING** — and take an adequate sample from the affected proximal part, not just a snip of the distal edge.
> **Treating a psoriatic or traumatic nail with months of terbinafine achieves nothing and risks harm.**
> **PARONYCHIA: ACUTE — usually staphylococcal, painful, red, may need incision and drainage. CHRONIC — associated with WET WORK and irritant exposure, with loss of the cuticle and secondary candida; the treatment is AVOIDING MOISTURE AND IRRITANTS plus a topical steroid, and antifungals alone do not work.**

## 0.6 Hair Loss

> [!danger] Scarring versus non-scarring is the critical distinction
> **LOOK FOR FOLLICULAR OSTIA — the visible pores from which hairs emerge.**
> **· OSTIA PRESENT → NON-SCARRING alopecia. The follicles are intact and regrowth is possible.**
> **· OSTIA ABSENT, with smooth shiny skin → SCARRING (cicatricial) alopecia. The follicle has been destroyed and THE LOSS IS PERMANENT.**
> **SCARRING ALOPECIA IS URGENT** — treatment cannot restore lost hair, only halt progression, so every week of delay costs follicles permanently. **These patients need prompt dermatology referral and usually a scalp biopsy.**
> **The scarring causes: LICHEN PLANOPILARIS · FRONTAL FIBROSING ALOPECIA (a receding frontal hairline with EYEBROW LOSS in postmenopausal women — increasingly recognised and frequently dismissed as normal recession) · DISCOID LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS · CENTRAL CENTRIFUGAL CICATRICIAL ALOPECIA (predominantly affecting Black women, beginning at the vertex and spreading centrifugally, and requiring early treatment) · and folliculitis decalvans.**

> [!warning] Telogen effluvium — the delay is why nobody connects it
> **Diffuse shedding of hair, noticed as handfuls in the shower and on the pillow, with reduced overall density but no bald patches.**
> **THE KEY FEATURE IS THE TIMING: IT OCCURS TWO TO FOUR MONTHS AFTER THE TRIGGER.**
> **Because of that delay, patients and clinicians do not connect it to the precipitating event, and the patient presents convinced something is currently wrong.**
> **THE TRIGGERS: a significant ILLNESS or FEVER · SURGERY and general anaesthesia · CHILDBIRTH (postpartum effluvium is very common and normal) · rapid WEIGHT LOSS or crash dieting · severe psychological STRESS · IRON DEFICIENCY · THYROID disease · and DRUGS.**
> **ASK WHAT HAPPENED THREE MONTHS AGO.** The answer is usually there, and identifying it is most of the treatment.
> **It is SELF-LIMITING and REVERSIBLE, with regrowth over months once the trigger has resolved — and explaining this, with the timeline, is what the patient needs.** **Check ferritin and thyroid function.**

> [!tip] The other non-scarring causes
> **· ANDROGENETIC ALOPECIA — patterned: temporal recession and vertex thinning in men; diffuse widening of the central part with preservation of the frontal hairline in women.** **In women with androgenetic alopecia PLUS hirsutism, acne or menstrual irregularity, consider hyperandrogenism and PCOS.** Cross-refer [[I4_Pituitary__Adrenal_and_Sex_Hormone]] 0.6.
> **· ALOPECIA AREATA — well-demarcated, smooth, round patches of complete hair loss with retained follicular ostia, sometimes with "EXCLAMATION MARK" hairs (tapering towards the scalp) at the margin, and often with NAIL PITTING.** Autoimmune, with associations including thyroid disease and vitiligo. **The course is unpredictable; the PSYCHOLOGICAL IMPACT is considerable and under-acknowledged, particularly with extensive loss.**
> **· TINEA CAPITIS — in CHILDREN: a scaly patch with BROKEN HAIRS, sometimes with occipital lymphadenopathy, and in inflammatory cases a boggy KERION which can scar.** **IT REQUIRES SYSTEMIC ANTIFUNGAL TREATMENT — topical antifungals do not penetrate the hair shaft and will not cure it.** **Confirm with mycology, and check household contacts and any pets.**
> **· TRACTION ALOPECIA — from sustained tension on the hair: tight braids, weaves, extensions, buns and turbans. It affects the FRONTAL AND TEMPORAL hairline, and it disproportionately affects women of African descent.** **It is REVERSIBLE IF ADDRESSED EARLY AND BECOMES SCARRING IF NOT — which makes early recognition and a respectful conversation about hairstyling practices genuinely preventive.**
> **· TRICHOTILLOMANIA — irregular patches with hairs of varying lengths, from pulling.** Cross-refer [[N5_Anxiety__OCD_and_Trauma]] 0.4.
> **· ANAGEN EFFLUVIUM — rapid, extensive loss days to weeks after chemotherapy.**
> **Investigation: FERRITIN and THYROID function in diffuse loss; androgens where clinically indicated; and a SCALP BIOPSY where scarring alopecia is suspected or the diagnosis is unclear.**

**Ix:** **A FULL SKIN EXAMINATION including scalp, soles, between the toes, nails and genitalia** (*why:* acral and subungual melanomas are diagnosed late because nobody looks; *what:* the lesion nobody presented with). **EXCISION BIOPSY with a narrow margin for any lesion suspicious of melanoma — NOT a shave or partial biopsy** (*why:* Breslow thickness determines staging and management, and partial sampling under-stages; *what:* thickness, ulceration, mitotic rate). **HISTOLOGY on every pyogenic granuloma and every lesion destroyed by cautery or curettage** (*why:* amelanotic melanoma and SCC mimic pyogenic granuloma exactly; *what:* the diagnosis). **BIOPSY of any non-healing, changing or unnameable lesion, and of any solitary scaly plaque not responding to appropriate treatment** (*why:* Bowen disease and superficial BCC are treated as eczema for months; *what:* histology). **NAIL CLIPPINGS AND SUBUNGUAL DEBRIS for microscopy and culture BEFORE systemic antifungals** (*why:* up to half of dystrophic nails are not fungal, and terbinafine and azoles carry hepatotoxicity and interactions; *what:* dermatophyte). **NAILFOLD CAPILLAROSCOPY (or dermatoscopic inspection of the nailfolds) in Raynaud phenomenon** (*why:* abnormal capillaries distinguish secondary from primary Raynaud and prompt connective tissue disease workup; *what:* dilated or dropped-out capillaries). **FERRITIN and TFTs in diffuse hair loss** (*why:* both are common, treatable and easily checked; *what:* deficiency, thyroid dysfunction). **SCALP BIOPSY where follicular ostia are absent** (*why:* scarring alopecia is permanent and progressive, so diagnosis is urgent; *what:* the specific cicatricial process). **Mycology in suspected tinea capitis, with contact and pet screening** (*why:* it needs systemic treatment and it spreads within households; *what:* the organism).
