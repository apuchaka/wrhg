## 0.1 Recognising Haemolysis

> [!tip] The laboratory signature — four tests together
> **· RETICULOCYTES — RAISED (the marrow is responding).**
> **· LDH — RAISED (released from lysed cells).**
> **· UNCONJUGATED BILIRUBIN — RAISED (haem breakdown exceeding conjugation capacity).**
> **· HAPTOGLOBIN — LOW OR ABSENT (consumed binding free haemoglobin).**
> **Haptoglobin is the most SPECIFIC of the four — but it is an ACUTE PHASE REACTANT, so it can be falsely normal in an inflamed patient**, which is the same trap as ferritin in iron deficiency. Cross-refer [[J1]] 0.2.
> **A raised LDH with a raised bilirubin and a low haptoglobin, in an anaemic patient with high reticulocytes, is haemolysis until proven otherwise.**

> [!info] Intravascular versus extravascular — and what each looks like
> **INTRAVASCULAR — red cells lysed within the circulation.**
> **Features: HAEMOGLOBINAEMIA · HAEMOGLOBINURIA (dark or "cola" urine, with a dipstick POSITIVE FOR BLOOD but NO red cells on microscopy — cross-refer [[H1]] 0.1) · haemosiderinuria · a profoundly low haptoglobin · and schistocytes on the film.**
> **Causes: microangiopathic haemolysis · ABO-incompatible transfusion reaction · paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria · mechanical valve haemolysis · severe G6PD crises · MALARIA · and clostridial sepsis.**
> **EXTRAVASCULAR — red cells removed by the reticuloendothelial system, principally the spleen.**
> **Features: SPLENOMEGALY · jaundice · SPHEROCYTES · a less dramatic haptoglobin fall · and no haemoglobinuria.**
> **Causes: autoimmune haemolytic anaemia · hereditary spherocytosis · hypersplenism.**

> [!danger] The blood film tells you the mechanism
> **· SPHEROCYTES → autoimmune haemolytic anaemia OR hereditary spherocytosis. THE DAT DISTINGUISHES THEM.**
> **· SCHISTOCYTES (fragments) → MICROANGIOPATHIC haemolysis — TTP, HUS, DIC, HELLP, malignant hypertension, mechanical valve. THIS IS THE URGENT ONE.**
> **· BITE CELLS and BLISTER CELLS, with Heinz bodies on supravital stain → OXIDATIVE haemolysis, i.e. G6PD deficiency.**
> **· SICKLE CELLS → sickle cell disease.**
> **· TARGET CELLS → thalassaemia, liver disease, post-splenectomy.**
> **· AGGLUTINATION → cold agglutinin disease.**
> **· PARASITES → malaria and babesiosis.**
> **· Blister/nucleated red cells with a leucoerythroblastic picture → marrow infiltration.**

> [!warning] The DAT is the branch point, and chronic haemolysis has predictable complications
> **DIRECT ANTIGLOBULIN TEST (Coombs): POSITIVE means IMMUNE-mediated; NEGATIVE means NON-immune.** That single result splits the differential in two.
> **THE COMPLICATIONS OF ANY CHRONIC HAEMOLYSIS:**
> **· PIGMENT GALLSTONES — from chronic bilirubin load. Common, and a cause of biliary presentations in these patients.**
> **· FOLATE DEFICIENCY — from increased erythropoietic turnover. These patients need folate supplementation.**
> **· APLASTIC CRISIS WITH PARVOVIRUS B19 — the virus infects erythroid precursors and shuts down production for a week or two. In someone with a normal red cell lifespan this is unnoticed; in someone whose cells survive days rather than months, the haemoglobin FALLS PRECIPITOUSLY.** **A sudden severe drop in haemoglobin with a LOW reticulocyte count in a chronic haemolytic patient is parvovirus B19 until proven otherwise.** Cross-refer [[M2]] 0.5.
> **· IRON OVERLOAD if regularly transfused.**
> **· Leg ulcers, pulmonary hypertension, and extramedullary haematopoiesis in severe chronic cases.**

## 0.2 Inherited Haemolytic Anaemias

> [!tip] Hereditary spherocytosis
> **A red cell membrane cytoskeleton defect (spectrin, ankyrin, band 3), usually autosomal dominant, causing spherical cells that are trapped and destroyed in the spleen.**
> **Features: anaemia, JAUNDICE, SPLENOMEGALY, and PIGMENT GALLSTONES — often presenting in childhood, sometimes with neonatal jaundice.**
> **THE LABORATORY CLUE: a RAISED MCHC — one of the few conditions that raises it, because the cell has lost membrane while retaining its haemoglobin content.**
> **THE DISCRIMINATOR FROM AUTOIMMUNE HAEMOLYSIS: both show spherocytes, but the DAT IS NEGATIVE in hereditary spherocytosis and POSITIVE in warm AIHA.**
> **Confirmed by EMA binding flow cytometry or osmotic fragility testing.**
> **Management: FOLATE supplementation, monitoring, cholecystectomy for symptomatic gallstones, and SPLENECTOMY in severe disease — which requires prior VACCINATION against encapsulated organisms and lifelong antibiotic prophylaxis and awareness of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection.** Cross-refer [[K3]] 0.5.

> [!danger] G6PD deficiency — and the assay is falsely normal during the crisis
> **X-linked, the commonest enzyme defect worldwide, with high prevalence in people of Mediterranean, African, Middle Eastern, South Asian and Southeast Asian ancestry.**
> **G6PD maintains the NADPH needed to protect the cell against oxidative stress. Under an oxidative load, haemoglobin denatures into HEINZ BODIES, which the spleen removes by "biting" pieces out of the cell — producing BITE CELLS and BLISTER CELLS.**
> **THE TRIGGERS:**
> **· INFECTION — the commonest trigger of all, and the one most often forgotten.**
> **· FAVA BEANS ("favism").**
> **· DRUGS: PRIMAQUINE and tafenoquine · DAPSONE · NITROFURANTOIN · sulfonamides including co-trimoxazole · METHYLENE BLUE (which matters because it is the antidote for methaemoglobinaemia and is contraindicated here) · RASBURICASE (used in tumour lysis — cross-refer [[J5]]) · some quinolones · and nalidixic acid.**
> **· NAPHTHALENE mothballs.**
> **THE TRAP: THE G6PD ASSAY IS FALSELY NORMAL DURING AN ACUTE HAEMOLYTIC EPISODE.** The most deficient cells have already been destroyed, and the reticulocytes replacing them have HIGHER enzyme levels.
> **So a normal G6PD level taken during a crisis does not exclude the diagnosis — REPEAT IT WEEKS AFTER RECOVERY.**
> **G6PD deficiency also causes NEONATAL JAUNDICE, sometimes severe, and should be considered in a jaundiced newborn of relevant ancestry.** Cross-refer [[M3]] 0.1.
> **Management is avoidance of triggers, supportive care during a crisis, and a documented drug alert.**

## 0.3 Autoimmune Haemolytic Anaemia

> [!tip] Warm versus cold — the difference changes the treatment
> | | **WARM AIHA** | **COLD AGGLUTININ DISEASE** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Antibody | **IgG** | **IgM** |
> | Optimal temperature | 37°C | Cold (peripheries) |
> | Site of destruction | **Extravascular (spleen)** | Complement-mediated, **intravascular** |
> | Film | **Spherocytes** | **AGGLUTINATION** |
> | Symptoms | Anaemia, jaundice, splenomegaly | **ACROCYANOSIS, worse in the cold** |
> | Causes | Idiopathic · **LYMPHOPROLIFERATIVE (CLL, lymphoma)** · SLE and autoimmune disease · drugs (methyldopa, penicillins, cephalosporins, **checkpoint inhibitors**) | ***MYCOPLASMA*** · **EBV** · lymphoproliferative disease |
> | First-line treatment | **CORTICOSTEROIDS** | **KEEP THE PATIENT WARM** — steroids are much less effective |
> **Warm AIHA: steroids first, then rituximab or splenectomy, and treat the underlying cause — which means looking for a lymphoproliferative disorder or connective tissue disease in a new presentation.**
> **Cold agglutinin disease: warming is the primary intervention — a warm room, warm clothing, warming intravenous fluids, and WARMING ANY TRANSFUSED BLOOD, which is a practical point that gets missed.** Treat the underlying infection or lymphoproliferative disorder; rituximab for persistent disease.
> **PAROXYSMAL COLD HAEMOGLOBINURIA — a Donath-Landsteiner biphasic antibody, classically in children after a viral illness, causing acute intravascular haemolysis on cold exposure.**

> [!warning] Transfusion in autoimmune haemolysis is complicated — but do not withhold it
> **The autoantibody reacts with essentially all donor cells, making crossmatching difficult and slow, and it can MASK an underlying alloantibody.**
> **Involve the transfusion laboratory EARLY and give them time.**
> **But: in life-threatening anaemia, the "least incompatible" unit is given rather than allowing the patient to die waiting for a perfect match.** **Do not withhold necessary transfusion because the crossmatch is difficult** — that decision belongs with a haematologist, made urgently, not with a junior doctor deferring.
> **EVANS SYNDROME — autoimmune haemolytic anaemia combined with immune thrombocytopenia** (and sometimes neutropenia), which warrants investigation for an underlying lymphoproliferative or autoimmune disorder.

## 0.4 Microangiopathic and Mechanical Haemolysis

> [!danger] Schistocytes plus thrombocytopenia is an emergency until proven otherwise
> **MICROANGIOPATHIC HAEMOLYTIC ANAEMIA — red cells sheared by fibrin strands or abnormal endothelium, producing SCHISTOCYTES, with consumption of platelets.**
> **The differential, and it must be worked through quickly:**
> **· TTP — plasma exchange is urgent and life-saving; DO NOT transfuse platelets.** Cross-refer [[J1]] 0.6.
> **· HUS — typically following STEC diarrhoea in children; atypical HUS is complement-mediated.** Cross-refer [[M5]] 0.2.
> **· DIC — with abnormal coagulation studies, which distinguishes it from TTP where the PT and APTT are typically NORMAL.** **That distinction is diagnostically useful and examinable.** Cross-refer [[A9]] 0.3.
> **· PRE-ECLAMPSIA and HELLP SYNDROME — always consider pregnancy.** Cross-refer [[O2]].
> **· MALIGNANT HYPERTENSION — check the blood pressure and the fundi.**
> **· Disseminated malignancy, vasculitis, scleroderma renal crisis, and drugs.**
> **A patient with anaemia, thrombocytopenia and fragments on the film needs a haematologist NOW, not in the morning.**

> [!warning] The mechanical and infective causes
> **· PROSTHETIC VALVE HAEMOLYSIS — mechanical shearing, and it is markedly worse with a PARAVALVULAR LEAK or valve dysfunction. New or worsening haemolysis in a patient with a mechanical valve should prompt an echocardiogram** — cross-refer [[B5]] 0.3.
> **· MALARIA — cross-refer [[K1]] 0.4. FEVER PLUS HAEMOLYSIS IN A RETURNED TRAVELLER IS MALARIA UNTIL THICK AND THIN FILMS SAY OTHERWISE.** Falciparum can cause massive intravascular haemolysis ("blackwater fever") and is a medical emergency.
> **· March haemoglobinuria** — after prolonged running or marching.
> **· Snake envenomation, burns, near-drowning.**

> [!tip] Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria — haemolysis with thrombosis
> **An acquired clonal defect in GPI-anchored complement regulatory proteins (CD55 and CD59), leaving red cells vulnerable to complement-mediated intravascular lysis.**
> **The triad: HAEMOLYSIS (intravascular, with haemoglobinuria — often most obvious in the morning) · THROMBOSIS, characteristically in UNUSUAL SITES including HEPATIC VEINS (Budd-Chiari syndrome), portal, cerebral and dermal veins · and CYTOPENIAS with marrow failure.**
> **THROMBOSIS IS THE LEADING CAUSE OF DEATH.**
> **THINK OF IT IN: unexplained intravascular haemolysis · thrombosis at an unusual site, particularly hepatic vein thrombosis · and unexplained cytopenias or aplastic anaemia.**
> **Diagnosed by FLOW CYTOMETRY (not by the old Ham test).** Treated with complement inhibitors such as eculizumab — **which require MENINGOCOCCAL VACCINATION and prophylaxis, because blocking terminal complement creates a specific and serious risk of meningococcal disease.** Cross-refer [[K3]] 0.5.

## 0.5 Sickle Cell Disease

**A/P:** **A single amino acid substitution — valine for glutamic acid at position 6 of the beta globin chain — produces haemoglobin S, which POLYMERISES when DEOXYGENATED.** The polymer distorts the cell into a rigid sickle shape, causing **VASO-OCCLUSION** (the source of the acute pain and organ damage) and **CHRONIC HAEMOLYSIS**.
**Sickle cell TRAIT is largely asymptomatic and is PROTECTIVE AGAINST FALCIPARUM MALARIA** — the reason the gene persists. **But trait is not entirely benign: it causes renal papillary necrosis and haematuria, and sickling can occur under extreme hypoxia, dehydration or at altitude.**

> [!danger] Vaso-occlusive crisis — and the systematic under-treatment of pain
> **Precipitants: INFECTION · DEHYDRATION · COLD · HYPOXIA · acidosis · physical or emotional STRESS · alcohol · and often nothing identifiable.**
> **THE PAIN IS SEVERE — frequently described by patients as worse than childbirth or a fracture — and it requires PROMPT and ADEQUATE ANALGESIA, usually including strong opioids, given rapidly on presentation.**
> **PATIENTS WITH SICKLE CELL DISEASE ARE SYSTEMATICALLY UNDER-TREATED FOR PAIN.** They wait longer in emergency departments, receive less analgesia, and are labelled as drug-seeking — a pattern that is well documented, that disproportionately affects patients of African ancestry, and that constitutes a genuine equity failure in healthcare.
> **These patients know their own crises and their own effective doses. Believe them.** **A patient who asks for a specific opioid at a specific dose is usually describing what has worked before, not manipulating you.**
> **Alongside analgesia: hydration, oxygen if hypoxic, warmth, treatment of any infective trigger, thromboprophylaxis, and INCENTIVE SPIROMETRY.**

> [!danger] Acute chest syndrome — and adequate analgesia prevents it
> **A new pulmonary infiltrate with fever, chest pain, cough, hypoxia or respiratory signs, in a patient with sickle cell disease.**
> **IT IS THE LEADING CAUSE OF DEATH in sickle cell disease.**
> **THE MECHANISM CONNECTS IT TO ANALGESIA: severe chest or back pain → the patient SPLINTS and breathes shallowly → atelectasis → hypoxia → local sickling → infiltrate.**
> **SO UNDER-TREATING PAIN CAUSES ACUTE CHEST SYNDROME, and adequate analgesia plus INCENTIVE SPIROMETRY prevents it.** **This is the strongest argument for treating the pain properly, and it should be made to anyone reluctant to give opioids.**
> **Management: antibiotics (covering atypicals), oxygen, adequate analgesia, bronchodilators, and TRANSFUSION or EXCHANGE TRANSFUSION for severe or deteriorating disease.** **Escalate early — it can deteriorate rapidly.**

> [!warning] The other acute complications
> **· STROKE — a significant risk in children.** **TRANSCRANIAL DOPPLER SCREENING identifies children at high risk, and regular transfusion programmes substantially reduce stroke incidence** — one of the clearest preventive successes in the condition.
> **· ACUTE SPLENIC SEQUESTRATION — in young children, with rapid splenic enlargement, a precipitous fall in haemoglobin and hypovolaemic shock.** **Parents are taught to palpate the spleen.** **A life-threatening emergency requiring urgent transfusion.**
> **· APLASTIC CRISIS — PARVOVIRUS B19, with a falling haemoglobin and LOW reticulocytes.**
> **· PRIAPISM — an emergency requiring urgent urological management.** Cross-refer [[H4]] 0.3.
> **· INFECTION — these patients are FUNCTIONALLY ASPLENIC from repeated splenic infarction, and are at risk of overwhelming infection with ENCAPSULATED ORGANISMS.** **They require vaccination and penicillin prophylaxis, and FEVER IN A SICKLE CELL PATIENT IS AN EMERGENCY requiring prompt assessment and antibiotics.** Cross-refer [[K3]] 0.5.

> [!tip] Chronic complications and long-term management
> **· AVASCULAR NECROSIS — particularly the femoral head. Hip pain in a sickle cell patient warrants imaging.**
> **· Proliferative retinopathy · sickle nephropathy and chronic kidney disease · PULMONARY HYPERTENSION · chronic LEG ULCERS · pigment gallstones · delayed growth and puberty · and iron overload in those regularly transfused.**
> **Management: HYDROXYCARBAMIDE (hydroxyurea), which raises fetal haemoglobin and reduces crises, acute chest syndrome and mortality — the single most important disease-modifying therapy · FOLATE · vaccination and penicillin prophylaxis · transcranial Doppler screening in children · transfusion programmes · newer targeted agents · and stem cell transplantation or gene therapy in selected patients.** `UNVERIFIED — all agents, doses and eligibility.`
> **AUSTRALIAN CONTEXT: sickle cell disease is relatively uncommon here but is increasing with migration, and it is therefore under-recognised.** **A patient of African, Middle Eastern, Mediterranean, Indian or Caribbean ancestry presenting with severe unexplained pain should prompt the thought**, and their disease may be less familiar to the treating team than it should be.

## 0.6 Thalassaemia

**A/P:** **Reduced or absent synthesis of alpha or beta globin chains → an IMBALANCE of chains → the excess unpaired chains precipitate → INEFFECTIVE ERYTHROPOIESIS in the marrow plus peripheral HAEMOLYSIS.**
**The severe anaemia drives marrow expansion, which produces the skeletal changes, and drives increased gut iron absorption, which contributes to overload even without transfusion.**

> [!tip] Beta thalassaemia — the spectrum
> **· BETA THALASSAEMIA MINOR (trait) — asymptomatic microcytosis with a normal or near-normal haemoglobin, a normal ferritin, and a RAISED HbA2 on electrophoresis.** **The key clinical action is NOT giving iron and DOING genetic counselling.** Cross-refer [[J1]] 0.2.
> **· BETA THALASSAEMIA MAJOR — presents AFTER ABOUT SIX MONTHS OF AGE, as fetal haemoglobin declines and the defective beta chain production is unmasked.** **This delayed presentation is characteristic and examinable.**
> **Features: severe transfusion-dependent anaemia · failure to thrive · HEPATOSPLENOMEGALY · and the skeletal changes of EXTRAMEDULLARY HAEMATOPOIESIS — frontal bossing, maxillary overgrowth ("chipmunk facies"), and a "HAIR-ON-END" appearance of the skull on radiograph.**
> **· BETA THALASSAEMIA INTERMEDIA — an intermediate phenotype, not regularly transfusion-dependent but with significant complications.**

> [!warning] Alpha thalassaemia — four genes, four phenotypes
> **There are four alpha globin genes, and severity depends on how many are lost:**
> **· ONE deleted — silent carrier, normal.**
> **· TWO deleted — alpha thalassaemia trait, with microcytosis and a NORMAL haemoglobin electrophoresis. This is why alpha thalassaemia trait requires GENETIC testing to diagnose, and why a normal electrophoresis does not exclude thalassaemia.**
> **· THREE deleted — HbH DISEASE, with moderate to severe haemolytic anaemia.**
> **· FOUR deleted — Hb BARTS HYDROPS FETALIS, which is incompatible with life and carries significant risk to the MOTHER (severe pre-eclampsia and obstetric complications).**
> **The last point is why ANTENATAL SCREENING AND PARTNER TESTING matter**, particularly in couples of Southeast Asian, Chinese and Mediterranean ancestry.

> [!danger] Iron overload is what kills transfusion-dependent patients
> **Each unit of transfused blood delivers iron, and the body has no mechanism to excrete it. Ineffective erythropoiesis also increases gut absorption.**
> **The consequences, in order of what causes death:**
> **· CARDIAC — iron cardiomyopathy and ARRHYTHMIA. THIS IS THE COMMONEST CAUSE OF DEATH in transfusion-dependent thalassaemia.**
> **· ENDOCRINE — diabetes mellitus, HYPOGONADISM (with delayed or absent puberty and infertility), hypothyroidism, hypoparathyroidism, growth failure, and osteoporosis.**
> **· HEPATIC — fibrosis and cirrhosis.**
> **IRON CHELATION IS THEREFORE ESSENTIAL AND LIFELONG**, and adherence to it is the main determinant of survival — which makes it a major focus of adolescent and transition care, where adherence typically falters. Cross-refer [[M7]] 0.4.
> **Monitoring: serum ferritin (imperfect), and CARDIAC AND HEPATIC MRI (T2*), which measures tissue iron directly and is far more informative than ferritin alone.**
> **`UNVERIFIED — chelating agents, doses, monitoring intervals and ferritin targets.`**

> [!danger] Do not give iron to a thalassaemia patient
> **They are anaemic and microcytic, so iron gets prescribed reflexively — and it adds to an iron burden that is already the thing most likely to kill them.**
> **Only give iron if deficiency has been PROVEN**, recognising that thalassaemia and iron deficiency can genuinely coexist.
> **Other management: FOLATE · vaccination and, where splenectomised, prophylaxis · monitoring for the endocrine complications · osteoporosis screening · and stem cell transplantation in selected patients.**
> **GENETIC COUNSELLING AND ANTENATAL SCREENING are central**, and identifying a carrier obliges an offer of partner testing and counselling.

**Ix:** **FBC, BLOOD FILM and RETICULOCYTE count** (*why:* the film identifies the mechanism and the reticulocytes confirm marrow response; *what:* spherocytes, schistocytes, bite cells, sickle cells, target cells, agglutination). **LDH, bilirubin (split) and HAPTOGLOBIN** (*why:* the haemolysis screen — and haptoglobin is an acute phase reactant so interpret with the CRP; *what:* the haemolytic pattern). **DIRECT ANTIGLOBULIN TEST** (*why:* the branch point between immune and non-immune haemolysis; *what:* IgG or complement coating). **G6PD assay — REPEATED AFTER RECOVERY if taken during a crisis** (*why:* it is falsely normal acutely because the deficient cells have already lysed; *what:* enzyme activity). **Haemoglobin ELECTROPHORESIS or HPLC — with GENETIC testing for alpha thalassaemia, which has a normal electrophoresis** (*why:* diagnoses the haemoglobinopathy and has genetic counselling implications; *what:* HbS, raised HbA2, HbH). **Thick and thin films for MALARIA in any febrile haemolysis with travel history** (*why:* it kills fastest and is treatable; *what:* parasites and species). **Flow cytometry for PNH** where unexplained intravascular haemolysis, unusual-site thrombosis, or cytopenias (*why:* it is diagnosable and treatable, and thrombosis is what kills; *what:* CD55/CD59-deficient clone). **Coagulation profile in a microangiopathic picture** (*why:* NORMAL clotting favours TTP; deranged favours DIC — a genuinely useful discriminator; *what:* PT, APTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer). **Parvovirus B19 serology or PCR in a chronic haemolytic patient with a falling haemoglobin and LOW reticulocytes** (*why:* aplastic crisis; *what:* acute infection). **Cardiac and hepatic MRI T2\* in transfusion-dependent patients** (*why:* measures tissue iron directly, and cardiac iron is what kills, while ferritin correlates imperfectly; *what:* iron loading). Echocardiography (*why:* pulmonary hypertension in sickle cell disease, and iron cardiomyopathy in thalassaemia; *what:* pressures, function).
