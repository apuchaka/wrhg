## 0.1 Allergy — Mechanisms and Classification

> [!info] The Gell and Coombs classification, and why it is clinically useful
> **· Type I — IgE-mediated, IMMEDIATE.** Onset within **minutes to about two hours**. Preformed IgE on mast cells and basophils cross-links on allergen contact → degranulation → histamine and other mediators. **Anaphylaxis, urticaria, angioedema, allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, IgE-mediated food allergy.**
> **· Type II — cytotoxic, IgG or IgM against cell-surface antigens.** **Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, Goodpasture syndrome, some drug-induced cytopenias.**
> **· Type III — immune complex.** **Serum sickness, vasculitis, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, some drug reactions.** Onset days to weeks.
> **· Type IV — delayed, T-cell mediated.** Onset **48 hours to weeks.** **Contact dermatitis, tuberculin reaction, most delayed drug exanthems, and the severe cutaneous adverse reactions — SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP.** Cross-refer [[A5_Toxicology_II_-_Poisoned_Patient__ADRs_and_Immunotherapy]] 0.6.

> [!tip] The timing of the reaction tells you the mechanism, and the mechanism tells you the danger
> **A reaction within an hour, with urticaria, angioedema, wheeze or hypotension, is IgE-mediated — and re-exposure risks anaphylaxis.**
> **A reaction days later, as a non-urticarial rash, is T-cell mediated — usually benign, but the severe forms (SJS/TEN, DRESS) are also T-cell mediated and are absolute contraindications to re-exposure.**
> **This distinction is the entire basis of drug allergy assessment** — see 0.3.

> [!danger] Sensitisation is not allergy
> **A positive skin prick test or specific IgE demonstrates SENSITISATION — the presence of IgE. It does not demonstrate clinical allergy.**
> **Many people are sensitised to foods they eat without any problem at all.**
> **Allergy = sensitisation PLUS clinical reactivity on exposure.**
> This is the single most important concept in allergy testing, and misunderstanding it is the reason **untargeted allergy panels cause harm**: they generate positives that lead to unnecessary elimination diets, nutritional deficiency, social restriction, anxiety — and, in children, **avoidance of a food actually increases the risk of developing genuine allergy to it.** See 0.4.

**S/Smx:** Determined by the mechanism and the organ involved.

**Ix:** See 0.2.

## 0.2 Allergy Assessment and Testing

> [!danger] The history is the diagnostic test. The laboratory tests only support it.
> **Ask, in detail:**
> **· What exactly was the exposure**, and how much?
> **· How long after exposure did symptoms begin?** — the single most discriminating question, per 0.1.
> **· What exactly happened?** Urticaria, angioedema, wheeze, vomiting, hypotension, collapse — versus flushing, nausea, headache, or a rash appearing days later.
> **· How long did it last, and what treatment was needed?**
> **· Has it happened again on re-exposure — and has the person had the food or drug since without problem?** **Tolerating the substance since effectively excludes the allergy.**
> **· Co-factors** — **exercise, alcohol, NSAIDs, intercurrent infection, menstruation** — which lower the reaction threshold and explain why someone reacts on one occasion and not another.

> [!tip] The tests, and what each is for
> **· Skin prick testing** — detects specific IgE, results in about 15 minutes, high negative predictive value. **Antihistamines must be withheld beforehand** or the test is uninterpretable. Not reliable in extensive eczema or dermographism.
> **· Serum specific IgE** — no need to stop antihistamines, and usable when skin testing is not. Similar interpretation.
> **· Component-resolved diagnostics** — measures IgE to individual protein components rather than the whole extract. **This distinguishes genuine allergy from cross-reactive pollen sensitisation** — the classic example being peanut, where sensitisation to the storage protein predicts true systemic allergy while sensitisation to a pollen cross-reactive component predicts mild or no reaction. Useful for resolving equivocal results.
> **· Oral food challenge — the gold standard**, performed in a supervised setting with resuscitation available. **It is the only test that definitively establishes or excludes clinical allergy**, and it is under-used relative to how much unnecessary avoidance it could end.
> **· Patch testing** — for **type IV** contact allergy, read at 48 and 96 hours.
> **· Tryptase** — **an acute level taken within hours of a suspected anaphylactic event, with a baseline taken later**, supports mast cell degranulation retrospectively. **A persistently raised baseline suggests mastocytosis or hereditary alpha tryptasaemia** and warrants investigation.
> `UNVERIFIED — antihistamine withholding periods, tryptase timing and thresholds, and challenge protocols.`

> [!danger] Unvalidated tests cause real harm — and counselling about them is an OSCE-level skill
> **IgG or IgG4 "food intolerance" testing, hair analysis, applied kinesiology, VEGA/electrodermal testing, cytotoxic food testing, and pulse testing have no diagnostic validity.**
> **IgG to food indicates exposure and tolerance, not allergy** — everyone has it to foods they eat regularly.
> **The harm is concrete**: patients undertake extensive unnecessary elimination diets, causing nutritional deficiency, weight loss, disordered eating patterns, social isolation, cost, and — most seriously — **delay in diagnosing the actual problem**, which is often coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease or a functional gut disorder.
> **How to handle it well**: do not dismiss the symptoms, which are real. Explain what the test does and does not measure, offer a proper assessment, and give a plan. **Contempt for the test tends to be heard as contempt for the patient**, and they simply go elsewhere.

**Ix:** As above, **selected on the basis of the history rather than ordered as a panel** (*why:* untargeted testing generates clinically meaningless positives that cause avoidance and harm; *what:* targeted confirmation of a clinical suspicion).

## 0.3 Drug Allergy and De-labelling

> [!danger] Most people labelled "penicillin allergic" are not
> **The large majority of penicillin allergy labels are incorrect.** Most were applied in childhood, and most represent **a viral exanthem coinciding with an antibiotic course, gastrointestinal upset, or a family member's reaction.** Even among those with genuine IgE-mediated allergy, **sensitivity wanes over time and most lose it after a decade.**
> **The label is not harmless. It causes:**
> **· Use of broader-spectrum, less effective, more toxic alternatives.**
> **· Worse clinical outcomes — higher rates of surgical site infection, MRSA, VRE and *Clostridioides difficile*.**
> **· Longer hospital stays and higher costs.**
> **· Increased antimicrobial resistance at a population level.**
> **De-labelling an incorrect penicillin allergy is one of the highest-value things a junior doctor can do**, and it takes a careful history rather than a test.

> [!tip] The history that sorts it
> **Features suggesting TRUE IgE-mediated allergy:** onset **within an hour** of the dose · **urticaria, angioedema, wheeze, stridor, hypotension, anaphylaxis** · required adrenaline or emergency treatment · reproducible on re-exposure.
> **Features suggesting it is NOT allergy:** **isolated gastrointestinal upset** (nausea, diarrhoea) · headache · **a delayed, non-itchy, non-urticarial rash in a child who also had a viral illness** · a family history only · a reaction the patient cannot describe · an event decades ago with no detail · a vasovagal episode during an injection · candidiasis.
> **The most useful single question: "have you had any penicillin-type antibiotic since?"** — **tolerating amoxicillin, flucloxacillin or a cephalosporin since the index event effectively excludes the allergy**, and this information is frequently already in the record.

> [!danger] The absolute contraindications — never re-expose, never de-label
> **· Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis**
> **· DRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)**
> **· Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis**
> **· Drug-induced organ injury — hepatitis, interstitial nephritis, pneumonitis**
> **· Drug-induced haemolysis or cytopenia**
> **· Serum sickness**
> **· Documented anaphylaxis**
> **These are not candidates for rechallenge under any circumstance, and the whole drug class is generally avoided.** Cross-refer [[A5_Toxicology_II_-_Poisoned_Patient__ADRs_and_Immunotherapy]] 0.6.
> **Everything else is potentially assessable**, and low-risk histories may be suitable for **direct oral rechallenge** in an appropriate setting, with higher-risk histories going through skin testing and graded challenge with immunology. `UNVERIFIED — risk stratification tools and rechallenge protocols.`

> [!warning] Cross-reactivity is lower than traditionally taught
> **Penicillin–cephalosporin cross-reactivity is substantially lower than the historical figures suggest**, and the risk depends on **side chain similarity rather than the shared beta-lactam ring.** Cephalosporins with dissimilar side chains can generally be used even in confirmed penicillin allergy.
> The old blanket rule of avoiding all beta-lactams denies patients the best available antibiotic on the basis of an over-estimate. `UNVERIFIED — the actual cross-reactivity figures and which cephalosporins are considered safe.`

> [!tip] Four specific non-allergy reactions worth knowing
> **· Radiocontrast reactions** — **these are NOT IgE-mediated and are NOT "iodine allergy".** **A shellfish allergy is NOT a contraindication to iodinated contrast**, and this myth persists widely and delays necessary imaging. The relevant allergen in shellfish is tropomyosin, not iodine. A previous contrast reaction is a risk factor for another; shellfish allergy is not.
> **· NSAID hypersensitivity** — usually pharmacological (COX-1 inhibition shunting arachidonic acid to leukotrienes) rather than IgE-mediated, so it is **cross-reactive across all NSAIDs.** **Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease** is the triad of **asthma, nasal polyps and NSAID sensitivity.**
> **· Local anaesthetic "allergy"** — **true IgE-mediated allergy to local anaesthetics is very rare.** Most reported events are **vasovagal episodes, palpitations from the adrenaline in the preparation, or anxiety.** Formal assessment usually clears the patient.
> **· Perioperative anaphylaxis** — the likely culprits are **neuromuscular blocking agents, antibiotics (usually given at induction), chlorhexidine and latex.** **It requires an acute tryptase, a baseline tryptase later, and formal specialist investigation to identify the agent** — because without it the patient carries an "anaesthetic allergy" label of unknown content into their next operation, which is genuinely dangerous.

> [!warning] Document what actually happened
> **"Allergy: penicillin" with no further detail is close to useless** and it propagates unchallenged for decades.
> **Record: the drug, the reaction, the time from dose to onset, the treatment required, and the date.** That record is what allows the next clinician to assess it rather than simply accept it.

**Ix:** **A structured allergy history** (*why:* it is the diagnostic test, and it resolves most labels without any investigation; *what:* timing, features, subsequent tolerance). Review of the medical record for subsequent beta-lactam exposure (*why:* documented tolerance since the event excludes the allergy and is often already recorded; *what:* prior administration without reaction). **Skin testing and graded oral challenge by an immunology service** for higher-risk histories (*why:* definitively resolves the label; *what:* reaction or tolerance). **Acute and baseline tryptase after a suspected perioperative or severe reaction** (*why:* confirms mast cell degranulation and supports later investigation; *what:* acute rise falling to baseline).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat the reaction. **Stop the drug and, for a severe reaction, all non-essential drugs.**

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
De-label where the history is clearly not allergic. Refer for formal assessment where the label matters clinically and the history is unclear.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Update the record, the discharge summary, the GP and the patient's own understanding** — a de-labelling that is not communicated to the GP will be reversed at the next presentation. Provide the patient with written documentation of what was found.

## 0.4 Food Allergy and the Prevention of Allergy

> [!warning] Australia has among the highest rates of childhood food allergy in the world
> The common allergens are **egg, cow's milk, peanut, tree nuts, sesame, soy, wheat, fish and shellfish.** **Sesame is now a declared allergen requiring labelling in Australia.**
> **Natural history differs by food:** milk, egg, soy and wheat allergy are frequently **outgrown** in childhood; **peanut, tree nut, fish and shellfish allergy usually persist for life.**

> [!danger] Early introduction PREVENTS allergy — this reverses the older advice
> **The previous recommendation to delay introduction of allergenic foods has been abandoned and reversed.**
> **Current Australian guidance is to introduce solid foods from around six months (and not before four months), including the common allergens — particularly peanut and egg — in the first year of life, and then to CONTINUE giving them regularly.**
> **Regular ongoing exposure matters as much as the initial introduction** — introducing peanut once and then stopping does not maintain tolerance.
> **This applies to infants with eczema and existing food allergy too, who are at highest risk and stand to benefit most**, though these infants should be discussed with a clinician first.
> `UNVERIFIED — the exact ASCIA infant feeding recommendations, including advice for high-risk infants and the frequency of ongoing exposure.`
> **Parents who were told the opposite ten years ago will need this explained**, and the explanation is worth giving properly.

> [!tip] Co-factors explain "why only sometimes"
> **A patient who eats a food repeatedly without problem and then reacts to it once has usually encountered a co-factor: exercise, alcohol, NSAIDs, intercurrent infection, or menstruation.**
> **Food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis** — the food alone is tolerated, and exercise within a few hours of eating it triggers anaphylaxis. **Wheat (omega-5 gliadin) is the classic culprit.** This is genuinely puzzling to patients and is missed unless the temporal relationship to exercise is asked about.

> [!tip] Pollen-food syndrome (oral allergy syndrome)
> **Itching and mild swelling of the lips, mouth and throat on eating RAW fruits, vegetables or nuts, in someone with pollen allergy** — caused by cross-reactivity between labile plant proteins and pollen allergens.
> **The proteins are destroyed by heat, so the cooked food is tolerated** — which is the diagnostic clue.
> **It is usually mild and rarely causes anaphylaxis**, so it does not generally warrant an adrenaline autoinjector or total avoidance — an important distinction from true food allergy, and one that prevents a lot of unnecessary restriction.

> [!warning] Allergy versus intolerance — a distinction worth being able to explain clearly
> **Food allergy is immune-mediated and reproducible, and small amounts can cause severe reactions.**
> **Food intolerance is not immune-mediated** — **lactose intolerance (enzyme deficiency), FODMAP sensitivity, food chemical sensitivity, caffeine and alcohol effects.** It is dose-dependent, does not cause anaphylaxis, and does not require an autoinjector.
> **Patients frequently use "allergy" for both**, and clarifying it changes both the assessment and the level of restriction required. **This is common OSCE material.**

**Ix:** **History first, then targeted testing** (*why:* untargeted panels generate sensitisation without allergy and lead to unnecessary avoidance; *what:* specific IgE or skin prick to the suspected food only). **Component-resolved testing where the result is equivocal** (*why:* distinguishes true allergy from pollen cross-reactivity; *what:* component-specific IgE). **Supervised oral food challenge** (*why:* the definitive test, and the way most unnecessary avoidance is ended; *what:* tolerance or reaction). Growth and nutritional assessment in children on elimination diets (*why:* multiple exclusions cause faltering growth and deficiency; *what:* growth trajectory, intake). Coeliac serology where wheat is implicated (*why:* coeliac disease is a distinct, common and different problem; *what:* tissue transglutaminase IgA — cross-refer [[C5_Bowel_Habit__Obstruction_and_Distension]] 0.4).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Anaphylaxis management per [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.9.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
**Targeted avoidance of the confirmed allergen only** — not blanket avoidance of everything that tested positive. Dietitian involvement, particularly in children and in multiple allergies.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**ASCIA action plan, adrenaline autoinjector with demonstrated technique, medical alert, and school or childcare management plans.** Re-assessment over time, since many childhood allergies are outgrown and **the label should be revisited rather than carried indefinitely.** Cross-refer 0.7.

## 0.5 Allergic Rhinitis and the Atopic March

> [!tip] The atopic march
> A characteristic sequence in atopic individuals: **eczema in infancy → food allergy → allergic rhinitis → asthma.** Not every child follows it, but recognising the pattern predicts risk and prompts earlier assessment.
> **Early aggressive eczema management may reduce progression**, on the basis that allergen sensitisation occurs through a disrupted skin barrier — which is the same logic that underlies early oral introduction in 0.4.

> [!warning] Allergic rhinitis is under-treated and it matters more than it appears
> **It impairs sleep, daytime concentration, school performance and work productivity**, and children are frequently labelled as inattentive when they are chronically obstructed and sleeping badly.
> **"United airways" — allergic rhinitis and asthma are the same inflammatory process in a continuous airway, and treating the rhinitis improves asthma control.** **Ask every asthmatic about nasal symptoms**, because it is a modifiable contributor that is routinely not addressed. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.1.

> [!tip] Management, with the one thing that determines success
> **· Allergen avoidance** — logical, but of limited efficacy for house dust mite in practice, and worth being honest about rather than prescribing elaborate and expensive measures with poor evidence.
> **· Intranasal corticosteroid — first-line for moderate to severe persistent rhinitis**, and more effective than antihistamines for congestion.
> **The technique determines whether it works**: **spray with the head slightly forward, using the opposite hand, aiming AWAY from the septum and toward the outer wall, and do not sniff hard.** Aiming at the septum causes epistaxis, irritation and, rarely, perforation — and it is what most patients do. **Demonstrating the technique is more valuable than the prescription.**
> **· Non-sedating oral antihistamines** — useful for sneeze, itch and rhinorrhoea, less so for congestion. **Avoid first-generation sedating antihistamines**, which impair cognition and driving and are still widely bought over the counter.
> **· Saline nasal irrigation** — cheap, effective, and under-recommended.
> **· Montelukast** — has a role, but carries **warnings about neuropsychiatric adverse effects including mood change, sleep disturbance and suicidal ideation**, which must be discussed and asked about at review.
> **· Intranasal decongestants** — **short-term only; prolonged use causes rhinitis medicamentosa with rebound congestion**, and patients become dependent on them.
> `UNVERIFIED — agents, doses, age restrictions and the current montelukast warnings.`

**Ix:** **Clinical diagnosis, with testing only where it will change management** — such as identifying a specific allergen for avoidance or for immunotherapy (*why:* rhinitis is diagnosed on history and examination, and testing everyone adds nothing; *what:* targeted skin prick or specific IgE). Nasal examination (*why:* identifies polyps, septal deviation and mucosal appearance, and **nasal polyps with asthma and NSAID sensitivity is Samter triad**; *what:* polyps, pale boggy mucosa). Spirometry (*why:* undiagnosed asthma is common in allergic rhinitis; *what:* reversible obstruction).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Not applicable.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Intranasal corticosteroid with taught technique, antihistamine, saline, and consideration of immunotherapy (0.7).

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Review technique and adherence, treat coexisting asthma, and consider immunotherapy in those with persistent symptoms despite optimal treatment.

## 0.6 Urticaria, Angioedema and Mast Cell Disorders

> [!danger] Chronic urticaria is usually NOT allergic — and extensive allergy testing is unhelpful
> **Acute urticaria (under six weeks)** is often triggered by infection, drugs or food.
> **Chronic urticaria (six weeks or more) is usually spontaneous and idiopathic, with an autoimmune basis in a substantial proportion. It is NOT usually caused by a food or an environmental allergen.**
> **Patients almost always want allergy testing**, and it almost always finds nothing relevant. **Explaining this well is the clinical skill** — the message is that the immune system is releasing histamine without an external trigger, that it is not dangerous, that it usually remits over months to years, and that it is treatable.
> **Treatment: a regular non-sedating antihistamine, taken DAILY rather than as needed — and up-titrated above the standard dose if required**, which is standard practice in urticaria and surprises patients and clinicians. **Omalizumab** for antihistamine-refractory disease. `UNVERIFIED — the permissible degree of up-dosing and the criteria for omalizumab.`
> **Chronic inducible urticarias** — **cold, cholinergic (heat and exertion), delayed pressure, dermographism, solar, aquagenic** — are diagnosed by the trigger and by provocation testing.

> [!danger] Angioedema WITHOUT urticaria is bradykinin-mediated until proven otherwise
> **This is the key distinction in angioedema, and it changes the treatment completely.**
> **· Angioedema WITH urticaria → histaminergic → responds to adrenaline, antihistamines and corticosteroids.**
> **· Angioedema WITHOUT urticaria → bradykinin-mediated → does NOT respond well to any of those.**
> **Bradykinin-mediated causes:**
> **· ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema** — may begin **years after starting the drug**, characteristically involves the lips, tongue and larynx, and **is an absolute lifelong contraindication to the whole class.** Cross-refer [[A2_Airway_Compromise__Stridor_and_Tracheostomy_Emergencies]] 0.2 and [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.9.
> **· Hereditary angioedema (C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency)** — **recurrent attacks from childhood or adolescence, a family history, NO urticaria and NO itch**, with attacks lasting days. **Abdominal attacks cause severe pain and vomiting and are repeatedly mistaken for a surgical abdomen, leading to unnecessary laparotomy.** **Laryngeal attacks are life-threatening.** Triggers include **trauma, DENTAL PROCEDURES, surgery, stress, infection and oestrogen (including the combined oral contraceptive).**
> **Specific treatments exist — C1 inhibitor concentrate and icatibant — and they work where adrenaline does not.** These patients should carry their own supply and a management plan, and **short-term prophylaxis is given before dental and surgical procedures.**
> **· Acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency** — later onset, associated with lymphoproliferative disease.

> [!warning] Red flags in urticaria that suggest it is something else
> **· Individual lesions lasting MORE than 24 hours in the same place** — ordinary urticarial weals migrate and resolve within hours.
> **· Painful or burning rather than itchy lesions.**
> **· Bruising or pigmentation as the lesion resolves.**
> **· Fever, arthralgia, or systemic upset.**
> **These suggest URTICARIAL VASCULITIS**, which requires **skin biopsy** and a search for an underlying cause (connective tissue disease, infection, malignancy, drugs) — a different disease with a different workup.

> [!tip] Mast cell disorders
> **Recurrent unexplained flushing, hypotension, syncope, abdominal pain and diarrhoea**, sometimes with anaphylaxis to stings or without an identifiable trigger.
> **A raised BASELINE tryptase is the screening clue** — either **mastocytosis** (with cutaneous urticaria pigmentosa and characteristic bone marrow findings) or **hereditary alpha tryptasaemia**, an increasingly recognised common genetic cause of an elevated baseline tryptase.
> **Anyone with anaphylaxis and no identifiable trigger, or unusually severe insect sting reactions, should have a baseline tryptase measured.**

**Ix:** **Baseline tryptase** (*why:* screens for mastocytosis and hereditary alpha tryptasaemia, both of which change risk and management; *what:* persistently elevated level). **C4, C1 inhibitor level AND C1 inhibitor function** in angioedema without urticaria (*why:* **C1 inhibitor may be present but non-functional in one subtype, so measuring only the level misses it — the functional assay is required**; *what:* low C4, low level or function). FBC, ESR, CRP (*why:* systemic inflammation suggesting vasculitis or an underlying disorder; *what:* raised markers). **Skin biopsy where lesions last beyond 24 hours or leave bruising** (*why:* urticarial vasculitis; *what:* leukocytoclastic vasculitis). Thyroid function and autoantibodies (*why:* autoimmune thyroid disease is associated with chronic spontaneous urticaria; *what:* abnormal TSH, antibodies). **Medication review, including ACE inhibitors and NSAIDs** (*why:* both cause or exacerbate; *what:* culprit drug). **Extensive food and environmental allergy testing is NOT indicated in chronic spontaneous urticaria** (*why:* it does not find a cause and generates misleading positives; *what:* not indicated).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Airway assessment in any angioedema involving the tongue or larynx. **Adrenaline if anaphylaxis cannot be excluded**, even in suspected bradykinin-mediated angioedema — but recognise it may not work, and give the specific agent where hereditary angioedema is known.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Regular up-titrated antihistamine for chronic urticaria. Cessation and permanent documentation of an ACE inhibitor. Specific therapy and a management plan for hereditary angioedema.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Family screening in hereditary angioedema.** Patient-held emergency medication and a written plan, including **prophylaxis before dental and surgical procedures.** Immunology follow-up.

## 0.7 Immunotherapy and Long-Term Allergy Management

> [!tip] Allergen immunotherapy — the only disease-modifying treatment in allergy
> Repeated administration of increasing doses of allergen, **subcutaneously or sublingually**, over a course of years, inducing tolerance.
> **Established indications: allergic rhinitis and rhinoconjunctivitis not controlled by optimal medical therapy · selected allergic asthma · and — with the strongest evidence of all — venom allergy.**
> **It requires a multi-year commitment**, and its benefit persists after the course is completed, which distinguishes it from symptomatic treatment.
> `UNVERIFIED — indications, duration, and Australian availability and funding.`

> [!info] Venom immunotherapy — and a distinctly Australian element
> **Venom immunotherapy is highly effective at preventing subsequent systemic sting reactions and is indicated after a systemic reaction to a sting**, particularly anaphylaxis.
> **In Australia the relevant insects are honey bees, wasps, and the JACK JUMPER ANT (*Myrmecia pilosula*)** — a significant cause of sting anaphylaxis in **Tasmania, Victoria and parts of South Australia.**
> **Jack jumper ant venom immunotherapy was developed in Australia and is a genuinely Australian contribution**, available through specialist centres. Worth knowing both clinically and as an example of locally relevant practice that overseas sources will not cover.
> **A large local reaction to a sting is not an indication for immunotherapy or an autoinjector** — only systemic reactions are.

> [!tip] Biologics in allergic disease
> **· Omalizumab (anti-IgE)** — severe allergic asthma and chronic spontaneous urticaria.
> **· Mepolizumab, benralizumab (anti-IL-5 pathway)** — severe eosinophilic asthma.
> **· Dupilumab (anti-IL-4/IL-13)** — severe atopic dermatitis, asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with polyps.
> These are specialist-initiated with defined eligibility and PBS criteria. `UNVERIFIED — indications and PBS authority requirements.`

> [!danger] After anaphylaxis — the long-term package that is routinely incomplete
> **· An adrenaline autoinjector, with the technique DEMONSTRATED and the patient's return demonstration observed** — not simply prescribed. **Technique degrades over time, so re-check it at every opportunity.** Trainer devices should be provided.
> **· Two devices** where the patient is far from medical help or has had a severe reaction, since a second dose is sometimes needed.
> **· An ASCIA Action Plan** — the standardised Australian document, personalised and given to the patient, school or workplace.
> **· A medical alert bracelet or equivalent.**
> **· Written allergen avoidance advice**, including reading food labels and asking in restaurants.
> **· Allergy or immunology referral** for confirmation of the trigger, since **an incorrectly identified allergen leaves the real one in the diet.**
> **· Documentation in the record and to the GP.**
> **· Checking the device has not expired** — expired autoinjectors are a common and entirely preventable failure.
> Cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.9.

> [!warning] Immunodeficiency
> Primary and secondary immunodeficiency, the warning signs, and the screening required before immunosuppression are built in **[[K3_Exposure__Tuberculosis__HIV_and_Immunodeficiency]] 0.5** and are not repeated here. The link between the two fields matters clinically: **allergy and immunodeficiency both present to immunology services, and some primary immunodeficiencies — notably common variable immunodeficiency — present with autoimmune and allergic-appearing features rather than infection.**

**Ix:** Specific IgE or skin prick testing to identify the allergen for immunotherapy (*why:* immunotherapy targets a defined allergen and must match the clinical history; *what:* confirmed sensitisation matching the history). **Baseline tryptase before venom immunotherapy** (*why:* an elevated baseline indicates mast cell disorder, which increases the risk of severe sting reactions and affects the treatment plan; *what:* elevated level). Spirometry (*why:* uncontrolled asthma increases the risk of immunotherapy reactions and should be optimised first; *what:* airflow obstruction).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Not applicable.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Immunotherapy or biologic therapy through a specialist service.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Re-check autoinjector technique and expiry at every contact.** Reassess childhood food allergies over time rather than assuming permanence. Ongoing avoidance education and action plan review.
