## 0.1 Approach to a Neck Lump

> [!danger] Three questions — age, site, duration
> **1. HOW OLD IS THE PATIENT? This is the single most useful discriminator.**
> **· CHILD or YOUNG ADULT → most neck lumps are REACTIVE/INFLAMMATORY or CONGENITAL.**
> **· ADULT OVER 40, particularly a SMOKER or drinker → A NECK LUMP IS MALIGNANT UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE — usually a metastatic node from a head and neck primary.**
> **2. WHERE IS IT?** See the anatomical map below.
> **3. HOW LONG HAS IT BEEN THERE?**
> **· Days, tender, with an obvious infective source → reactive.**
> **· Weeks to months, painless, progressively enlarging → neoplastic.**
> **· Present for years, unchanged → likely benign or congenital.**

> [!tip] The anatomical map
> **· MIDLINE — THYROGLOSSAL DUCT CYST (moves UP with TONGUE PROTRUSION) · thyroid (moves with SWALLOWING) · dermoid cyst · submental nodes.**
> **· ANTERIOR TRIANGLE — lymph NODES (the commonest) · BRANCHIAL CLEFT CYST (anterior to the upper third of sternocleidomastoid) · CAROTID BODY TUMOUR (pulsatile, and characteristically mobile SIDE-TO-SIDE but NOT UP AND DOWN, because it is tethered to the carotid bifurcation) · salivary gland (submandibular) · laryngocele.**
> **· POSTERIOR TRIANGLE — lymph nodes, including TUBERCULOUS nodes and LYMPHOMA · cystic hygroma · cervical rib.**
> **· PAROTID region — salivary gland pathology.** Cross-refer [[F3]] 0.6.
> **· SUPRACLAVICULAR FOSSA — ALWAYS PATHOLOGICAL.** **A LEFT supraclavicular node (VIRCHOW node) suggests abdominal malignancy — gastric, pancreatic, oesophageal, ovarian — via the thoracic duct. A RIGHT supraclavicular node suggests thoracic or oesophageal disease.** **There is no such thing as a benign reactive supraclavicular node in an adult.**

> [!warning] Examination — and the part that is skipped
> **The lump itself: SITE · SIZE · SHAPE · SURFACE · CONSISTENCY (soft, rubbery, firm, hard, fluctuant) · MOBILITY and FIXATION to skin or deep structures · PULSATILITY and bruit · TRANSILLUMINATION · TENDERNESS · movement with SWALLOWING and with TONGUE PROTRUSION.**
> **THEN — AND THIS IS THE PART THAT DETERMINES THE OUTCOME — EXAMINE THE DRAINAGE AREA:**
> **· The SCALP (part the hair — melanomas and skin cancers hide there) · the EARS and canals · the NOSE · the ENTIRE ORAL CAVITY including under the tongue · the OROPHARYNX · the THYROID · and the SKIN of the face and neck.**
> **· ALL cervical nodal levels, plus the AXILLAE, GROINS, abdomen (hepatosplenomegaly) and, where indicated, the breast and testes.**
> **A NECK NODE IN AN ADULT IS A METASTASIS UNTIL THE PRIMARY IS FOUND OR EXCLUDED — and the primary is frequently in a place that requires a nasendoscope to see.** **Which is why the referral is to ENT for panendoscopy, not to a surgeon for excision.**

## 0.2 Cervical Lymphadenopathy

> [!tip] Reactive versus concerning
> **REACTIVE NODES: small, soft or rubbery, MOBILE, often TENDER, with an identifiable infective source, and RESOLVING over weeks.** Common and normal, particularly in children, in whom small palpable cervical nodes are almost universal.
> **RED FLAGS: size above about 2 cm · PERSISTENCE beyond four to six weeks or PROGRESSIVE enlargement · HARD, FIXED or MATTED · NON-tender · SUPRACLAVICULAR location · associated B SYMPTOMS (fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss) · hepatosplenomegaly · and no identifiable infective cause.** Cross-refer [[J4]] 0.4 and [[B6]] 0.8.

> [!warning] The causes
> **· VIRAL — EBV, cytomegalovirus, and HIV SEROCONVERSION (test for it).**
> **· BACTERIAL — streptococcal and staphylococcal from a head and neck source; CAT SCRATCH DISEASE (*Bartonella*, with a history of a kitten scratch and a regional node that may suppurate); dental infection.**
> **· MYCOBACTERIAL — TUBERCULOUS lymphadenitis ("scrofula") presents as painless, matted, slowly enlarging nodes that may form a COLD ABSCESS and a discharging SINUS.** **Important in migrants from high-prevalence countries and in the immunosuppressed, and frequently diagnosed late.** **In young children, ATYPICAL (non-tuberculous) mycobacterial lymphadenitis causes a chronic, violaceous, painless node that may discharge.** Cross-refer [[K3]] 0.3.
> **· TOXOPLASMOSIS — often posterior cervical, with a mild systemic illness.**
> **· MALIGNANT — METASTATIC squamous cell carcinoma from a head and neck primary (the priority in adults) · LYMPHOMA · thyroid carcinoma · nasopharyngeal carcinoma · and metastases from below the clavicles (lung, breast, gastrointestinal, genitourinary) presenting in the supraclavicular fossa.**
> **· INFLAMMATORY — sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Kikuchi disease.**
> **· DRUGS — phenytoin and others.**

> [!danger] The biopsy rules run in opposite directions for the two main diagnoses
> **THIS IS THE HIGH-YIELD POINT AND IT IS EASY TO GET WRONG.**
> **· SUSPECTED METASTATIC SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA (an adult with a firm neck node): DO **NOT** PERFORM AN OPEN EXCISION BIOPSY AS THE FIRST STEP.**
> **Open biopsy of a metastatic node before the primary has been sought BREACHES TISSUE PLANES, seeds tumour, compromises subsequent neck dissection, and is associated with higher local recurrence.**
> **The correct pathway is: ENT referral → nasendoscopy and examination under anaesthesia with panendoscopy to find the primary → FINE NEEDLE ASPIRATION or CORE biopsy of the node → imaging → multidisciplinary discussion.**
> **· SUSPECTED LYMPHOMA: an EXCISIONAL biopsy IS required, because classification depends on NODAL ARCHITECTURE, which FNA destroys.** Cross-refer [[J4]] 0.4.
> **So the same node gets a needle if it is likely carcinoma and an excision if it is likely lymphoma — and the way to reconcile this is to REFER RATHER THAN OPERATE, and let the assessment determine the approach.**
> **The one rule to carry: DO NOT TAKE OUT AN ADULT'S NECK NODE WITHOUT AN ENT OPINION FIRST.**

## 0.3 Congenital and Midline Lumps

> [!tip] Thyroglossal duct cyst
> **A remnant of the thyroglossal duct, which descends from the foramen caecum at the tongue base through or around the HYOID BONE to the thyroid.**
> **THE SIGN: it is MIDLINE (or just off it) and it MOVES UPWARDS ON TONGUE PROTRUSION — because it remains tethered to the tongue base — as well as moving with swallowing.**
> **Usually presents in childhood or young adulthood, sometimes after becoming infected during an upper respiratory infection.**
> **Definitive treatment is the SISTRUNK PROCEDURE, which removes the cyst, the tract, AND THE CENTRAL PORTION OF THE HYOID BONE** — because simple excision leaves the tract and recurs.
> **One caution: rarely the cyst contains the patient's ONLY functioning thyroid tissue, so thyroid status and imaging are considered before excision.**

> [!danger] A "branchial cyst" in an adult over 40 may be a cystic metastasis
> **A true BRANCHIAL CLEFT CYST is a smooth, fluctuant swelling ANTERIOR TO THE UPPER THIRD OF STERNOCLEIDOMASTOID, typically presenting in adolescents and young adults, sometimes enlarging after an upper respiratory infection.**
> **BUT: A CYSTIC NECK MASS IN THAT SAME POSITION IN AN ADULT OVER ABOUT 40 IS FREQUENTLY A CYSTIC LYMPH NODE METASTASIS FROM AN HPV-RELATED OROPHARYNGEAL CARCINOMA — of the TONSIL or TONGUE BASE.**
> **This is increasingly common as HPV-related oropharyngeal cancer rises, and the primary is often small and asymptomatic while the node is large.**
> **Treating it as a branchial cyst and excising it is a serious error — it delays diagnosis and compromises definitive treatment.**
> **THE RULE: A NEW CYSTIC NECK LUMP IN AN ADULT IS A METASTASIS UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE, however "branchial" it looks.** See 0.5.

> [!tip] The others
> **· CYSTIC HYGROMA (macrocystic lymphatic malformation) — soft, TRANSILLUMINATES, usually in the POSTERIOR triangle, presenting in infancy; may enlarge with infection or haemorrhage and can compromise the airway.**
> **· DERMOID CYST — midline, but does NOT move with tongue protrusion.**
> **· CAROTID BODY TUMOUR (paraganglioma) — a slowly growing, PULSATILE mass at the carotid bifurcation, classically mobile side-to-side but not vertically, sometimes with a bruit.** **DO NOT BIOPSY — it is vascular. Image it (with the "lyre sign" of splayed carotid vessels) and refer.** A small proportion are secretory or part of a familial syndrome.
> **· LARYNGOCELE — an air-filled dilatation that may enlarge with Valsalva, associated with raised intralaryngeal pressure (glassblowers, wind instrument players) — and it can be secondary to a laryngeal tumour obstructing the saccule, so it warrants laryngoscopy.**

## 0.4 Thyroid Nodules

> [!tip] Assessment sequence
> **A thyroid lump MOVES WITH SWALLOWING.**
> **1. THYROID FUNCTION TESTS FIRST.** **A nodule in a patient with a SUPPRESSED TSH may be a functioning ("hot") nodule, which is almost never malignant and is managed as thyrotoxicosis with a nuclear medicine scan — a completely different pathway from a euthyroid nodule.** Cross-refer [[I1]] 0.3.
> **2. ULTRASOUND — which characterises the nodule and stratifies risk using standardised sonographic features (composition, echogenicity, shape, margin, and echogenic foci or microcalcification).**
> **3. FINE NEEDLE ASPIRATION, guided by the sonographic risk category and the size — not by size alone.** `UNVERIFIED — the risk stratification systems and the size thresholds for FNA.`
> **Thyroid nodules are extremely common and mostly benign, and the purpose of the pathway is to avoid operating on the many while identifying the few.**

> [!danger] The red flags in a thyroid nodule
> **· RAPID growth.**
> **· HARD, FIXED or irregular.**
> **· HOARSENESS — implying recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement, which means malignancy until proven otherwise.**
> **· Cervical LYMPHADENOPATHY.**
> **· STRIDOR or dysphagia.**
> **· Age extremes — a nodule in a CHILD or in someone over about 60.**
> **· MALE sex.**
> **· PREVIOUS NECK IRRADIATION — particularly in childhood, and including radiotherapy for lymphoma, which markedly increases thyroid cancer risk and is frequently not asked about.**
> **· FAMILY HISTORY of thyroid cancer, or of MEN2 (medullary thyroid carcinoma with phaeochromocytoma and hyperparathyroidism).**

> [!warning] The cancers, and the one limitation of FNA
> **· PAPILLARY — the commonest, generally excellent prognosis, spreads via LYMPHATICS to cervical nodes.**
> **· FOLLICULAR — spreads HAEMATOGENOUSLY (bone, lung).**
> **THE KEY LIMITATION: FINE NEEDLE ASPIRATION CANNOT DISTINGUISH A FOLLICULAR ADENOMA FROM A FOLLICULAR CARCINOMA.** **The distinction depends on CAPSULAR AND VASCULAR INVASION, which requires the whole lesion — so a "follicular neoplasm" on FNA means DIAGNOSTIC HEMITHYROIDECTOMY, not reassurance.** **This is a favourite examination point and it explains why some patients with a benign-sounding cytology result still need surgery.**
> **· MEDULLARY — arises from parafollicular C cells, secretes CALCITONIN, associated with MEN2.** **Screen for phaeochromocytoma BEFORE thyroid surgery in suspected MEN2 — operating on an undiagnosed phaeochromocytoma is catastrophic.** Cross-refer [[I4]] 0.5.
> **· ANAPLASTIC — an elderly patient with a RAPIDLY enlarging, hard, fixed neck mass with hoarseness and stridor. Aggressive, with a very poor prognosis, and it is one of the few thyroid presentations that is a genuine emergency because of airway compromise.**
> **· LYMPHOMA — arising in a background of HASHIMOTO thyroiditis; consider it in rapid enlargement of a known Hashimoto gland.**
> **RETROSTERNAL GOITRE — may cause thoracic inlet obstruction, demonstrated by PEMBERTON SIGN (facial plethora, distress and venous distension on raising both arms above the head).**

## 0.5 Head and Neck Cancer

> [!danger] The presenting features — this is the list to know
> **Any of these in an adult, particularly a smoker or drinker over 40, warrants urgent ENT referral:**
> **· A NECK LUMP persisting beyond a few weeks — including a CYSTIC one.**
> **· An ORAL ULCER OR LESION persisting beyond THREE WEEKS.**
> **· HOARSENESS persisting beyond THREE WEEKS.**
> **· PERSISTENT UNILATERAL OTALGIA WITH A NORMAL EAR EXAMINATION.** Cross-refer [[F1]] 0.2.
> **· DYSPHAGIA or ODYNOPHAGIA.**
> **· UNILATERAL nasal obstruction or blood-stained discharge.** Cross-refer [[F2]] 0.5.
> **· UNILATERAL SEROUS OTITIS MEDIA IN AN ADULT.** Cross-refer [[F1]] 0.3 and [[F2]] 0.5.
> **· TRISMUS, a cranial nerve palsy, or unexplained facial numbness.**
> **· Unexplained weight loss with any of the above.**
> **Several of these are individually unremarkable symptoms, which is exactly why the rules are expressed as duration thresholds — three weeks, and refer.**

> [!warning] HPV-related oropharyngeal cancer is a different disease
> **The epidemiology of head and neck cancer has changed: while smoking- and alcohol-related cancers are declining, HPV-RELATED OROPHARYNGEAL CANCER (tonsil and tongue base) is RISING.**
> **The differences that matter:**
> **· YOUNGER patients, frequently NON-SMOKERS and non-drinkers — so the classic risk profile does not apply, and clinicians are less suspicious.**
> **· It typically presents with a PAINLESS NECK LUMP — often CYSTIC — rather than with local symptoms, because the primary is small and hidden in a tonsillar crypt or the tongue base.**
> **· It has a SIGNIFICANTLY BETTER PROGNOSIS than the smoking-related equivalents.**
> **· HPV VACCINATION, which in Australia is given to both boys and girls, is expected to reduce its incidence over coming decades.** Cross-refer [[O6]] 0.2.
> **The clinical consequence: a persistent neck lump in a fit, healthy, non-smoking 45-year-old is not reassuring.**

> [!tip] Treatment morbidity — and two things to arrange
> **Head and neck cancer treatment is among the most morbid in oncology, because the anatomy involved does swallowing, speech, breathing and appearance.**
> **The long-term consequences: DYSPHAGIA and aspiration · XEROSTOMIA (permanent after radiotherapy, causing rampant dental caries, candidiasis and dysphagia) · loss of TASTE · trismus · lymphoedema · voice and speech change · disfigurement · and substantial psychological and social impact, with high rates of depression.**
> **TWO THINGS TO ARRANGE THAT ARE FREQUENTLY OMITTED:**
> **1. DENTAL ASSESSMENT AND TREATMENT BEFORE RADIOTHERAPY.** **Irradiated mandible heals poorly, and a dental extraction AFTER radiotherapy risks OSTEORADIONECROSIS — a difficult, painful and sometimes disastrous complication.** **Any necessary dental work must be completed before treatment starts.** Cross-refer [[F3]] 0.6.
> **2. ANNUAL THYROID FUNCTION TESTS after neck radiotherapy.** **HYPOTHYROIDISM is common, develops months to years later, is easily treated, and is regularly missed because nobody is checking — with the fatigue attributed to the cancer or its treatment.** Cross-refer [[I1]] 0.2.
> **Also: smoking and alcohol cessation genuinely improves treatment outcomes and reduces SECOND PRIMARY cancers — the concept of FIELD CANCERISATION means the whole mucosa has been exposed, so these patients remain at risk of new primaries indefinitely and need long-term surveillance.**

## 0.6 Facial Pain and Swelling

> [!danger] Trigeminal neuralgia — and when to image
> **PAROXYSMAL, ELECTRIC-SHOCK-LIKE or stabbing pain, lasting SECONDS to a couple of minutes, in the distribution of the trigeminal nerve — most often V2 and V3 — with complete freedom between attacks.**
> **TRIGGERED by light touch, chewing, talking, brushing teeth, cold air or wind** — patients characteristically avoid touching the face, avoid shaving, and may lose weight because eating provokes it.
> **CARBAMAZEPINE is first-line and the response is often dramatic — a good response supports the diagnosis.** `UNVERIFIED — doses and monitoring.`
> **MRI IS INDICATED to exclude a secondary cause, and the features that make a secondary cause more likely are: AGE UNDER 40 · BILATERAL symptoms · SENSORY LOSS or other neurological signs · V1 involvement · and poor response to treatment.**
> **THE ONE TO KNOW: TRIGEMINAL NEURALGIA IN A YOUNG PATIENT, ESPECIALLY IF BILATERAL, SUGGESTS MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS.** Also cerebellopontine angle tumours and vascular compression. Cross-refer [[D1]] 0.4 and [[D4]] 0.1.

> [!warning] The other causes of facial pain
> **· DENTAL DISEASE — the commonest cause of facial pain overall, and the first thing to exclude. Percuss the teeth.**
> **· GIANT CELL ARTERITIS — JAW CLAUDICATION (pain on chewing that eases with rest, which is close to specific), temporal tenderness, headache, scalp tenderness and visual symptoms in a patient over 50.** **START CORTICOSTEROIDS IMMEDIATELY — do not wait for the biopsy or the ESR.** Cross-refer [[D1]] 0.3.
> **· TEMPOROMANDIBULAR JOINT DYSFUNCTION — pain around the joint and the ear, worse with chewing, with clicking, limited opening, tenderness over the joint and masseter, and often BRUXISM and stress.** **Managed conservatively: soft diet, jaw rest, analgesia, a splint, and addressing stress and clenching.**
> **· SINUSITIS — cross-refer [[F2]] 0.2.**
> **· SALIVARY — pain with eating suggests obstruction.** Cross-refer [[F3]] 0.6.
> **· HERPES ZOSTER of V1 — with HUTCHINSON SIGN (vesicles on the nose tip or side) predicting ocular involvement, and POST-HERPETIC NEURALGIA afterwards.** Cross-refer [[E1]] and [[G1]] 0.5.
> **· CLUSTER HEADACHE — strictly unilateral, severe, orbital or temporal, lasting 15 to 180 minutes, with prominent autonomic features (lacrimation, conjunctival injection, rhinorrhoea, ptosis) and AGITATION rather than the stillness of migraine.** Cross-refer [[D1]] 0.4.
> **· PERSISTENT IDIOPATHIC FACIAL PAIN — a constant, poorly localised, often bilateral deep ache that does not follow anatomical boundaries, with a normal examination and normal imaging.**
> **THE HARM TO AVOID: these patients undergo REPEATED DENTAL PROCEDURES — extractions, root canals, apicectomies — chasing a cause that is not there, each of which fails and some of which worsen the pain.** **Recognising the pattern, explaining it, and managing it with a chronic pain approach prevents that.** Cross-refer [[N6]] 0.2 and [[L3]] 0.5.
> **· BURNING MOUTH SYNDROME — a burning sensation with a normal-looking mouth. Exclude candidiasis, haematinic deficiency, diabetes, dry mouth and drug causes first.**

> [!tip] Facial swelling
> **· INFECTIVE — dental abscess (the commonest), sinusitis with periorbital involvement, salivary gland infection, cellulitis, and LUDWIG ANGINA.** Cross-refer [[F3]] 0.2.
> **· ANGIOEDEMA — allergic, ACE inhibitor-related (which can occur years after starting), or hereditary.** Cross-refer [[K4]] 0.1 and [[F3]] 0.2.
> **· SUPERIOR VENA CAVA OBSTRUCTION — facial and upper limb swelling with distended non-pulsatile neck and chest wall veins, worse on bending forward, with a positive Pemberton sign.** Cross-refer [[J5]] 0.3.
> **· SYSTEMIC — nephrotic syndrome (periorbital oedema, characteristically worse in the morning), heart failure, hypothyroidism (myxoedema), Cushing syndrome, and superior vena caval or lymphatic obstruction from tumour.**
> **· FACIAL NERVE PALSY presenting as facial asymmetry rather than swelling — and the three things to check are FOREHEAD SPARING (which indicates an upper motor neurone lesion and means stroke pathway), the EAR (for Ramsay Hunt vesicles), and the PAROTID (for a mass, which means malignancy until proven otherwise).** Cross-refer [[D5]] and [[F3]] 0.6.

**Ix:** **A FULL EXAMINATION OF THE UPPER AERODIGESTIVE TRACT, scalp and skin in any adult with a neck lump** (*why:* the node is a metastasis until the primary is excluded, and the primary is often silent; *what:* the primary tumour). **URGENT ENT REFERRAL FOR NASENDOSCOPY BEFORE ANY NECK BIOPSY IN AN ADULT** (*why:* open biopsy of a metastatic node compromises subsequent neck dissection and increases recurrence; *what:* the primary, and the correct biopsy approach). **FNA or CORE biopsy for suspected carcinoma; EXCISIONAL biopsy for suspected LYMPHOMA** (*why:* the requirements are opposite — carcinoma must not be opened, lymphoma requires architecture; *what:* cytology or histology). **THYROID FUNCTION TESTS BEFORE imaging a thyroid nodule** (*why:* a suppressed TSH indicates a functioning nodule requiring a completely different pathway; *what:* TSH). **Thyroid ULTRASOUND with risk stratification, then FNA by category** (*why:* avoids operating on the many benign nodules; *what:* sonographic risk features). **Calcitonin and screening for phaeochromocytoma where medullary carcinoma or MEN2 is suspected — BEFORE surgery** (*why:* operating on an undiagnosed phaeochromocytoma is catastrophic; *what:* calcitonin, metanephrines). **HIV serology, EBV serology, and consideration of TB in persistent lymphadenopathy** (*why:* all present with cervical nodes and all are treatable; *what:* the infective cause). **MRI in trigeminal neuralgia, particularly under 40 or if bilateral** (*why:* multiple sclerosis and posterior fossa lesions; *what:* demyelination, tumour, vascular loop). **DENTAL ASSESSMENT BEFORE head and neck radiotherapy** (*why:* prevents osteoradionecrosis, and extraction after radiotherapy is hazardous; *what:* dental disease requiring pre-treatment). **ANNUAL TFTs after neck radiotherapy** (*why:* hypothyroidism is common, late, treatable and routinely missed; *what:* raised TSH).
