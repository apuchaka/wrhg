## 0.1 Tachyarrhythmia — The Approach

**D:** A heart rate above the normal range arising from an abnormal rhythm rather than an appropriate physiological response.

> [!danger] The first question is always stability, not rhythm
> **Adverse features — shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia, or heart failure — mean the patient needs synchronised DC cardioversion now**, regardless of what the rhythm turns out to be. Do not spend time on rhythm analysis in an unstable patient, and do not attempt drug therapy first.
> Conversely, in a **stable** patient there is time to obtain a 12-lead ECG, think, and get help. The commonest errors are cardioverting a stable patient in a hurry and drug-treating an unstable one slowly.
> `UNVERIFIED — synchronised cardioversion energies, sedation, and the number of attempts before escalation are omitted; obtain from ANZCOR Guideline 11.9.`

> [!tip] The four-box classification
> Once stable, two questions sort almost every tachycardia:
> | | **Regular** | **Irregular** |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Narrow (QRS normal)** | Sinus tachycardia · AVNRT · AVRT · atrial flutter with fixed block · atrial tachycardia | **AF** · flutter with variable block · **multifocal atrial tachycardia** |
> | **Broad (QRS wide)** | **VT until proven otherwise** · SVT with aberrancy · antidromic AVRT | **Polymorphic VT / torsades** · **pre-excited AF** · AF with bundle branch block |
> **The irregular broad-complex box contains the two rhythms where the wrong drug kills**, which is why it is worth recognising as a box rather than as a list.

> [!warning] Is the tachycardia the problem, or the response to it?
> **Sinus tachycardia is almost never the primary problem.** It is the response to hypovolaemia, sepsis, pain, anxiety, hypoxia, anaemia, PE, thyrotoxicosis, alcohol withdrawal or a drug. **Rate-controlling a compensatory sinus tachycardia removes the compensation and causes decompensation.** Ask what the heart is compensating for before slowing it.
> The same caution applies to AF with a rapid ventricular response in sepsis — the sepsis frequently needs treating more than the rate does.

**S/Smx:** Palpitations, dyspnoea, chest pain, lightheadedness, syncope, anxiety, polyuria (from atrial natriuretic peptide release in SVT). Adverse features as above.

**Ix:** **12-lead ECG during the tachycardia, and a rhythm strip** (*why:* a rhythm captured only on the monitor is frequently uninterpretable afterwards, and the tachycardia may terminate before anyone thinks to record it — the 12-lead during the episode is the single most valuable investigation and is routinely missed; *what:* rate, regularity, QRS width, P wave relationship, axis). Post-conversion 12-lead ECG (*why:* reveals pre-excitation, prior infarction, prolonged QT and Brugada pattern, all of which are invisible during the tachycardia and all of which change management; *what:* delta wave, Q waves, QT interval). UEC with potassium, magnesium and calcium (*why:* electrolyte disturbance both causes and perpetuates arrhythmia and is correctable; *what:* low potassium and magnesium). TFTs (*why:* thyrotoxicosis is a common and reversible driver, particularly of AF; *what:* suppressed TSH). FBC (*why:* anaemia precipitates and sustains tachycardia; *what:* haemoglobin). Troponin (*why:* interpreted with care — tachycardia itself raises troponin through demand ischaemia (type 2), so an elevation does not establish ACS as the cause; *what:* elevation in context — cross-refer [[B1_Chest_Pain_Framework_and_Cardiac_Biomarkers]] 0.5). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* structural heart disease strongly favours VT in a broad complex tachycardia, and identifies the cardiomyopathy that changes long-term management; *what:* LV function, wall motion, valvular disease). Drug and toxicology history (*why:* stimulants, QT-prolonging drugs, digoxin toxicity and caffeine are all correctable causes; *what:* offending agent).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess stability. Unstable → synchronised cardioversion. Stable → 12-lead, correct electrolytes, identify the rhythm, treat accordingly, and get senior help.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Rhythm-specific, as below.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Identify and treat precipitants. Cardiology referral for recurrent or symptomatic arrhythmia, with consideration of ablation.

## 0.2 Narrow Complex Tachycardia

**D:** Tachycardia with a QRS of normal width, therefore originating at or above the AV node.

**A/P:** A narrow QRS means the ventricles are being depolarised through the normal His-Purkinje system, so the arrhythmia's origin must be supraventricular. **AVNRT** uses a re-entry circuit within the AV node itself; **AVRT** uses an accessory pathway connecting atrium and ventricle; **atrial flutter** is a macro-re-entrant circuit within the atrium; **AF** is disorganised atrial activity.

> [!tip] Regular at about 150 — think flutter
> Atrial flutter has an atrial rate of roughly 300, and with **2:1 conduction the ventricular rate is about 150 and metronomically regular.** Any regular narrow tachycardia near 150 should prompt a deliberate hunt for flutter waves — they hide in the T waves and are easiest to see in the inferior leads and V1. **Adenosine, by transiently blocking the AV node, unmasks them**, which makes it a diagnostic as well as a therapeutic agent.

> [!info] Vagal manoeuvres — do them properly
> The **modified Valsalva** — standard strain, then immediately laid flat with the legs passively raised — is substantially more effective than the conventional Valsalva and should be the default technique. It is free, it works in a meaningful proportion of SVT, and it is worth doing well before reaching for a drug. `UNVERIFIED — strain pressure and duration.`

> [!warning] Adenesine — warn the patient first
> Adenosine causes a brief but genuinely unpleasant sensation of chest tightness, flushing and impending doom, and patients who are not warned find it frightening. Explain it, have the defibrillator and a rhythm strip running, and give it **rapidly through a large proximal vein with an immediate flush** — its half-life is measured in seconds and a slow push simply does not work.
> **Caution in asthma**, where it can cause bronchospasm. **Reduce the dose** in patients on dipyridamole or with a transplanted heart, where the effect is markedly potentiated. `UNVERIFIED — doses, dose escalation and the adjustments above.`

> [!danger] Do not give AV nodal blocking drugs in pre-excited AF
> An **irregular, broad, very fast** tachycardia in a patient with Wolff-Parkinson-White is atrial fibrillation conducting down an accessory pathway. **Adenosine, verapamil, diltiazem, beta-blockers and digoxin block the AV node and thereby push conduction preferentially down the accessory pathway**, accelerating the ventricular rate and precipitating ventricular fibrillation.
> **Treat with synchronised cardioversion, or with an antiarrhythmic that acts on the accessory pathway** under specialist direction. This is one of the highest-consequence drug errors in acute cardiology and it is examined frequently.

> [!info] Multifocal atrial tachycardia
> **At least three distinct P wave morphologies with varying PR intervals, irregularly irregular, at a rate above 100.** It is routinely misdiagnosed as AF — **the presence of discrete P waves is what distinguishes it**, and looking for them is the whole diagnosis.
> Strongly associated with **severe COPD and hypoxia**, and with theophylline toxicity, hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia.
> **The treatment is the underlying lung disease and the electrolytes, not the rhythm.** Antiarrhythmics work poorly, cardioversion does not work at all (it is not re-entrant), and **beta-blockers are relatively contraindicated in the severe airways disease these patients have.** Magnesium and verapamil are sometimes used. Recognising MAT primarily means recognising that the patient's respiratory disease is decompensated.

**S/Smx:** Abrupt onset and offset palpitations in AVNRT and AVRT — patients often describe it starting and stopping "like a switch", which distinguishes it from sinus tachycardia. Neck pulsation from cannon A waves. Dyspnoea, chest discomfort, presyncope.

**Ix:** As per 0.1, with particular attention to the **post-conversion ECG for a delta wave** (*why:* identifies Wolff-Parkinson-White, which changes acute management, long-term management and driving and sporting advice; *what:* short PR with slurred QRS upstroke). Continuous rhythm recording during vagal manoeuvres and adenosine (*why:* the response is diagnostic — termination suggests an AV-node-dependent circuit, transient slowing unmasking flutter waves suggests atrial flutter, and no effect suggests VT or an atrial tachycardia; *what:* the response). Ambulatory monitoring where episodes are paroxysmal and undocumented (*why:* the diagnosis depends on capturing the rhythm; *what:* the arrhythmia during symptoms). TFTs and electrolytes as above.

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Unstable → cardioversion. Stable → vagal manoeuvres, then adenosine, then rate-limiting calcium channel blocker or beta-blocker as directed — **except in pre-excited AF.** Correct electrolytes.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
**Catheter ablation is curative in AVNRT and AVRT** with high success rates, and should be offered rather than committing a young patient to lifelong drug therapy. Flutter ablation is likewise highly effective.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Trigger avoidance — caffeine, alcohol, stimulants, sleep deprivation. Teach the patient the modified Valsalva so they can terminate episodes themselves. Cardiology follow-up, and driving advice where syncope occurred — cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.6.

## 0.3 Broad Complex Tachycardia

**D:** Tachycardia with a widened QRS, which must be treated as ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise.

> [!danger] Assume VT
> **The great majority of broad complex tachycardias are VT**, and the proportion rises to near-certainty in a patient with known ischaemic heart disease or previous myocardial infarction. **Treating VT as SVT is dangerous; treating SVT as VT is usually harmless.** Where the diagnosis is uncertain, treat as VT.
> **Verapamil given to a patient with VT causes profound hypotension and cardiac arrest.** This is the specific error the "assume VT" rule exists to prevent.

> [!tip] Features favouring VT over SVT with aberrancy
> **· AV dissociation** — P waves marching through at their own rate, independent of the QRS. The most specific sign, and worth hunting for.
> **· Capture beats** (a normal narrow QRS interrupting the run) and **fusion beats** (a hybrid morphology) — both essentially diagnostic.
> **· Very broad QRS**, extreme axis deviation, **concordance** across the precordial leads (all positive or all negative).
> **· Known structural heart disease or prior MI** — clinically the single most useful discriminator.
> Formal algorithms exist (Brugada, Vereckei) but are not needed to make the safe decision. `UNVERIFIED — algorithm criteria.`

> [!warning] Polymorphic VT and torsades de pointes
> **Torsades** is polymorphic VT on a background of a **prolonged QT**, with the characteristic twisting-around-the-baseline morphology.
> Causes of QT prolongation: **drugs** — many antipsychotics, antiemetics including ondansetron and metoclopramide, macrolides, quinolones, antiarrhythmics, methadone, and combinations of these · **electrolytes** — hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia · **congenital long QT syndromes** · bradycardia · hypothyroidism.
> **Management: intravenous magnesium regardless of the serum level, aggressive potassium and magnesium correction, and cessation of every QT-prolonging drug.** Increasing the heart rate shortens the QT, so overdrive pacing or isoprenaline is used in bradycardia-dependent torsades. **If pulseless, it is defibrillated.** `UNVERIFIED — magnesium dose and potassium targets.`
> **Check the medication chart** — polypharmacy with two or three QT-prolonging agents is a common iatrogenic cause and is entirely preventable.

**S/Smx:** Palpitations, chest pain, dyspnoea, presyncope, syncope, or cardiac arrest. Haemodynamic tolerance varies widely and **a patient who is talking to you can still be in VT** — stability does not exclude it.

**Ix:** 12-lead ECG during the tachycardia, examined specifically for AV dissociation, capture and fusion beats (*why:* these findings settle the diagnosis and are only visible on a 12-lead, not on a monitor strip; *what:* dissociated P waves, capture and fusion complexes). Post-conversion ECG (*why:* prior infarction, prolonged QT, Brugada pattern and epsilon waves of arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy all appear only in sinus rhythm; *what:* Q waves, QT, Brugada morphology). **Potassium, magnesium and calcium** (*why:* correctable and frequently causative, and magnesium is given in torsades regardless; *what:* low values). Troponin (*why:* ischaemia as a cause, interpreted with the demand-ischaemia caveat; *what:* elevation). Echocardiography (*why:* structural disease supports VT and identifies the cardiomyopathy driving it; *what:* impaired LV, scar, RV abnormality). Full medication review (*why:* QT-prolonging drug combinations are a common preventable cause; *what:* offending agents). Digoxin level where relevant (*why:* toxicity causes a range of arrhythmias and is treatable; *what:* level with potassium).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Unstable → **synchronised cardioversion.** Pulseless → **defibrillate**, cross-refer [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.4. Stable VT → antiarrhythmic under senior direction, with correction of electrolytes. Torsades → magnesium. **Do not give verapamil.**

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat ischaemia. Cardiology involvement. **Implantable cardioverter defibrillator** assessment for VT in the context of structural heart disease. Ablation for recurrent monomorphic VT.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Family screening** where a channelopathy or inherited cardiomyopathy is identified — long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, ARVC and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy all have heritable forms and the index case's relatives may be at risk of sudden death. Driving restriction and documentation of QT-prolonging drug avoidance.

## 0.4 Atrial Fibrillation and Flutter

**D:** Disorganised atrial electrical activity with an irregularly irregular ventricular response (AF), or organised macro-re-entrant atrial activity at around 300 per minute with variable AV conduction (flutter).

**A/P:** Atrial remodelling and ectopic activity, frequently originating around the **pulmonary vein ostia**, produce chaotic atrial depolarisation → loss of coordinated atrial contraction → **loss of the atrial contribution to ventricular filling**, which matters most in diastolic dysfunction and stenotic valve disease → plus **stasis in the left atrial appendage** → thrombus formation → embolic stroke. The stroke risk is the reason AF matters more than the palpitations suggest.

> [!danger] Anticoagulation is decided independently of everything else about the rhythm
> **The decision to anticoagulate depends on stroke risk, not on whether the AF is paroxysmal, persistent or permanent, and not on whether a rhythm control strategy has succeeded.**
> A patient in sinus rhythm after cardioversion or ablation still has the stroke risk their risk score describes. Stopping anticoagulation because "the AF is fixed" is a recognised and serious error.
> Risk is stratified with **CHA₂DS₂-VASc**, and bleeding risk considered with tools such as HAS-BLED — **which is used to identify and modify reversible bleeding risk factors, not to withhold anticoagulation.** `UNVERIFIED — score components, point allocations and the thresholds at which anticoagulation is recommended require verification against current CSANZ/Heart Foundation guidance.`

> [!warning] Which anticoagulant
> **Direct oral anticoagulants are preferred to warfarin** in most patients — comparable or better efficacy, less intracranial haemorrhage, no monitoring.
> **The exceptions requiring warfarin are mechanical prosthetic heart valves and moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis**, historically termed "valvular AF". DOACs are contraindicated in these.
> Dosing is adjusted for **renal function, age and weight**, and under-dosing a DOAC to reduce bleeding risk is common and leaves the patient with the bleeding risk and without the stroke protection. **Aspirin is not adequate stroke prevention in AF** and should not be used as a substitute.

> [!tip] Rate versus rhythm control
> Both are reasonable initial strategies for most patients, and **rate control does not mean giving up.** Rhythm control is favoured in younger patients, those with symptoms despite rate control, heart failure, and **early after first diagnosis**, where there is evidence that early rhythm control improves outcomes.
> Rate control agents: beta-blockers and non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, with digoxin as an adjunct — **digoxin controls rate at rest but poorly on exertion**, so it is a poor sole agent in an active patient.
> `UNVERIFIED — agent choice, doses and rate targets from eTG.`

> [!danger] The 48-hour rule for cardioversion
> Cardioverting AF of uncertain or greater than 48 hours' duration without either **adequate anticoagulation for a defined period beforehand** or a **transoesophageal echocardiogram excluding atrial thrombus** risks embolising a thrombus at the moment sinus rhythm and atrial contraction return.
> **Anticoagulation is also required for a period after cardioversion**, because atrial mechanical function ("atrial stunning") recovers more slowly than electrical function and the stasis persists. `UNVERIFIED — the duration cut-off, the pre- and post-cardioversion anticoagulation periods.`

> [!warning] Always look for the precipitant in new AF
> **Sepsis · thyrotoxicosis · alcohol binge ("holiday heart") · pulmonary embolism · electrolyte disturbance · post-operative state · myocardial ischaemia · obstructive sleep apnoea · mitral valve disease.** New AF in an unwell patient is frequently a symptom of the acute illness, and treating the illness often resolves the rhythm. **Checking the TSH in every new AF is a small habit that finds a treatable cause reasonably often.**

**S/Smx:** Palpitations, dyspnoea, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, chest discomfort, presyncope — or entirely asymptomatic and found incidentally, which is common and does not reduce the stroke risk. Irregularly irregular pulse with a pulse deficit.

**Ix:** ECG (*why:* confirms the rhythm and distinguishes AF from flutter and MAT; *what:* absent P waves with irregular ventricular response, or sawtooth flutter waves). **TFTs** (*why:* thyrotoxicosis is a specifically treatable cause and is easily missed in an older patient presenting only with AF; *what:* suppressed TSH). UEC, magnesium, FBC (*why:* electrolyte disturbance and anaemia as precipitants, and renal function determines DOAC dosing; *what:* potassium, magnesium, creatinine, haemoglobin). **Transthoracic echocardiography** (*why:* identifies valvular disease — particularly mitral stenosis, which changes the anticoagulant choice — assesses LV function and atrial size, and informs rhythm control decisions; *what:* valve lesions, LV function, atrial dimensions). Transoesophageal echocardiography before cardioversion where indicated (*why:* excludes left atrial appendage thrombus, which transthoracic imaging cannot reliably see; *what:* appendage thrombus). Ambulatory monitoring for paroxysmal AF (*why:* documents the rhythm and quantifies burden, and is relevant after cryptogenic stroke; *what:* AF episodes). Sleep study where OSA is suspected (*why:* untreated OSA substantially reduces the success of rhythm control and ablation; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Unstable → synchronised cardioversion. Stable → rate control, correct precipitants and electrolytes, and start the anticoagulation assessment.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Rate or rhythm control strategy, anticoagulation per risk, and treatment of the underlying cause. Catheter ablation for symptomatic patients failing or declining drug therapy — **pulmonary vein isolation** being the standard approach. Left atrial appendage occlusion in patients who cannot be anticoagulated.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Risk factor modification substantially reduces AF burden and improves ablation success** — weight loss, alcohol reduction, blood pressure control, treatment of sleep apnoea and exercise. This is genuinely disease-modifying and is under-emphasised relative to drugs and procedures. Annual review of anticoagulation, renal function and rate control.

## 0.5 Symptomatic Bradycardia

**D:** A heart rate below the normal range causing symptoms or haemodynamic compromise.

**R/Causes:**
**Physiological** — athletic training, sleep, vagal tone. **Drugs** — beta-blockers including eye drops, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, digoxin, amiodarone, clonidine, ivabradine, opioids, and cholinesterase inhibitors. **Ischaemia** — particularly inferior myocardial infarction, where sinus bradycardia and AV block are common, usually vagally mediated and often transient. **Degenerative conduction system disease** and sick sinus syndrome. **Metabolic** — hypothyroidism and myxoedema, hypothermia, **hyperkalaemia**, hypoxia. **Raised intracranial pressure** with the Cushing response. **Infection** — Lyme carditis, and aortic root abscess complicating endocarditis.

> [!info] AV block — the distinction that determines urgency
> **First degree** — prolonged PR, every beat conducts. Usually benign.
> **Second degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach)** — **progressive PR lengthening until a beat drops.** The block is usually **within the AV node**, it is often vagally mediated or drug-related, it responds to atropine, and it rarely progresses. Generally benign.
> **Second degree Mobitz II** — **constant PR interval with sudden unheralded dropped beats.** The block is **infranodal** (His-Purkinje). It is **not** reliably atropine-responsive, it progresses unpredictably to complete heart block, and it usually needs pacing. **This is the dangerous one.**
> **Third degree (complete)** — **AV dissociation** with an independent escape rhythm. A **narrow** escape suggests a junctional origin and relative stability; a **broad, slow** escape suggests a ventricular origin and is unstable.

> [!danger] Atropine may not work — and can make things worse
> Atropine acts by blocking vagal tone **at the AV node.** In **infranodal block** — Mobitz II and complete heart block with a broad escape — the AV node is not the problem, so atropine does not improve conduction. Worse, it can **increase the atrial rate without increasing conduction**, effectively increasing the degree of block.
> **Atropine is also ineffective in the transplanted (denervated) heart**, where there is no vagal innervation to block.
> In these situations move directly to **transcutaneous pacing and a chronotropic infusion**, and arrange transvenous pacing. `UNVERIFIED — atropine dose and maximum, infusion agents and rates, and transcutaneous pacing settings are omitted; obtain from ANZCOR Guideline 11.9.`

> [!warning] Transcutaneous pacing is painful and frequently ineffective
> It requires **adequate analgesia and sedation**, and **electrical capture must be confirmed mechanically** — a pacing spike followed by a QRS on the monitor does not mean the heart is contracting. **Feel for a pulse or confirm on echocardiography.** Failure to confirm mechanical capture is a common error that leads to a false sense of security in a patient who is not being perfused.

> [!tip] Bradycardia with a wide QRS — think potassium
> **Hyperkalaemia produces bradycardia with QRS widening**, peaked T waves and loss of P waves, progressing to a sine wave pattern and arrest. It is fast to check, immediately treatable, and will not respond to pacing. Check a gas.

**S/Smx:** Fatigue, exertional intolerance, dizziness, presyncope, syncope, confusion in the elderly, dyspnoea, chest pain, and — with adverse features — hypotension, heart failure and reduced conscious state.

**Ix:** 12-lead ECG and rhythm strip (*why:* classifies the block, which determines whether atropine will work and whether pacing is needed; *what:* PR relationship, dropped beats, AV dissociation, escape width). **VBG with potassium** (*why:* hyperkalaemia is a rapidly reversible cause that pacing will not address; *what:* potassium). **Full medication review including eye drops** (*why:* drug-induced bradycardia is the commonest reversible cause and topical beta-blockers are systemically absorbed and routinely overlooked; *what:* rate-limiting agents). Digoxin level (*why:* toxicity causes bradyarrhythmia and is treatable with specific antibody fragments; *what:* level in context of potassium and renal function). TFTs (*why:* hypothyroidism; *what:* raised TSH). Troponin and ECG for ischaemia (*why:* inferior MI causes AV block, and reperfusion may resolve it without pacing; *what:* inferior ST elevation). Core temperature (*why:* hypothermia; *what:* low temperature). Ambulatory monitoring where symptoms are intermittent (*why:* correlates symptoms with rhythm, which is the basis of a pacing decision in intermittent disease; *what:* symptom-rhythm correlation). Echocardiography (*why:* structural disease and, with fever, endocarditis with aortic root abscess, which is a surgical emergency presenting as new AV block; *what:* vegetations, abscess, LV function).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess for adverse features. Oxygen, IV access, monitoring. **Correct reversible causes — potassium, drugs, hypothermia, hypoxia.** Atropine where the block is nodal. **Transcutaneous pacing with sedation** and confirmed mechanical capture, or a chronotropic infusion, where atropine fails or the block is infranodal. Arrange transvenous pacing and cardiology involvement.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Cease the offending drug where possible. Reperfusion for ischaemic cause. **Permanent pacemaker** for irreversible symptomatic bradycardia, Mobitz II or complete heart block.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Device follow-up. Driving restrictions after syncope and after device implantation — cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.6. Medication review at every subsequent presentation, since rate-limiting drugs are frequently restarted by another team.

## 0.6 Cardiac Device Events

**D:** Problems arising in patients with a permanent pacemaker, implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) or cardiac resynchronisation device.

> [!danger] Electrical storm
> **Three or more appropriate ICD shocks within 24 hours.** This is a medical emergency with high mortality — the shocks are a symptom of recurrent ventricular arrhythmia, and the arrhythmia is the problem.
> Management involves treating the underlying trigger (ischaemia, electrolytes, heart failure decompensation, drug effect), **beta-blockade**, antiarrhythmic therapy, **sedation** — which is genuinely therapeutic, since sympathetic drive perpetuates the storm — and urgent cardiology and electrophysiology involvement. `UNVERIFIED — agents and doses.`

> [!warning] Inappropriate shocks
> Shocks delivered for a rhythm that is not ventricular arrhythmia — most often **AF with a rapid ventricular response**, sinus tachycardia, **lead fracture** or oversensing. They are extremely distressing and cause genuine psychological harm, and repeated inappropriate shocks are a common reason for presentation.
> **The device must be interrogated** to determine whether shocks were appropriate — this is not something that can be determined clinically, and it directs everything that follows.

> [!danger] Magnet response differs between pacemakers and ICDs — this is examined
> **A magnet placed over an ICD suspends tachyarrhythmia detection and therapy — it stops shocks. It does NOT affect the pacing function.**
> **A magnet placed over a pacemaker causes asynchronous pacing at a fixed magnet rate.** It does not "turn it off".
> Confusing these is dangerous. Applying a magnet to an ICD in a patient with genuine ongoing VT removes their protection. Applying one to a pacemaker expecting it to stop shocks achieves nothing.
> Magnet application to an ICD is used for **repeated inappropriate shocks**, in a monitored setting, with cardiology involvement, and the magnet must not be forgotten in place.

> [!tip] Device infection
> Presents as pocket pain, erythema, swelling, discharge or erosion, or as **bacteraemia and endocarditis** with no local signs at all. **Staphylococcal bacteraemia in a patient with any cardiac device should be assumed to involve the device until proven otherwise.**
> **Antibiotics alone rarely cure device infection — complete system removal, including the leads, is usually required.** Take blood cultures before antibiotics, obtain a transoesophageal echocardiogram, and involve cardiology and infectious diseases early.

> [!info] Other device problems worth recognising
> **Failure to capture** — pacing spikes with no following QRS. Lead displacement, fracture, exit block, or metabolic causes.
> **Failure to sense** — the device paces inappropriately because it does not see intrinsic beats, risking an R-on-T phenomenon.
> **Oversensing** — the device interprets noise, myopotentials or T waves as beats and inhibits pacing, which is dangerous in a pacing-dependent patient.
> **Twiddler's syndrome** — the patient manipulates the generator in its pocket, twisting and dislodging the leads.
> **Pacemaker syndrome** — symptoms of fatigue, dyspnoea and neck pulsation from loss of AV synchrony in single-chamber ventricular pacing.

> [!warning] Reading the ECG in a paced patient
> Right ventricular pacing produces a **left bundle branch block morphology**, which obscures the ST changes of myocardial infarction. The **Sgarbossa criteria** (and their modified version) are used to identify infarction in the presence of LBBB or ventricular pacing. `UNVERIFIED — criteria and point values.` Do not conclude "paced rhythm, uninterpretable" and stop — a patient with a device can still be having a STEMI.

**S/Smx:** Shocks (single or multiple), palpitations, syncope, dizziness, dyspnoea, pocket pain or swelling, fever, and hiccups or diaphragmatic twitching from phrenic nerve stimulation by a malpositioned lead.

**Ix:** **Device interrogation** (*why:* it is the definitive investigation — it records every arrhythmia, every therapy delivered, whether shocks were appropriate, and lead integrity, and no clinical assessment substitutes for it; *what:* stored electrograms, therapies delivered, lead impedance and thresholds). 12-lead ECG (*why:* identifies the underlying rhythm, capture and sensing problems, and ischaemia using Sgarbossa criteria; *what:* pacing spikes with or without capture, ST changes). CXR (*why:* lead position, fracture, displacement, and Twiddler's coiling are visible; *what:* lead integrity and position). UEC with potassium and magnesium (*why:* electrolyte disturbance both provokes arrhythmia and raises pacing thresholds; *what:* derangement). Troponin (*why:* ischaemia as the driver of an arrhythmic storm; *what:* elevation). **Blood cultures before antibiotics** where infection is suspected (*why:* organism identification determines management and the need for system removal; *what:* staphylococcal or other growth). Transoesophageal echocardiography for suspected device infection or endocarditis (*why:* transthoracic imaging poorly visualises leads and small vegetations; *what:* lead vegetations, valvular involvement). Chest and pocket examination (*why:* erosion and pocket infection are clinical diagnoses; *what:* erythema, fluctuance, erosion).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess the patient, not just the device. Continuous monitoring. Treat the underlying arrhythmia or precipitant. **Magnet for repeated inappropriate ICD shocks, in a monitored setting, with cardiology contacted.** Sedation and beta-blockade for electrical storm. Blood cultures before antibiotics if infection is possible.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Device interrogation and reprogramming. Lead revision. **Complete system extraction** for infection. Ablation for recurrent VT.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Regular device follow-up. **Psychological support after shocks** — anxiety and avoidance after an ICD shock are common, under-recognised and treatable. Driving restrictions after a shock or after implantation, which differ for appropriate and inappropriate therapy — cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.6. Advance care planning, including **deactivation of ICD shock therapy at end of life**, which should be discussed proactively rather than during a terminal admission where an unsuspended device will shock a dying patient repeatedly.
