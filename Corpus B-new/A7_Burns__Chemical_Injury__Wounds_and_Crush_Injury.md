## 0.1 Burns — Assessment

**D:** Tissue injury from thermal, chemical, electrical or radiation energy, classified by depth and by percentage of total body surface area involved.

**A/P:** Energy transfer denatures protein and causes coagulative necrosis at the point of contact, with concentric **zones of injury**: a central **zone of coagulation** (irreversibly dead), surrounded by a **zone of stasis** (compromised perfusion, potentially salvageable, and the tissue that early cooling and adequate resuscitation are trying to save), surrounded by a **zone of hyperaemia** (inflammation, will recover). **The zone of stasis can convert to necrosis with hypoperfusion, hypothermia, oedema or infection**, which is why the burn seen at 48 hours may be deeper than the burn seen at presentation — and why depth assessment is deferred and repeated.

> [!tip] Depth assessment
> | Depth | Appearance | Blisters | Capillary refill | Sensation | Healing |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | **Superficial (epidermal)** | Red, dry | **No** | Brisk | **Painful** | Days, no scar |
> | **Superficial partial (superficial dermal)** | Pink, moist | Yes | Brisk | **Very painful** | ~2 weeks, minimal scar |
> | **Deep partial (deep dermal)** | Blotchy red/white | Yes | **Sluggish or absent** | **Reduced** | Slow, scarring, often grafted |
> | **Full thickness** | White, waxy, leathery, charred | No | **Absent** | **Painless** | Will not heal, requires grafting |
> **Reduced sensation and absent capillary refill indicate a deeper burn — the painless burn is the worrying one.**

> [!danger] Superficial burns are NOT counted in the total body surface area
> Simple erythema — sunburn, a scald that is red but unblistered — is **excluded from the TBSA calculation.** Including it inflates the estimate, which then inflates the calculated fluid resuscitation and causes harm through over-resuscitation. This is one of the commonest errors in burns assessment.

> [!tip] Estimating TBSA
> **Rule of nines** in adults — a rapid approximation dividing the body into multiples of nine percent.
> **Lund and Browder chart** in children — required, because a child's head accounts for a proportionally much larger and the legs a smaller share of surface area than an adult's, and the rule of nines therefore systematically misestimates in children.
> **Palmar surface** — the patient's own palm including the fingers approximates a small fixed percentage, useful for scattered or irregular burns.
> `UNVERIFIED — the specific percentages assigned to each body region in the rule of nines, the palmar surface percentage, and the age-adjusted Lund and Browder values are omitted. Use a printed chart, which every emergency department has, rather than recall.`

> [!warning] Burns unit referral — the categories, not the numbers
> Referral is indicated for: burns exceeding a defined TBSA threshold · **any full thickness burn** · burns to **face, hands, feet, perineum, genitalia or across major joints** · **circumferential** burns · electrical and chemical burns · **inhalation injury** · burns at extremes of age · burns in patients with significant comorbidity · burns with associated trauma · and **any burn where non-accidental injury is suspected.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the TBSA percentage thresholds for referral, which differ for adults and children, are omitted. Obtain from ANZBA and your state burns service referral criteria.`

> [!danger] First aid: twenty minutes of cool running water, and it works late
> **Cool running water for 20 minutes is the single most effective first aid measure**, reducing burn depth and improving outcome. Critically, **it remains beneficial for up to three hours after the injury**, so it should be started even in a patient presenting hours later — this is frequently not done because the injury is assumed to be "set". This is standard ANZBA teaching and a reliable exam point.
> **Do not use ice or iced water** — it causes vasoconstriction, deepens the burn and risks hypothermia.
> **Watch for hypothermia** during cooling, particularly in children and large burns. Cool the burn, warm the patient.

**S/Smx:** Pain (or its absence in full thickness), erythema, blistering, eschar, oedema. Assess for inhalation injury: facial burns, singed nasal hair, carbonaceous sputum, hoarseness, stridor — cross-refer [[A2]] 0.2.

**Ix:** Serial depth and TBSA assessment (*why:* depth evolves over the first 48 hours and the initial assessment underestimates in a meaningful proportion, so a single early assessment should not drive definitive decisions; *what:* depth conversion, refined TBSA). Weight (*why:* fluid resuscitation is weight-based and an estimate propagates into every subsequent calculation; *what:* weight in kilograms). Carboxyhaemoglobin by co-oximetry in enclosed-space fires (*why:* standard oximetry reads falsely normal and CO poisoning coexists; *what:* elevated COHb — cross-refer [[A5]] 0.4). VBG with lactate (*why:* a markedly raised lactate out of proportion to the burn suggests concurrent cyanide poisoning from combustion of synthetic materials; *what:* acidosis, high lactate). FBC, UEC, coagulation, group and hold (*why:* baseline before large-volume resuscitation and possible surgery; *what:* baseline values). CK and urinalysis in electrical or deep burns (*why:* rhabdomyolysis; *what:* raised CK, dipstick-positive blood without RBCs). Bronchoscopy where inhalation injury is suspected (*why:* directly assesses airway involvement and grades it; *what:* soot, mucosal oedema, ulceration). Photographic documentation (*why:* enables serial comparison, remote burns unit consultation, and — where non-accidental injury is a concern — a contemporaneous record; *what:* pattern, distribution, demarcation).

## 0.2 Burns — Resuscitation and Management

**A/P:** Beyond a threshold TBSA, burn injury produces a **systemic inflammatory response with generalised capillary leak** — not just local oedema. Plasma shifts into the interstitium throughout the body, producing hypovolaemic shock despite total body fluid excess, alongside myocardial depression and increased vascular resistance. This is the "burn shock" that fluid resuscitation formulae address, and it is why a large burn is a systemic illness rather than a skin injury.

> [!danger] The fluid formula is omitted
> `UNVERIFIED — the Parkland (or modified Parkland) formula, the crystalloid volume in mL/kg per %TBSA, the proportion given in the first eight hours, the timing reference point (from time of INJURY, not from time of presentation — this is the part most often got wrong), the urine output targets for adults and children, and the paediatric maintenance additions are ALL omitted from this file. Obtain them from ANZBA and your local burns protocol. This is precisely the category of figure this corpus has previously carried incorrectly.`
> Two principles that are safe to state: the formula gives a **starting point, not a prescription** — fluid is titrated to urine output and clinical response, and both over- and under-resuscitation cause harm. And the clock runs **from the time of the burn**, so a patient presenting four hours late has already lost part of the first period and the calculation must account for it.

> [!danger] Circumferential burns and escharotomy
> A circumferential full-thickness burn forms an inelastic constricting eschar. As oedema develops beneath it: around a **limb** → progressive vascular compromise and compartment syndrome; around the **chest** → restriction of chest wall expansion and ventilatory failure that no amount of oxygen will fix. **Escharotomy is the treatment and it is time-critical.** Monitor distal perfusion and ventilatory pressures actively in any circumferential burn.

> [!warning] Analgesia is systematically inadequate in burns
> Partial thickness burns are extremely painful, dressing changes more so. Use multimodal analgesia with opioid titration, and anticipate procedural analgesia needs. Under-treatment of burn pain is common and avoidable — cross-refer [[F0.4]] 0.9.

> [!danger] Non-accidental injury
> Patterns raising concern in a child: **sharply demarcated "glove and stocking" or "doughnut" scald distributions** suggesting forced immersion · symmetrical burns · burns with no splash marks · **cigarette burns** — discrete, round, uniform · burns to the buttocks or perineum · **delayed presentation** · a history inconsistent with the injury or with the child's developmental stage · a changing history. Any of these mandates escalation and a child protection discussion. Cross-refer [[P3]] Safeguarding.

**Ix:** As per 0.1, with serial UEC, urine output monitoring and weight (*why:* resuscitation is titrated to urine output and daily weight tracks fluid accumulation; *what:* hourly urine output, weight trend). Serial FBC and albumin (*why:* haemoconcentration then dilution, and protein loss from the burn surface; *what:* haematocrit, falling albumin). Wound swabs where infection is suspected — not routinely (*why:* burns are colonised, so routine swabs generate misleading results and drive unnecessary antibiotics; *what:* organism where clinical infection is present).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Stop the burning process. **Twenty minutes of cool running water.** A–E, with early consideration of intubation for inhalation injury — **intubate early rather than late**, since airway oedema progresses over hours. High-flow oxygen. Large-bore IV access, preferably through unburnt skin. Weight-based fluid resuscitation titrated to urine output. Analgesia. Cover with cling film applied in strips, not circumferentially. **Keep the patient warm.** Tetanus status. Contact the burns service early — most Australian burns units will accept a photograph and a phone call.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Burns unit transfer per referral criteria. Escharotomy for circumferential burns. Early excision and grafting of deep burns. Nutritional support, since the hypermetabolic response to a major burn is profound and prolonged. Infection surveillance.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Scar management with pressure garments and silicone, physiotherapy and occupational therapy to prevent contracture, itch management, psychological support, and long-term follow-up. **Sun protection of healed burns**, which pigment abnormally and burn easily.

## 0.3 Chemical Burns

**D:** Tissue injury from contact with a corrosive or reactive substance.

**A/P:** The mechanism differs by class, and the difference is clinically important.
**Acids** cause **coagulative necrosis** → the coagulated protein forms an eschar that limits further penetration → injury tends to be more superficial and self-limiting.
**Alkalis** cause **liquefactive necrosis** → saponification of fat and dissolution of protein → **no barrier forms and penetration continues** → alkali burns are typically deeper, progress for longer, and are more damaging than acid burns of equivalent concentration. This is counterintuitive to most people's expectations and is a reliable exam discriminator.

> [!danger] Hydrofluoric acid is a systemic emergency, not a skin injury
> HF penetrates tissue readily and **the fluoride ion chelates calcium and magnesium**, causing profound systemic hypocalcaemia and hypomagnesaemia with hyperkalaemia and fatal arrhythmia. **Death has followed exposure of a relatively small body surface area.** Pain is characteristically severe and out of proportion to the visible injury, and may be **delayed by hours** with dilute solutions — so an initially unimpressive presentation is not reassuring.
> Treatment is **calcium** — topical calcium gluconate gel, and intra-arterial or intravenous calcium for significant exposures, alongside cardiac monitoring and serial calcium, magnesium and potassium. Found in rust removers, glass etching products, and industrial cleaning agents. `UNVERIFIED — calcium gluconate gel concentration, intra-arterial technique and systemic dosing require verification with the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26.`

> [!warning] Irrigation rules
> **Copious and prolonged irrigation with water is the mainstay**, and for alkali burns it needs to continue far longer than instinct suggests.
> **Brush off dry powder before irrigating** — some agents, notably elemental sodium, potassium and dry lime, **react exothermically with water** and will worsen the injury.
> **Do not attempt chemical neutralisation** — the neutralisation reaction is itself exothermic and causes additional thermal injury.
> Remove contaminated clothing and protect yourself with PPE — secondary contamination of staff is a real event.

> [!tip] Cement burns
> Wet cement is strongly alkaline. Injury is typically to the knees and lower legs from kneeling in it, develops slowly over hours while the person continues working, and **presents late with a deep alkali burn.** A classic occupational presentation and easily missed because the patient does not connect the exposure with the injury.

**S/Smx:** Pain, erythema, ulceration, eschar, with the severity often evolving over hours. Systemic features in HF and in absorbed agents.

**Ix:** Identification of the agent, ideally from the **Safety Data Sheet**, which is legally required to be available in Australian workplaces (*why:* management is entirely agent-dependent and the SDS gives composition and first aid guidance; *what:* substance, concentration, recommended decontamination). pH testing of the affected area where feasible (*why:* guides how long to continue irrigation, since the endpoint is normalisation rather than a fixed time; *what:* surface pH). **Serial calcium, magnesium and potassium with continuous ECG monitoring in HF exposure** (*why:* the systemic electrolyte derangement is what kills, and it develops after the skin injury is apparent; *what:* falling calcium and magnesium, rising potassium, QT prolongation and arrhythmia). UEC and VBG (*why:* systemic absorption and metabolic derangement; *what:* acidosis, electrolyte disturbance). Serial wound assessment (*why:* chemical burns progress and the depth at presentation underestimates the final injury; *what:* depth conversion).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
PPE. Remove clothing. Brush off dry agent. **Copious prolonged irrigation.** Identify the agent and call **13 11 26**. Specific antidote where one exists — calcium for HF. Cardiac monitoring for HF and systemically absorbed agents.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Burns unit referral — **all chemical burns meet referral criteria.** Surgical debridement. Correction of electrolyte derangement.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Occupational assessment and **SafeWork SA notification** where work-related. Review of workplace handling procedures and PPE. Cross-refer [[P1]] Occupational Exposure.

## 0.4 Chemical Eye Injury

**D:** Chemical contact with the ocular surface — one of the few true ophthalmic emergencies where treatment precedes assessment.

**A/P:** As in 0.3, alkalis penetrate rapidly through the cornea into the anterior chamber via liquefactive necrosis, damaging the corneal endothelium, iris, lens and trabecular meshwork within minutes, and destroying the **limbal stem cells** that regenerate corneal epithelium. Loss of limbal stem cells leads to permanent corneal opacification and vision loss. Acids coagulate corneal protein, which limits penetration and generally produces a less severe injury.

> [!danger] Irrigate first — before visual acuity, before history, before anything
> **This is the only ocular presentation where treatment precedes assessment.** Every minute of continued chemical contact causes further irreversible injury. Start irrigation at triage, in the waiting room, with whatever clean fluid is available if sterile saline is not immediately to hand. Documenting visual acuity first is the wrong instinct here.
> Use topical anaesthetic to permit adequate irrigation — blepharospasm otherwise makes it impossible. A **Morgan lens** or IV giving set directed across the eye, with copious volume.
> **Evert both eyelids and sweep the fornices** for retained particulate matter, particularly with lime, cement or plaster — retained particles continue to release alkali and irrigation alone will not clear them.
> **Irrigate until the pH normalises**, then recheck after a delay, since pH can rebound as chemical leaches from the tissue. `UNVERIFIED — the target pH range and the recheck interval require verification.`

> [!warning] Limbal blanching is the prognostic sign
> **A white, ischaemic limbus indicates destruction of the limbal vessels and stem cells and predicts poor outcome.** Paradoxically, a severely injured eye may look **deceptively white and quiet** because the conjunctival vessels have been destroyed — whereas a mildly injured eye is angrily red. **A white eye after an alkali injury is worse, not better.** This inversion of the usual rule is the single most important observation in the section.

**S/Smx:** Severe pain, blepharospasm, lacrimation, blurred vision, conjunctival injection or blanching, corneal haze or opacity, and epithelial defect.

**Ix:** **pH testing of the conjunctival fornix with litmus paper** (*why:* determines whether irrigation has been adequate and when to stop, and it is the only objective endpoint available; *what:* pH before, during and after irrigation, with a repeat after a delay). Visual acuity — **after irrigation** (*why:* baseline for progression and medicolegal documentation, but it must not delay treatment; *what:* acuity in each eye). Fluorescein staining with slit lamp or blue light (*why:* demonstrates the epithelial defect and its extent, which grades severity and tracks healing; *what:* area of uptake). Assessment of limbal perfusion (*why:* limbal ischaemia is the principal prognostic determinant; *what:* extent of limbal blanching in clock hours). Intraocular pressure (*why:* alkali injury damages the trabecular meshwork and causes acute and chronic glaucoma; *what:* raised pressure). Lid eversion and fornix inspection (*why:* retained particulate perpetuates the injury and is missed unless actively looked for; *what:* retained solid material). Agent identification via Safety Data Sheet (*why:* alkali versus acid changes prognosis and follow-up intensity; *what:* substance and concentration).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
**Irrigate immediately and copiously**, with topical anaesthetic, lid eversion and fornix sweeping. Continue until pH normalises and recheck. **Urgent ophthalmology referral for all but the most trivial exposures.**

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Ophthalmology management — topical steroids, cycloplegics, ascorbate and citrate in some protocols, pressure control, and surgical intervention including limbal stem cell transplantation for severe injury.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Long-term follow-up for glaucoma, corneal scarring, symblepharon and dry eye. Eye protection education and occupational review. Cross-refer [[E1]] Red & Painful Eye.

## 0.5 Minor Traumatic Wound

**D:** A wound not requiring operative management, where the clinical tasks are excluding occult injury, deciding on closure, and preventing infection.

> [!danger] Examine the wound through the full range of motion the limb was in when injured
> A tendon injured with the finger flexed retracts, and the divided end **will not be visible when you examine the hand extended.** A partially divided tendon may look intact at rest and rupture later. **Explore in the position of injury**, and test each tendon individually against resistance. Missed tendon and nerve injuries in hand wounds are among the commonest and most consequential emergency department errors.

> [!warning] Wounds that should not be closed primarily
> **Bites** — particularly cat bites and any bite to the hand · **puncture wounds**, especially through footwear · heavily contaminated or devitalised wounds · wounds presenting beyond a time threshold · wounds with retained foreign material.
> These are irrigated, debrided and left for **delayed primary closure or healing by secondary intention.** Closing an infected or contaminated wound converts a manageable problem into an abscess.

> [!danger] The "fight bite"
> A small wound over the **metacarpophalangeal joint** sustained by punching someone in the mouth. The patient rarely volunteers the mechanism. **The tooth penetrates the extensor tendon and joint capsule with the fist clenched, and the injury tract seals as the hand opens** — so a trivial-looking 5 mm laceration overlies a contaminated joint. These require exploration, washout, antibiotics and hand surgical involvement. Assume any wound over the MCP joints is a fight bite until proven otherwise.

> [!tip] Foreign bodies
> **Glass is radio-opaque** — plain radiography detects most glass fragments, and any wound caused by glass warrants an X-ray. **Wood, plastic and thorns are radiolucent** — ultrasound is the better modality. A retained foreign body is a common cause of a wound that fails to heal or repeatedly becomes infected, and a common source of medicolegal complaint.

> [!warning] Tetanus
> Assess immunisation status and wound type — tetanus-prone wounds include those contaminated with soil, saliva or faeces, puncture wounds, and wounds with devitalised tissue. Both vaccine and immunoglobulin may be indicated depending on history and wound type. `UNVERIFIED — the vaccination schedule, booster intervals and immunoglobulin indications require verification against the Australian Immunisation Handbook.`

**A/P:** Wound healing proceeds through haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Contamination, devitalised tissue, foreign material and impaired perfusion all prolong the inflammatory phase and predispose to infection — which is the logic behind irrigation, debridement and selective delayed closure.

**S/Smx:** Assess: mechanism, time since injury, contamination, and the patient's tetanus status, diabetes, immunosuppression, anticoagulation and vascular disease. Examine: neurovascular status distally, individual tendon function, joint involvement, and depth.

**Ix:** Structured neurovascular and tendon examination distal to the wound, documented (*why:* deficits found later are attributed to the treating clinician if not documented at presentation, and more importantly a missed tendon or nerve injury has a far worse outcome when repaired late; *what:* sensation in each nerve distribution, individual tendon function against resistance, capillary refill, pulses). Plain radiography where glass, metal or a fracture is possible (*why:* glass is radio-opaque and retained fragments are a common and preventable cause of poor healing; *what:* radio-opaque foreign body, fracture, air in a joint). Ultrasound for suspected radiolucent foreign body (*why:* wood, thorns and plastic are invisible on plain films; *what:* echogenic foreign body with shadowing). Wound exploration in a bloodless field with adequate anaesthesia and lighting (*why:* the definitive assessment — inadequate exploration is the mechanism by which injuries are missed, and "the wound was too painful to explore" is not an acceptable reason; *what:* depth, tendon, joint capsule, foreign material). Wound swab only where infection is established (*why:* swabbing a fresh wound cultures colonisers and misleads; *what:* organism in established infection).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Haemostasis, adequate analgesia and anaesthesia, **copious irrigation**, debridement of devitalised tissue, exploration, and closure or planned delayed closure. Tetanus prophylaxis. Antibiotic prophylaxis for bites, contaminated wounds, and wounds involving bone, joint or tendon. `UNVERIFIED — antibiotic indications and regimens from eTG Antibiotic.`

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Hand surgery or plastics referral for tendon, nerve, joint or complex wounds. Delayed primary closure where indicated.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Wound review at a defined interval, suture or staple removal at a site-appropriate time, scar advice, and **explicit written return advice for infection.** Cross-refer [[Examination]] Wound Management — Basic Principles.

## 0.6 Crush Injury and Rhabdomyolysis

**D:** Injury from prolonged compression of muscle, and the systemic syndrome that follows its release.

**A/P:** Sustained compression → direct muscle injury and ischaemia → myocyte membrane failure with influx of sodium, water and calcium and efflux of potassium, phosphate, myoglobin and creatine kinase. **While the limb remains compressed, these products are sequestered.** On release, reperfusion flushes them into the circulation → **crush syndrome**: sudden hyperkalaemia with arrhythmia, metabolic acidosis, hypocalcaemia, hypovolaemia as fluid sequesters into the reperfused limb, and **myoglobin-induced acute kidney injury** through tubular obstruction, direct toxicity and renal vasoconstriction.

> [!danger] The deterioration happens on release, not during compression
> A patient can be alert and talking while trapped, and arrest within minutes of extrication. **Where possible, establish IV access and begin aggressive fluid resuscitation BEFORE the crushing force is released** — this is the central principle of crush injury management and it inverts the usual instinct to extricate first. `UNVERIFIED — pre-release fluid regimens and the management of anticipated hyperkalaemia at extrication require verification against a current disaster medicine or retrieval source.`

> [!danger] Compartment syndrome — the classic "5 Ps" are a trap
> Pallor, pulselessness, paralysis and paraesthesia are **late findings**, and by the time they appear the limb is often already lost. **Pulses are typically present in compartment syndrome**, because compartment pressure rises above capillary and venous pressure long before it reaches arterial pressure — waiting for a pulseless limb is waiting too long.
> The **earliest and most reliable signs are pain out of proportion to the injury, and pain on passive stretch of the muscles within the compartment.** A patient requiring escalating opioid doses after a fracture or crush is describing compartment syndrome until proven otherwise.
> **Fasciotomy is time-critical.** Where clinical suspicion is high, it should not wait for pressure measurement, and it certainly should not wait for pulses to disappear. Compartment pressures are measured when the patient cannot report symptoms — obtunded, intubated, or under regional block. `UNVERIFIED — compartment pressure and delta pressure thresholds require verification.`

> [!warning] Regional anaesthesia and compartment syndrome
> A dense nerve block abolishes the pain that is the diagnostic sign. This is a real consideration in limb trauma and is why the decision to block a limb at risk of compartment syndrome should involve a senior clinician — cross-refer [[F0.4]] 0.11.

**S/Smx:** Swollen tense painful limb, pain on passive stretch, sensory disturbance, weakness, dark tea-coloured urine, and systemic features of hyperkalaemia and hypovolaemia.

**Ix:** **CK** (*why:* the diagnostic marker of rhabdomyolysis and its trajectory predicts the risk of renal failure — a single value at presentation may underestimate, so serial measurement is needed; *what:* markedly elevated CK, rising or falling). **UEC with potassium, calcium and phosphate** (*why:* hyperkalaemia is the immediate life threat and hypocalcaemia accompanies it; *what:* rising potassium, rising creatinine, low calcium, high phosphate). **ECG with continuous monitoring** (*why:* hyperkalaemic changes precede arrest and the ECG is faster than the laboratory; *what:* peaked T waves, widened QRS, loss of P waves, sine wave). Urinalysis (*why:* myoglobinuria is dipstick-positive for blood with **no red cells on microscopy** — a cheap and characteristic finding; *what:* positive dipstick, absent RBCs). VBG (*why:* metabolic acidosis and rapid potassium; *what:* acidosis, potassium). Coagulation profile (*why:* DIC complicates severe crush injury; *what:* deranged INR, low fibrinogen). Hourly urine output (*why:* the resuscitation is titrated to urine output to maintain flow through the tubules; *what:* output per hour). Compartment pressure measurement where the patient cannot report symptoms (*why:* the clinical signs are unavailable in an obtunded or blocked patient; *what:* compartment and delta pressure). Radiography of the affected region (*why:* associated fractures; *what:* fracture).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
**Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation, ideally started before release.** Continuous cardiac monitoring. **Treat hyperkalaemia urgently** — calcium for membrane stabilisation, insulin-dextrose and salbutamol for shift, and definitive removal by dialysis where severe. Maintain a high urine output. Analgesia. Serial compartment assessment. `UNVERIFIED — all fluid rates, urine output targets, and hyperkalaemia treatment doses are omitted; obtain from local protocol and eTG.`

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
**Fasciotomy for compartment syndrome.** Renal replacement therapy for refractory hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis or established acute kidney injury. Debridement of necrotic muscle. **Avoid nephrotoxins** — NSAIDs, contrast, aminoglycosides — in a patient with established or threatened myoglobinuric renal injury. The role of urinary alkalinisation and mannitol is debated and is a specialist decision.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Renal follow-up, since most myoglobinuric acute kidney injury recovers but some does not. Rehabilitation for the injured limb, management of fasciotomy wounds and their scarring, and psychological support after entrapment, which is frequently traumatic. **Occupational notification where work-related.**
