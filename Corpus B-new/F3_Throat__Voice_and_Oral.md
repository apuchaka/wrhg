## 0.1 Sore Throat

> [!danger] The Australian exception to "most sore throats don't need antibiotics"
> **In the general population, most sore throats are VIRAL, antibiotics shorten symptoms by very little, and the main justification for treating group A streptococcal pharyngitis in low-risk settings is symptomatic.**
> **Clinical scores (Centor, McIsaac) — fever, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes, absence of cough, adjusted for age — estimate the probability of streptococcal infection but DO NOT reliably distinguish it, and they are decision aids rather than rules.** `UNVERIFIED — the criteria and their interpretation.`
> **BUT THE THRESHOLD IS COMPLETELY DIFFERENT IN POPULATIONS AT HIGH RISK OF ACUTE RHEUMATIC FEVER.**
> **For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in high-risk settings, and for Māori and Pacific Islander peoples, sore throat is treated with antibiotics on a MUCH LOWER threshold — because the purpose is PREVENTING ACUTE RHEUMATIC FEVER AND RHEUMATIC HEART DISEASE, not shortening a sore throat.**
> **Australia has among the highest rates of ARF and RHD in the world in these populations, and primary prevention through treating streptococcal infection is one of the few points at which the chain can be broken.**
> **Know which guideline applies to the patient in front of you.** Cross-refer [[AU1]] 0.4, [[F0.5]] 0.10 and [[K2]] 0.6. `UNVERIFIED — the RHDAustralia criteria and regimens.`

> [!warning] Infectious mononucleosis — three things beyond the diagnosis
> **Adolescents and young adults: marked tonsillar enlargement with exudate, PALATAL PETECHIAE, GENERALISED lymphadenopathy (including posterior cervical, unlike streptococcal pharyngitis), splenomegaly, and profound fatigue.**
> **Atypical lymphocytes on the film; heterophile antibody testing and EBV serology.**
> **1. THE AMOXICILLIN RASH — a widespread morbilliform eruption after amoxicillin in EBV infection. It is NOT an allergy, and it should not generate a lifelong penicillin allergy label** — but it routinely does. Cross-refer [[K4]] 0.3 and [[G1]] 0.4.
> **2. SPLENIC RUPTURE — avoid contact sport and heavy lifting for a defined period, because the enlarged spleen can rupture with minor trauma or even spontaneously.** **This advice must be given explicitly.** `UNVERIFIED — the duration.`
> **3. Airway obstruction from massive tonsillar hypertrophy occasionally requires corticosteroids and admission.**
> **Prolonged post-viral fatigue is common and should be anticipated and explained rather than investigated repeatedly.**

> [!danger] Two sore throats that are not sore throats
> **1. HIV SEROCONVERSION — fever, PHARYNGITIS, rash, generalised lymphadenopathy and MOUTH ULCERS, two to six weeks after exposure.** **It is diagnosed as glandular fever or a viral illness, and the patient leaves at the point of PEAK INFECTIVITY.** **TEST — with a combined antigen/antibody assay, because antibody alone may be negative in the window period.** Cross-refer [[G1]] 0.5 and [[K3]] 0.5.
> **2. AGRANULOCYTOSIS — a sore throat with FEVER in a patient taking CARBIMAZOLE, PROPYLTHIOURACIL, CLOZAPINE, sulfasalazine, methotrexate or chemotherapy IS AGRANULOCYTOSIS UNTIL A FULL BLOOD COUNT SAYS OTHERWISE.**
> **This is the specific instruction these patients are given at the start of treatment, and it is the clinician's job to act on it: URGENT FBC, withhold the drug, and treat as neutropenic sepsis if febrile.** **Do not send them home with penicillin.** Cross-refer [[I1]] 0.3, [[N3]] 0.6 and [[K1]] 0.5.

> [!warning] The complications of streptococcal and pharyngeal infection
> **· PERITONSILLAR ABSCESS (quinsy) — see 0.2.**
> **· LEMIERRE SYNDROME — septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, usually caused by *Fusobacterium necrophorum*.**
> **The picture: a young, previously healthy person with a sore throat that is NOT SETTLING or has worsened after several days, developing UNILATERAL NECK PAIN AND SWELLING, rigors and high fever, and then SEPTIC PULMONARY EMBOLI with pleuritic chest pain and hypoxia.**
> **It is uncommon, it is lethal, and it is diagnosed late because the sore throat is assumed to be viral.** **A persistently unwell young person with a sore throat and unilateral neck signs needs blood cultures and contrast imaging of the neck and chest.**
> **· ACUTE RHEUMATIC FEVER and POST-STREPTOCOCCAL GLOMERULONEPHRITIS.** Cross-refer [[H1]] 0.3.
> **· SCARLET FEVER — a sandpaper rash with circumoral pallor and a strawberry tongue.** Cross-refer [[M2]] 0.5.

## 0.2 The Airway-Threatening Throat

> [!danger] The red flags — and the examination NOT to perform
> **RED FLAGS: DROOLING or inability to swallow saliva · STRIDOR · sitting FORWARD in a TRIPOD position · a MUFFLED, "HOT POTATO" VOICE · TRISMUS · severe PAIN OUT OF PROPORTION to visible findings · unilateral neck swelling · systemic toxicity · rapid progression · and surgical emphysema in the neck.**
> **IF THESE ARE PRESENT: DO NOT EXAMINE THE THROAT WITH A SPATULA, DO NOT LIE THE PATIENT FLAT, DO NOT ATTEMPT CANNULATION OR BLOOD TESTS FIRST, AND DO NOT SEND THEM TO RADIOLOGY.**
> **Keep them upright, calm and with a parent if a child. Give oxygen if tolerated. GET SENIOR ANAESTHETIC AND ENT HELP TO THE PATIENT.**
> **Distressing a patient with critical airway narrowing can precipitate complete obstruction.** Cross-refer [[A2]] 0.3.

> [!warning] Adult epiglottitis — the throat can look normal
> **Since Hib vaccination, epiglottitis (supraglottitis) is now seen MORE OFTEN IN ADULTS than in children.**
> **THE KEY FEATURE: SEVERE ODYNOPHAGIA AND PAIN THAT IS COMPLETELY OUT OF PROPORTION TO THE APPEARANCE OF THE OROPHARYNX — which may look entirely normal, because the pathology is below the level you can see.**
> **With a muffled voice, drooling, and a tender larynx on external palpation.**
> **A patient who says they cannot swallow their own saliva, with a normal-looking throat, has a supraglottic problem until proven otherwise** — and the diagnosis is made by flexible nasendoscopy performed by someone able to secure the airway.

> [!tip] The deep space infections
> **· PERITONSILLAR ABSCESS (QUINSY) — unilateral severe throat pain, TRISMUS, a muffled voice, drooling, and UVULAR DEVIATION AWAY from the swollen side with medial displacement of the tonsil.** **Treated with drainage (aspiration or incision) plus antibiotics — antibiotics alone are usually insufficient.**
> **· PARAPHARYNGEAL and RETROPHARYNGEAL ABSCESS — more common in children. Neck stiffness or REFUSAL TO MOVE THE NECK, fever, drooling, and a change in voice.** **It is mistaken for meningitis because of the neck stiffness.** Requires contrast CT and surgical drainage.
> **· LUDWIG ANGINA — a rapidly spreading BILATERAL infection of the submandibular and sublingual spaces, usually of DENTAL origin (a lower molar).**
> **THE DANGER IS THE TONGUE: the floor of mouth becomes woody and indurated, the TONGUE IS PUSHED UPWARDS AND BACKWARDS, and the airway obstructs.**
> **Features: bilateral submandibular swelling, a raised tongue, drooling, dysphagia, trismus and systemic illness.**
> **It is a SURGICAL AIRWAY EMERGENCY requiring immediate senior airway involvement, intravenous antibiotics and surgical drainage** — and the airway must be secured early, because it becomes rapidly impossible.

> [!danger] Angioedema — and the two types that do not respond to adrenaline
> **· ACE INHIBITOR-INDUCED ANGIOEDEMA — bradykinin-mediated, affecting the lips, tongue, floor of mouth and larynx.**
> **THE TRAP: IT CAN OCCUR AT ANY TIME, INCLUDING YEARS AFTER STARTING THE DRUG** — so a long-standing prescription does not exclude it, and the connection is frequently not made.
> **It does NOT respond well to adrenaline, antihistamines or corticosteroids, because it is not histamine-mediated. STOP THE DRUG PERMANENTLY, secure the airway if needed, and never re-challenge (including with an ARB in most cases).**
> **· HEREDITARY ANGIOEDEMA (C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency) — recurrent episodes of angioedema WITHOUT urticaria, often with abdominal pain from bowel wall oedema, frequently with a family history, and precipitated by trauma, dental work and stress.**
> **It also does NOT respond to adrenaline, antihistamines or steroids, and requires SPECIFIC therapy (C1 inhibitor concentrate, icatibant).**
> **Recognising that a patient with angioedema and NO urticaria is unlikely to be having an allergic reaction is the key step.** Cross-refer [[K4]] 0.1.

## 0.3 Hoarseness and the Voice

> [!danger] Hoarseness persisting beyond three weeks requires laryngoscopy
> **This is the single rule to carry from this section.**
> **The larynx cannot be examined without a scope, so persistent hoarseness cannot be assessed adequately in general practice or on a ward — it requires ENT referral for laryngoscopy.**
> **The threshold is lower still in a SMOKER, a heavy drinker, or a patient over 50, and in anyone with associated dysphagia, otalgia, weight loss, haemoptysis, stridor or a neck lump.**
> **LARYNGEAL CANCER presents with hoarseness EARLY (because a small glottic tumour changes the voice) — which makes it one of the head and neck cancers most curable when acted on promptly, and one most often delayed by months of reassurance.**

> [!warning] Left vocal cord palsy means look at the chest
> **The RIGHT recurrent laryngeal nerve loops around the subclavian artery; the LEFT loops around the AORTIC ARCH and has a long intrathoracic course.**
> **THEREFORE A LEFT VOCAL CORD PALSY POINTS TO PATHOLOGY IN THE CHEST, and the causes include:**
> **· LUNG CANCER, particularly a left apical or mediastinal tumour.**
> **· Mediastinal lymphadenopathy.**
> **· AORTIC ARCH ANEURYSM.**
> **· Left atrial enlargement (historically in mitral stenosis).**
> **· Oesophageal and thyroid malignancy.**
> **· Surgery — thyroid, parathyroid, cardiac, oesophageal and carotid.**
> **A NEW UNEXPLAINED VOCAL CORD PALSY REQUIRES IMAGING FROM THE SKULL BASE TO THE MEDIASTINUM**, because the entire course of the nerve must be assessed. **Investigating only the neck misses the lung cancer.**

> [!tip] The other causes of hoarseness
> **· ACUTE LARYNGITIS — viral or from voice overuse. Self-limiting; VOICE REST (not whispering, which strains the cords more than quiet speech), hydration, and avoiding smoke.**
> **· LARYNGOPHARYNGEAL REFLUX — throat clearing, globus, cough and hoarseness, often WITHOUT heartburn. Managed with lifestyle measures and acid suppression, though the evidence is mixed and it is over-diagnosed.**
> **· VOCAL CORD NODULES and POLYPS — from voice use and abuse; common in teachers, singers, call centre workers and children who shout. Speech pathology is the primary treatment.**
> **· REINKE OEDEMA — diffuse polypoid cord swelling, strongly associated with SMOKING, producing a characteristically deep, gravelly voice, classically in a middle-aged woman smoker.**
> **· INHALED CORTICOSTEROIDS — causing candidiasis and a steroid myopathy of the laryngeal muscles.** **CHECK INHALER TECHNIQUE, use a SPACER, and RINSE AND SPIT after use.** Cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.1.
> **· HYPOTHYROIDISM — a deep, slow, hoarse voice from mucopolysaccharide deposition. Check the TFTs.**
> **· NEUROLOGICAL — Parkinson disease (soft, monotonous hypophonia), motor neurone disease (with bulbar features and a poor cough — a red flag), myasthenia gravis (fatigable voice, worse through the day), and stroke.** Cross-refer [[D4]] 0.3 and [[D6]] 0.5.
> **· FUNCTIONAL (muscle tension) dysphonia — often with a normal cough and normal laughter, which is a useful clue, and treated by speech pathology.**

## 0.4 Oropharyngeal Dysphagia

> [!tip] Separate oropharyngeal from oesophageal — the history does it
> **· OROPHARYNGEAL — difficulty INITIATING the swallow. COUGHING OR CHOKING at the moment of swallowing · NASAL REGURGITATION · drooling · a wet or gurgly voice after swallowing · repeated swallows for one bolus · and recurrent chest infections from ASPIRATION.**
> **· OESOPHAGEAL — the swallow starts normally and then food STICKS, seconds later, retrosternally.** Cross-refer [[C6]] 0.1.
> **Asking "does it go wrong the moment you swallow, or does it stick afterwards?" separates them, and directs the patient to speech pathology and ENT versus to endoscopy.**

> [!warning] The causes, and the pouch
> **· NEUROLOGICAL — STROKE (the commonest cause overall, and dysphagia after stroke is both common and a major determinant of pneumonia and mortality) · motor neurone disease · Parkinson disease · multiple sclerosis · myasthenia gravis · and dementia.**
> **· STRUCTURAL — tumours of the tongue base, tonsil, pharynx and larynx · post-radiotherapy fibrosis · cervical osteophytes · and PHARYNGEAL POUCH.**
> **PHARYNGEAL (ZENKER) POUCH — a posterior pharyngeal mucosal herniation in an older patient. Features: dysphagia · REGURGITATION OF UNDIGESTED FOOD hours after eating, sometimes at night onto the pillow · HALITOSIS · a gurgling sound in the neck · and aspiration.**
> **THE PRACTICAL WARNING: DO NOT PASS A NASOGASTRIC TUBE BLINDLY in a patient with a suspected pouch — the tube enters the pouch and can PERFORATE it.** **Diagnosis is by contrast swallow, not by endoscopy first.**
> **· Xerostomia, poor dentition and ill-fitting dentures — mundane, common, and easily correctable causes that are routinely overlooked.**

> [!danger] Aspiration, swallow assessment, and the decisions that follow
> **A patient with suspected aspiration needs a SPEECH PATHOLOGY swallow assessment before oral intake, and instrumental assessment (videofluoroscopy or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation) where the bedside assessment is inconclusive.**
> **SILENT ASPIRATION — aspiration WITHOUT coughing — occurs in a substantial proportion, particularly after stroke and in the elderly, and it means that the absence of coughing does NOT mean the swallow is safe.**
> **Modified diets and thickened fluids are used, but the evidence for thickened fluids is weaker than their ubiquity suggests, and they reduce fluid intake and are disliked.**
> **AND THE HARDER DECISION: in ADVANCED DEMENTIA, tube feeding does NOT prevent aspiration pneumonia, does not prolong life, and does not improve comfort — while adding restraint, discomfort and complications.** **CAREFUL HAND FEEDING is the recommended alternative, and this conversation should be had before a crisis rather than during one.** Cross-refer [[GER2]] 0.3, 0.5 and [[D2]] 0.3.

> [!tip] Globus
> **A persistent sensation of a lump in the throat, WITHOUT true dysphagia — and characteristically BETTER when actually eating or drinking, and worse when swallowing saliva or when anxious.**
> **It is common, benign and frequently associated with reflux or anxiety.**
> **But it is a diagnosis of exclusion: TRUE dysphagia, pain, weight loss, a neck lump, hoarseness, or a unilateral sensation still require investigation** — and the sensation of a lump is exactly how some tongue base and hypopharyngeal tumours present.
> **Explaining the mechanism relieves considerable anxiety, and the anxiety itself perpetuates the symptom.**

## 0.5 Oral Lesions and Oral Cancer

> [!danger] Any oral ulcer or lesion persisting beyond three weeks needs biopsy
> **This is the rule that matters, and it parallels the three-week rule for hoarseness.**
> **ORAL CANCER is common, is frequently diagnosed late, and has a poor prognosis when advanced — while an early lesion is highly curable.**
> **RISK FACTORS: SMOKING and all forms of tobacco · ALCOHOL — and smoking and alcohol are SYNERGISTIC, multiplying rather than adding risk · BETEL QUID and ARECA NUT chewing, which is important in South Asian, Southeast Asian and Pacific communities and should be asked about specifically · HPV (associated with OROPHARYNGEAL cancer, occurring in younger, often non-smoking patients, and carrying a better prognosis) · SUN exposure for lip cancer · and immunosuppression.**
> **THE HIGH-RISK SITES ARE THE ONES NOT EASILY SEEN: the LATERAL BORDER and VENTRAL SURFACE OF THE TONGUE, and the FLOOR OF THE MOUTH.**
> **SO EXAMINE PROPERLY: good light, remove dentures, use a gloved hand and a gauze swab to hold and lift the tongue, inspect and PALPATE the floor of mouth and the lateral tongue bilaterally, and feel the neck.** **A cursory look with a torch misses exactly the lesions that matter.**

> [!warning] The premalignant lesions
> **· LEUKOPLAKIA — a white patch that CANNOT BE RUBBED OFF (which distinguishes it from candida, which can). It has malignant potential and requires biopsy.**
> **· ERYTHROPLAKIA — a red velvety patch. It is LESS COMMON but has a MUCH HIGHER malignant potential than leukoplakia, and a high proportion already show dysplasia or carcinoma on biopsy.** **A red patch is more worrying than a white one — which is counterintuitive and is a favourite examination point.**
> **· Speckled leukoplakia (mixed red and white) — also high risk.**
> **· ORAL LICHEN PLANUS, particularly the EROSIVE form, carries a small but real risk of squamous cell carcinoma and warrants long-term surveillance.** Cross-refer [[G4]] 0.3.
> **· ORAL SUBMUCOUS FIBROSIS — from betel quid, with trismus and a pale fibrotic mucosa; premalignant.**

> [!tip] Ulcers, candida, and the systemic associations
> **· APHTHOUS ULCERS — recurrent, painful, shallow, with a yellow-grey base and an erythematous halo, healing without scarring. Usually idiopathic and benign.**
> **BUT SEVERE, UNUSUALLY FREQUENT OR LARGE APHTHOUS ULCERATION SHOULD PROMPT: haematinic deficiency (IRON, B12, FOLATE) · COELIAC DISEASE · INFLAMMATORY BOWEL DISEASE · BEHÇET DISEASE (with genital ulcers and uveitis) · HIV · and neutropenia.** **Check a full blood count, ferritin, B12, folate and coeliac serology.** Cross-refer [[C5]] 0.4 and [[L2]] 0.5.
> **· ORAL CANDIDIASIS — pseudomembranous (white plaques that WIPE OFF leaving an erythematous base), erythematous, or angular cheilitis.**
> **ORAL CANDIDA IN AN ADULT IS NOT NORMAL — look for the reason: INHALED CORTICOSTEROIDS (check technique, spacer use and rinsing) · DENTURES and poor oral hygiene · DIABETES · antibiotics · dry mouth · IMMUNOSUPPRESSION · and HIV, in which oral candidiasis and oral hairy leukoplakia are recognised markers.** Cross-refer [[K3]] 0.5.
> **· HERPETIC gingivostomatitis, herpangina and hand-foot-and-mouth disease.**
> **· ORAL MANIFESTATIONS OF SYSTEMIC DISEASE: glossitis and angular cheilitis in iron and B12 deficiency · cobblestoning and oral ulceration in CROHN disease · desquamative gingivitis in pemphigoid and lichen planus · painful persistent erosions in PEMPHIGUS VULGARIS (which often starts in the mouth — cross-refer [[G2]] 0.2) · MACROGLOSSIA in amyloidosis · and Kaposi sarcoma.**

## 0.6 Salivary Glands and Dental Problems

> [!tip] Salivary swelling — the history tells you the mechanism
> **· OBSTRUCTIVE (SIALOLITHIASIS) — the defining feature is PAIN AND SWELLING RELATED TO EATING, coming on within minutes of a meal and settling afterwards.** **Stones are commonest in the SUBMANDIBULAR gland, because its secretions are more viscous and its duct runs UPHILL against gravity.** **Bimanually palpate the floor of mouth along the duct; imaging and sialendoscopy or removal.**
> **· ACUTE BACTERIAL SIALADENITIS — a hot, tender, swollen gland with PUS EXPRESSIBLE FROM THE DUCT, typically in a DEHYDRATED, elderly or POST-OPERATIVE patient with reduced salivary flow.** **Treated with hydration, sialogogues (sour sweets, lemon), massage, oral hygiene and antibiotics** — and it is largely preventable with attention to hydration and mouth care in inpatients.
> **· MUMPS — bilateral parotid swelling with systemic illness; check immunisation status; and remember orchitis, oophoritis, pancreatitis, meningitis and deafness as complications.**
> **· SJÖGREN SYNDROME — dry eyes and dry mouth with bilateral gland enlargement, anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies.** **These patients have a significantly increased risk of LYMPHOMA, particularly with persistent unilateral gland enlargement — which should prompt investigation rather than reassurance.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.4.
> **· Sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease, HIV-associated lymphoepithelial lesions, and drug-induced.**

> [!danger] Two rules about salivary gland tumours
> **1. THE SMALLER THE GLAND, THE HIGHER THE PROBABILITY THAT A TUMOUR IS MALIGNANT.**
> **Most PAROTID tumours are BENIGN (pleomorphic adenoma being the commonest). SUBMANDIBULAR tumours are malignant more often, and MINOR salivary gland tumours (palate, lip) are malignant more often still.**
> **This inverse relationship is counterintuitive and is reliably examined.**
> **2. A FACIAL NERVE PALSY WITH A PAROTID MASS MEANS MALIGNANCY UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE.**
> **The facial nerve runs THROUGH the parotid gland. Benign tumours displace it; malignant tumours invade it.** **Facial weakness with a parotid lump is never reassuring, and neither is pain or fixation.**
> **AND: DO NOT PERFORM AN INCISIONAL BIOPSY OF A PAROTID MASS.** **It risks tumour seeding along the biopsy tract and injury to the facial nerve.** **Assessment is by imaging plus FINE NEEDLE ASPIRATION or core biopsy, and definitive diagnosis is by formal parotidectomy.** Cross-refer [[F4]].

> [!warning] Dental problems, and the equity dimension
> **Dental pain and dental infection are common presentations to emergency departments and general practice, and a substantial proportion of them are there because the patient CANNOT ACCESS OR AFFORD A DENTIST.**
> **In Australia, dental care sits largely OUTSIDE Medicare. Public dental waiting lists are long, and poor oral health disproportionately affects Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, people on low incomes, people in rural and remote areas, people with disability, and residents of aged care.**
> **Poor oral health is not cosmetic: it causes pain, malnutrition, infection, social withdrawal, and is associated with cardiovascular disease and aspiration pneumonia.**
> **What a doctor can actually do: treat the pain and the infection properly · recognise spreading infection (see Ludwig angina in 0.2) · know the local public dental pathway and any emergency access · and, for inpatients and aged care residents, ensure MOUTH CARE is actually happening, because it is among the most neglected aspects of nursing care and it prevents aspiration pneumonia.** Cross-refer [[AU1]] 0.1 and [[GER2]] 0.3.

> [!danger] Two dental points with wider consequences
> **1. MEDICATION-RELATED OSTEONECROSIS OF THE JAW — associated with BISPHOSPHONATES and DENOSUMAB, and with antiangiogenic agents. Risk is much higher with the high doses used in malignancy than with osteoporosis doses.**
> **THE PREVENTIVE ACTION: ARRANGE A DENTAL ASSESSMENT AND COMPLETE ANY NEEDED EXTRACTIONS OR INVASIVE DENTAL WORK BEFORE STARTING therapy where possible, and maintain good oral hygiene during it.**
> **This is a genuine, simple, preventive step that is frequently omitted when bone protection is started — and osteonecrosis is difficult to treat once established.** Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.6 and [[J4]] 0.2.
> **2. AVULSED PERMANENT TOOTH — reimplant immediately if possible; otherwise transport in MILK or saliva, NEVER water. Time-critical.** **Do not reimplant deciduous teeth.** Cross-refer [[L8]] 0.2.
> **XEROSTOMIA — from anticholinergic and many other drugs, Sjögren syndrome, and head and neck radiotherapy.** **Its consequences are underestimated: rampant dental CARIES, candidiasis, dysphagia, denture intolerance, altered taste and disturbed sleep.** **Review the drug chart, encourage saliva substitutes and sugar-free stimulants, and arrange dental review.** Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.4.

**Ix:** **URGENT FULL BLOOD COUNT for a sore throat with fever in any patient on carbimazole, propylthiouracil, clozapine or other marrow-suppressing drug** (*why:* agranulocytosis, and these patients are specifically instructed to present with this symptom; *what:* neutrophil count). **Throat swab and consideration of antibiotics per the applicable guideline — noting that the threshold differs in high acute rheumatic fever risk populations** (*why:* preventing ARF is the purpose in those settings; *what:* group A streptococcus). **EBV serology, film and LFTs in suspected glandular fever** (*why:* confirms, and the splenomegaly advice follows; *what:* atypical lymphocytes, transaminitis). **HIV testing with a combined antigen/antibody assay in a febrile pharyngitis with rash, lymphadenopathy or mouth ulcers** (*why:* seroconversion is the greatest missed opportunity in HIV care; *what:* infection). **LARYNGOSCOPY VIA ENT for hoarseness beyond three weeks** (*why:* the larynx cannot be examined any other way, and laryngeal cancer presents early with hoarseness; *what:* the cords and the supraglottis). **CT from SKULL BASE TO MEDIASTINUM for an unexplained vocal cord palsy** (*why:* the left recurrent laryngeal nerve loops around the aortic arch, so imaging the neck alone misses the cause; *what:* the lesion along the nerve's course). **BIOPSY of any oral lesion persisting beyond three weeks, and of any erythroplakic patch** (*why:* early oral cancer is curable and late oral cancer is not, and red patches carry higher risk than white; *what:* dysplasia or carcinoma). **FBC, ferritin, B12, folate and coeliac serology in severe or atypical recurrent aphthous ulceration** (*why:* haematinic deficiency, coeliac disease and IBD present this way; *what:* deficiency, positive serology). **CONTRAST SWALLOW rather than blind nasogastric intubation where a pharyngeal pouch is suspected** (*why:* a blind tube can enter and perforate the pouch; *what:* the pouch). **Speech pathology swallow assessment before oral intake in suspected aspiration** (*why:* silent aspiration means the absence of coughing does not indicate a safe swallow; *what:* swallow safety). **Imaging and FNA — NOT incisional biopsy — for a parotid mass** (*why:* incisional biopsy risks seeding and facial nerve injury; *what:* the lesion). **Dental assessment BEFORE starting bisphosphonate or denosumab therapy** (*why:* prevents osteonecrosis of the jaw, and it is a simple step that is routinely omitted; *what:* dental disease requiring treatment first).
