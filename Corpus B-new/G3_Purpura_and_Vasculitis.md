## 0.1 Approach to the Non-Blanching Rash

> [!info] What purpura is
> **Purpura is EXTRAVASATED BLOOD in the skin — so it does not blanch, because the blood is outside the vessels and cannot be pressed away.**
> **PETECHIAE — pinpoint. PURPURA — intermediate. ECCHYMOSES — large.** `UNVERIFIED — the size definitions.`
> **Compare with TELANGIECTASIA and erythema, which DO blanch because the blood is still within vessels.**

> [!danger] Three questions cover the entire differential
> **QUESTION 1: IS THE PATIENT SICK?**
> **A non-blanching rash in an unwell, febrile or hypotensive patient is MENINGOCOCCAL SEPSIS until proven otherwise — give antibiotics immediately, before any investigation.** Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.9.
> **Also consider: DIC · purpura fulminans · thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura · severe sepsis of any cause · and viral haemorrhagic fever with the right exposure history.**
> **QUESTION 2: IS IT PALPABLE?**
> **PALPABLE PURPURA MEANS VASCULITIS — inflammation in the vessel wall producing a raised, infiltrated lesion — until proven otherwise.**
> **NON-PALPABLE (macular) purpura means a PLATELET problem, a COAGULATION problem, or a VESSEL WALL/SUPPORTING TISSUE problem.**
> **Run your fingers over it with your eyes shut if necessary. This single physical sign splits the differential more efficiently than any test.**
> **QUESTION 3: WHAT IS THE PLATELET COUNT?**
> **This divides the non-palpable group into thrombocytopenic and non-thrombocytopenic causes.**

> [!tip] Distribution tells you a great deal
> **· DEPENDENT AREAS — lower legs and ankles when ambulant, buttocks and back when bedbound. This gravity-dependence is characteristic of VASCULITIS, and finding purpura on the BACK of a bedbound patient rather than the legs is the same phenomenon, not a different disease.**
> **· ABOVE THE NIPPLE LINE (face, neck, upper chest) after coughing, vomiting or straining — MECHANICAL petechiae from raised venous pressure. Benign in a well patient.**
> **· SUN-EXPOSED EXTENSOR FOREARMS AND DORSAL HANDS in an older person — senile purpura.**
> **· PRESSURE POINTS AND PROTECTED SITES — consider trauma, and consider abuse.**
> **· PERIFOLLICULAR — think scurvy.**
> **· PERIORBITAL after minor pressure — think amyloid.**

> [!warning] What to ask and what to check in every case
> **HISTORY: fever and systemic illness · ARTHRALGIA · ABDOMINAL PAIN · HAEMATURIA · numbness or weakness (mononeuritis multiplex) · weight loss · MUCOSAL bleeding · recent infection, particularly streptococcal · DRUGS started in the preceding weeks · travel · and bleeding history.**
> **EXAMINATION: the whole skin including the back and buttocks · mucosae (wet purpura) · blood pressure · joints · and a neurological examination for mononeuritis.**
> **CHECK THE URINE. EVERY TIME.**
> **Glomerulonephritis accompanying cutaneous vasculitis is SILENT, determines the prognosis, and is detected by a dipstick that takes thirty seconds.** **A patient discharged with "a rash on the legs" and undetected nephritis is a preventable renal failure.**

## 0.2 Thrombocytopenic and Platelet-Related Purpura

> [!danger] Wet purpura predicts serious bleeding
> **"WET PURPURA" — haemorrhagic bullae in the MOUTH, bleeding gums, epistaxis, or retinal haemorrhage — indicates a substantially higher risk of serious bleeding, including intracranial haemorrhage, than skin (dry) purpura alone.**
> **LOOK IN THE MOUTH of any patient with purpura and thrombocytopenia.** It changes the urgency.

> [!tip] The causes, in outline
> **The full framework is in [[J1]] 0.6. In summary:**
> **· REDUCED PRODUCTION — marrow failure, infiltration, myelodysplasia, B12 and folate deficiency, alcohol, drugs, viral infection.**
> **· INCREASED DESTRUCTION — IMMUNE THROMBOCYTOPENIA · drug-induced · HEPARIN-INDUCED THROMBOCYTOPENIA (which causes THROMBOSIS, not bleeding) · TTP and HUS · DIC.**
> **· SEQUESTRATION — hypersplenism, most often from cirrhosis.**
> **· INFECTION — DENGUE (important in returned travellers, with thrombocytopenia, myalgia and retro-orbital pain), HIV, EBV, and severe sepsis.**
> **· PREGNANCY — gestational thrombocytopenia, pre-eclampsia and HELLP.**
> **ALWAYS EXCLUDE PSEUDOTHROMBOCYTOPENIA (EDTA-induced platelet clumping) with a film and a citrate sample before investigating an unexpected isolated low count.** Cross-refer [[J1]] 0.6.

> [!warning] Purpura with a normal platelet count and normal clotting
> **Platelet FUNCTION may be impaired despite a normal number:**
> **· ANTIPLATELET DRUGS — aspirin, clopidogrel — and this is by far the commonest cause.**
> **· URAEMIA — uraemic platelet dysfunction, responsive to desmopressin and dialysis.**
> **· Myeloproliferative neoplasms, particularly essential thrombocythaemia at very high counts, through acquired von Willebrand syndrome.**
> **· Inherited platelet function disorders.**
> **· Liver disease and paraproteinaemia.**
> **Or the vessel wall and its supporting tissue may be the problem — see 0.5.**

## 0.3 Cutaneous Small Vessel Vasculitis

> [!info] The clinical picture
> **PALPABLE PURPURA, symmetrical, on DEPENDENT areas, sometimes with a burning or painful quality, occasionally blistering, becoming necrotic or ulcerating in severe cases.**
> **The histological correlate is LEUCOCYTOCLASTIC VASCULITIS — neutrophilic infiltration of small vessel walls with fibrinoid necrosis and nuclear dust.**
> **It is a REACTION PATTERN, not a diagnosis — the task is to find what provoked it.**

> [!warning] The causes — a structured list, because the history is the investigation
> **· INFECTION — streptococcal (ask about a recent sore throat) · hepatitis B and C · HIV · INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS (which must be considered in anyone with purpura, fever and a murmur) · and many others.**
> **· DRUGS — the commonest identifiable cause. BETA-LACTAM antibiotics · SULFONAMIDES · NSAIDs · ALLOPURINOL · THIAZIDES · anticonvulsants · and many more. Typically 7 to 10 days after starting.**
> **· AUTOIMMUNE DISEASE — systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren syndrome.**
> **· MALIGNANCY — particularly haematological, and particularly in an older patient with unexplained vasculitis.**
> **· IgA VASCULITIS — see below.**
> **· CRYOGLOBULINAEMIA — with hepatitis C.**
> **· IDIOPATHIC — a substantial proportion, after a thorough search.**

> [!danger] Biopsy early, and take two
> **· A LESIONAL biopsy for histology should be taken from a FRESH lesion — ideally less than 48 hours old.** **Older lesions show non-specific change and the diagnostic features are lost.**
> **· A PERILESIONAL biopsy for DIRECT IMMUNOFLUORESCENCE — because DETECTING IgA deposition establishes IgA VASCULITIS, which has different implications (renal follow-up, and in children the risk of intussusception).** **This is missed if DIF is not requested, and IgA vasculitis in adults is then not recognised as such.**
> **Alongside: urinalysis and urine microscopy, renal function, blood pressure, FBC and film, LFT, inflammatory markers, ANA, ANCA, COMPLEMENT (low complement suggests lupus or cryoglobulinaemia), CRYOGLOBULINS, hepatitis B and C serology, HIV, ASOT, and blood cultures where endocarditis is possible.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.6.

> [!warning] IgA vasculitis — the renal involvement can appear weeks later
> **The classic tetrad: PALPABLE PURPURA over the BUTTOCKS and EXTENSOR SURFACES of the legs · ARTHRALGIA or arthritis · ABDOMINAL PAIN · and NEPHRITIS.**
> **Predominantly a childhood disease, often following an upper respiratory infection, but it occurs in adults where it tends to be more severe with more renal involvement.**
> **TWO THINGS THAT MATTER:**
> **1. IN CHILDREN, the abdominal pain can herald INTUSSUSCEPTION** (typically ileo-ileal, so it may not be seen on a standard ultrasound view) — cross-refer [[M5]] 0.1.
> **2. THE NEPHRITIS MAY APPEAR WEEKS AFTER THE RASH HAS RESOLVED.**
> **So these patients need URINALYSIS AND BLOOD PRESSURE MONITORING FOR MONTHS after the acute episode**, on a defined schedule — and this follow-up is routinely not arranged, because by the time the nephritis develops the rash has gone and everyone has moved on. **The renal outcome determines the long-term prognosis.** `UNVERIFIED — the monitoring schedule.`
> Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.5 and [[H1]] 0.3.

> [!tip] Management of skin-limited disease
> **Identify and REMOVE the trigger — stop the suspected drug, treat the infection.**
> **Most skin-limited cutaneous small vessel vasculitis is SELF-LIMITING over weeks.**
> **Supportive: rest, LEG ELEVATION (which genuinely helps, given the gravity-dependence), compression where tolerated, and analgesia.**
> **Colchicine, dapsone or short-course corticosteroids for persistent or symptomatic skin disease.**
> **SYSTEMIC IMMUNOSUPPRESSION IS FOR ORGAN INVOLVEMENT, not for the rash** — which is why the urine dipstick and the neurological examination matter more than the appearance of the legs.

## 0.4 The Systemic Vasculitides

> [!danger] The red flags that mean this is not skin-limited
> **· FEVER, weight loss and night sweats.**
> **· MONONEURITIS MULTIPLEX — a wrist drop or foot drop, or patchy asymmetrical sensory loss. This is vasculitis of the vasa nervorum and it is a hallmark of systemic disease.** **Ask about it and test for it; it is missed if you only look at the skin.**
> **· HAEMATURIA or PROTEINURIA.**
> **· HAEMOPTYSIS or unexplained hypoxia — PULMONARY-RENAL SYNDROME is an emergency.** Cross-refer [[H1]] 0.3.
> **· Sinus or nasal disease, epistaxis, crusting or hearing loss — suggesting granulomatosis with polyangiitis.**
> **· ASTHMA with eosinophilia — suggesting eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis.**
> **· Digital ischaemia, gangrene or nail-fold infarcts.**
> **· Severe abdominal pain — mesenteric vasculitis.**
> **· Scleritis, episcleritis or visual change.**
> **Any of these means urgent ANCA, anti-GBM, complement, urine microscopy and specialist involvement — not a dermatology outpatient appointment in six weeks.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.5.

> [!warning] Livedo reticularis versus livedo racemosa — a distinction worth making
> **· LIVEDO RETICULARIS — a REGULAR, COMPLETE, closed net-like violaceous pattern, usually symmetrical, often on the legs.** **Frequently PHYSIOLOGICAL — cutis marmorata in infants and in cold-exposed adults — and it resolves on warming.**
> **· LIVEDO RACEMOSA — an IRREGULAR, BROKEN, BRANCHING pattern with open, incomplete rings, persisting on warming.** **This is PATHOLOGICAL.**
> **Livedo racemosa suggests: ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME · vasculitis, particularly polyarteritis nodosa · CHOLESTEROL EMBOLISATION · Sneddon syndrome (livedo with stroke) · and calciphylaxis.**
> **The distinction is straightforward once you know to look for whether the net is closed or broken, and it converts an incidental finding into a diagnostic lead.**

> [!tip] The cutaneous features of specific vasculitides
> **· POLYARTERITIS NODOSA (medium vessel) — livedo racemosa, tender subcutaneous NODULES along vessels, ulcers, digital gangrene, and MONONEURITIS MULTIPLEX. Associated with HEPATITIS B. Renal microaneurysms on angiography, and characteristically it SPARES the glomeruli (so it causes hypertension and infarction rather than glomerulonephritis).**
> **· GRANULOMATOSIS WITH POLYANGIITIS — palpable purpura, ulcers and pyoderma-like lesions, alongside SADDLE-NOSE deformity, nasal crusting, cavitating lung lesions and glomerulonephritis.**
> **· EOSINOPHILIC GRANULOMATOSIS WITH POLYANGIITIS — purpura and nodules, with ASTHMA, EOSINOPHILIA and neuropathy.**
> **· CRYOGLOBULINAEMIC VASCULITIS — purpura (often worse after cold exposure or standing), arthralgia, neuropathy and glomerulonephritis. TEST FOR HEPATITIS C.**
> **· BEHÇET DISEASE — recurrent oral AND genital ULCERS, uveitis, erythema nodosum-like lesions, and PATHERGY (a papule or pustule forming at a needle puncture site — which can be tested deliberately).**

## 0.5 Non-Inflammatory and Vessel Wall Purpura

> [!tip] Senile purpura — common, benign, and worth naming
> **Well-demarcated, dark purple, non-palpable ecchymoses on the EXTENSOR FOREARMS AND DORSAL HANDS of older people, appearing after minimal or unrecalled trauma, resolving over weeks WITHOUT the colour change of a normal bruise, and often leaving brown staining.**
> **Caused by loss of dermal collagen and perivascular support, from ageing and chronic SUN DAMAGE — so it occurs on sun-exposed sites and the skin there is thin and atrophic.**
> **It is BENIGN and requires NO investigation** — but two things are worth doing: **check whether the patient is on topical or systemic CORTICOSTEROIDS (which produce the same picture and are modifiable) and ensure that purpura elsewhere or systemic features are not being overlooked.**
> **Naming it and explaining it relieves considerable anxiety in patients who fear a blood disorder.**

> [!danger] Scurvy — re-emerging, and repeatedly missed
> **Vitamin C deficiency, and it is NOT of historical interest — it occurs now, and the diagnosis is regularly delayed by months.**
> **THE SKIN SIGNS ARE DISTINCTIVE: PERIFOLLICULAR PURPURA (purpura arranged around hair follicles) with CORKSCREW HAIRS (coiled, fragmented hairs trapped in the follicle) — most often on the legs.**
> **Plus: swollen, spongy, BLEEDING GUMS (in dentate patients) · poor wound healing and breakdown of old scars · fatigue and depression · arthralgia and haemarthrosis · and anaemia.**
> **WHO GETS IT: people with severely restricted diets — including ARFID and other eating disorders · ALCOHOL dependence · isolated elderly people living alone · people with severe mental illness · those with malabsorption or after bariatric surgery · and people experiencing homelessness or food insecurity.**
> **Because it presents with purpura, bleeding and fatigue, it is investigated as a haematological or vasculitic problem, sometimes with a bone marrow biopsy, before anyone takes a dietary history.**
> **ASK WHAT THE PATIENT ACTUALLY EATS.**
> **The response to vitamin C replacement is rapid and dramatic**, which makes the diagnosis satisfying and the delay regrettable. Cross-refer [[GER2]] 0.3.

> [!warning] The other structural and vascular causes
> **· CORTICOSTEROID PURPURA — from systemic or potent topical steroids, with skin atrophy, striae and easy bruising.**
> **· AL AMYLOIDOSIS — PERIORBITAL ("raccoon eye") and "PINCH" PURPURA appearing after trivial pressure, coughing or rubbing the eyes, caused by amyloid infiltration of vessel walls plus acquired factor X deficiency.** **Uncommon but close to specific.** Cross-refer [[J4]] 0.3.
> **· CHOLESTEROL EMBOLISATION — days to weeks AFTER an arterial procedure or after starting anticoagulation, with livedo racemosa, BLUE TOES WITH PRESERVED PULSES, eosinophilia, low complement and progressive renal impairment.** Cross-refer [[H3]] 0.2.
> **· CALCIPHYLAXIS — in dialysis patients and advanced chronic kidney disease: exquisitely PAINFUL retiform (net-like) purpura progressing to black necrotic ulcers, typically on the thighs, abdomen and other fatty areas. Very high mortality, and the pain precedes the visible lesion.**
> **· WARFARIN-INDUCED SKIN NECROSIS — within days of starting warfarin without heparin cover, in fatty areas, caused by the transient procoagulant state as protein C falls first.** Cross-refer [[J3]] 0.5.
> **· PURPURA FULMINANS — widespread retiform purpura with DIC, in meningococcal and other severe sepsis, and in protein C deficiency.**
> **· Inherited connective tissue disorders — Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, pseudoxanthoma elasticum.**
> **· Trauma, mechanical purpura, and FACTITIOUS purpura.**

## 0.6 Purpura in Specific Settings

> [!danger] Children
> **The full paediatric approach is in [[M2]] 0.5. The essentials:**
> **· A non-blanching rash in an unwell child is MENINGOCOCCAL DISEASE until antibiotics have been given.**
> **· PETECHIAE CONFINED ABOVE THE NIPPLE LINE after forceful coughing or vomiting, in a WELL child, is a recognisable BENIGN mechanical pattern — but the child must be genuinely well and the distribution must fit.**
> **· ITP — purpura and bruising in an otherwise well child, often post-viral.**
> **· LEUKAEMIA — purpura with PALLOR, bone pain, lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly. A full blood count and film today.**
> **· IgA VASCULITIS — buttocks and extensor legs, with the follow-up obligation described in 0.3.**
> **· NON-ACCIDENTAL INJURY — BRUISING IN A NON-MOBILE INFANT IS ABUSE UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE ("if they don't cruise, they don't bruise"), along with bruises in protected sites (ears, neck, torso, buttocks, inner thighs), patterned bruises, injuries inconsistent with the developmental stage, and delayed presentation.** Cross-refer [[GER4]] and [[L7]] 0.5.

> [!warning] Older people
> **· Senile purpura, corticosteroids and anticoagulants account for most of it.**
> **· BUT DO NOT ATTRIBUTE PURPURA TO ANTICOAGULATION WITHOUT CHECKING THE PLATELET COUNT AND CONSIDERING VASCULITIS.** "He's on warfarin" is an explanation that stops the assessment, in the same way it does with haematuria.
> **· ELDER ABUSE — bruising in unusual or protected sites, bruises of different ages, PATTERNED bruising (grip marks on the upper arms, restraint marks at the wrists), injuries inconsistent with the history, delayed presentation, and a carer who answers for the patient or will not leave the room.** **Ask to see the patient alone.** Cross-refer [[GER4]] and [[GER1]] 0.5.
> **· And in a frail older person with unexplained purpura and poor intake, consider SCURVY.**

> [!tip] Three other contexts
> **· THE RETURNED TRAVELLER — DENGUE (thrombocytopenia, myalgia, retro-orbital pain, and a characteristic recovery rash) · RICKETTSIAL infection (look for an ESCHAR) · MENINGOCOCCAL disease · malaria · and, with the right exposure history and in the right epidemiological context, viral haemorrhagic fever, which requires isolation and public health notification before anything else.** Cross-refer [[K1]] 0.4.
> **· INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS — splinter haemorrhages, conjunctival and palatal petechiae, JANEWAY LESIONS (non-tender palmar macules) and OSLER NODES (tender pulp nodules), with fever and a murmur.** **Consider it in anyone with purpura and fever, particularly people who inject drugs or have prosthetic valves — and take blood cultures BEFORE antibiotics.** Cross-refer [[B5]] 0.1.
> **· THE BEDBOUND PATIENT — dependent purpura appears on the BACK, buttocks and flanks rather than the legs, and is easily missed if the patient is not rolled.** **Roll them.**

**Ix:** **PALPATE the rash, and check the URINE — in every case** (*why:* palpability separates vasculitis from everything else, and silent glomerulonephritis determines the prognosis; *what:* palpable lesions, haematuria and proteinuria). **FBC with film, coagulation profile** (*why:* separates thrombocytopenic, coagulopathic and vessel-wall causes, and the film may show the cause; *what:* platelet count, fragments, blasts). **UEC and blood pressure** (*why:* renal involvement; *what:* impairment, hypertension). **Blood cultures where febrile — BEFORE antibiotics where the patient is stable enough** (*why:* endocarditis and sepsis; *what:* organism). **SKIN BIOPSY of a FRESH (under 48-hour) lesion for histology, PLUS a perilesional specimen for direct immunofluorescence** (*why:* older lesions lose the diagnostic features, and DIF is what identifies IgA vasculitis; *what:* leucocytoclastic vasculitis, immunoreactant class). **ANA, ANCA, COMPLEMENT, CRYOGLOBULINS, rheumatoid factor** (*why:* the autoimmune and cryoglobulinaemic causes, and low complement narrows the differential; *what:* the serological pattern — cross-refer [[L2]] 0.6). **HEPATITIS B and C, HIV serology, ASOT** (*why:* treatable infective triggers — hepatitis B with polyarteritis nodosa, hepatitis C with cryoglobulinaemia; *what:* infection). **A DRUG HISTORY covering the preceding weeks** (*why:* drugs are the commonest identifiable cause of cutaneous vasculitis and stopping them is the treatment; *what:* the temporal relationship). **A DIETARY history where perifollicular purpura, gum changes or corkscrew hairs are present** (*why:* scurvy is missed because nobody asks what the patient eats; *what:* vitamin C intake). **Serum electrophoresis and free light chains where periorbital or pinch purpura** (*why:* AL amyloidosis; *what:* paraprotein — cross-refer [[J4]] 0.3).
