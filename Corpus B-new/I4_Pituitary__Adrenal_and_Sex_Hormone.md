## 0.1 The Pituitary and Hypopituitarism

**A/P:** The **anterior pituitary** secretes **ACTH, TSH, LH and FSH, growth hormone and prolactin** under hypothalamic control via the portal system. The **posterior pituitary** stores and releases **ADH and oxytocin**, which are synthesised in the hypothalamus.
**Prolactin is unique in being under TONIC INHIBITION by dopamine** — every other anterior pituitary hormone is under stimulatory control. **This single fact explains two clinical phenomena**: dopamine antagonist drugs raise prolactin, and **a lesion compressing the pituitary stalk causes prolactin to RISE while every other hormone falls.**

> [!tip] The order of hormone loss in a compressive lesion
> Classically **growth hormone → gonadotrophins (LH, FSH) → TSH → ACTH**, remembered as **"Go Look For The Adenoma."**
> **So the earliest features are growth hormone deficiency (non-specific fatigue, reduced quality of life) and hypogonadism (loss of libido, amenorrhoea, erectile dysfunction, loss of body hair)** — which is why pituitary disease is typically present for years before diagnosis, the symptoms having been attributed to ageing, stress or depression.
> **A raised prolactin with low other pituitary hormones suggests stalk compression rather than a prolactinoma** — and the distinction matters because the treatments differ. See 0.2.

> [!warning] Causes of hypopituitarism
> **· Pituitary adenoma and its treatment — surgery and radiotherapy.**
> **· Pituitary apoplexy** — see below.
> **· Sheehan syndrome** — pituitary infarction following major postpartum haemorrhage. **Classically presents with FAILURE TO LACTATE and failure to resume menses**, though it may present years later with insidious hypopituitarism. **Ask about the obstetric history in any woman with unexplained hypopituitarism.**
> **· Traumatic brain injury** — a genuinely under-recognised cause. Pituitary dysfunction after significant head injury is common and is frequently missed because the symptoms are attributed to the brain injury itself.
> **· Infiltrative and inflammatory** — sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, haemochromatosis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, **lymphocytic hypophysitis** (including in pregnancy) and **immune checkpoint inhibitor hypophysitis**, which is increasingly common and often permanent. Cross-refer [[A5]] 0.7.
> **· Craniopharyngioma, metastases, empty sella, and genetic causes.**

> [!danger] Secondary adrenal insufficiency looks different from primary — and the differences are diagnostic
> **· NO hyperpigmentation** — because there is no ACTH excess (in primary disease, the POMC precursor of ACTH stimulates melanocytes).
> **· NORMAL potassium** — because **aldosterone is regulated by the renin-angiotensin system, not by ACTH**, so mineralocorticoid function is preserved. **Hyperkalaemia therefore points to PRIMARY adrenal failure.**
> **· Hyponatraemia still occurs**, through cortisol deficiency causing inappropriate ADH release.
> **· Hypotension is usually less profound** than in primary disease.

> [!danger] Give hydrocortisone before thyroxine
> **In any patient with combined pituitary hormone deficiency, replace glucocorticoid FIRST.**
> **Thyroid hormone increases cortisol clearance and metabolic demand, so giving thyroxine to a patient with unrecognised adrenal insufficiency can precipitate an adrenal crisis.** The same principle appears in myxoedema coma. Cross-refer [[I1]] 0.1 and 0.4.

> [!danger] Pituitary apoplexy
> **Sudden haemorrhage or infarction into a pituitary tumour** — often the first presentation of a previously unknown adenoma.
> **Presentation: abrupt severe headache (frequently thunderclap), visual field loss, ophthalmoplegia from cavernous sinus involvement (III, IV, VI), reduced consciousness, and meningism** — and it is **misdiagnosed as subarachnoid haemorrhage or meningitis.** Cross-refer [[D1]] 0.2 and [[D7]] 0.6.
> **The immediate priority is HYDROCORTISONE** — acute ACTH deficiency causes adrenal crisis and is the reversible cause of death. **Give it before imaging confirms anything.**
> Then urgent MRI, ophthalmological assessment of fields and acuity, and neurosurgical involvement, with surgery for visual compromise or deteriorating consciousness.

> [!tip] Posterior pituitary — diabetes insipidus
> **Cranial (central) DI** — deficient ADH: pituitary surgery, tumour, trauma, infiltration.
> **Nephrogenic DI** — renal resistance to ADH: **lithium (the commonest), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia**, chronic kidney disease.
> **Polyuria and polydipsia with dilute urine despite a rising serum osmolality.** Distinguished from primary polydipsia by water deprivation testing or copeptin measurement, and from cranial DI by the response to desmopressin.
> **After pituitary surgery, a triphasic response can occur — transient DI, then a phase of SIADH as stored hormone is released, then permanent DI** — so **a post-operative patient can swing from hypernatraemia to hyponatraemia and back**, which is confusing if not anticipated. Cross-refer [[H3]].

**S/Smx:** Insidious and non-specific — **fatigue, weight change, loss of libido, amenorrhoea, erectile dysfunction, loss of axillary and pubic hair, pallor with fine wrinkled skin, cold intolerance, postural dizziness, and hypoglycaemia.** Plus mass effect: **headache and bitemporal visual field loss.**

**Ix:** **Paired anterior pituitary hormones with their target hormones, taken in the morning — cortisol with ACTH, free T4 with TSH, LH and FSH with testosterone or oestradiol, prolactin, and IGF-1** (*why:* **a target hormone must be interpreted alongside its trophic hormone — a low free T4 with a non-elevated TSH is central hypothyroidism, whereas the free T4 alone looks like ordinary hypothyroidism**; *what:* the paired pattern). **Short synacthen test** (*why:* assesses the adrenal axis, though it may be falsely normal in very recent-onset secondary insufficiency before adrenal atrophy develops; *what:* cortisol response). **MRI pituitary with dedicated sequences** (*why:* CT is inadequate for the sella; *what:* adenoma, stalk, apoplexy, infiltration). **Formal visual field assessment** (*why:* **bitemporal hemianopia may be asymptomatic until advanced, and its presence or progression drives the surgical decision**; *what:* field defect). Sodium and osmolality, paired with urine osmolality (*why:* DI and SIADH; *what:* the pattern). Ferritin and transferrin saturation, ACE and calcium (*why:* haemochromatosis and sarcoidosis as infiltrative causes; *what:* the underlying disease).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
**Hydrocortisone for suspected apoplexy or crisis, before confirmation.**

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Replace the deficient hormones — **glucocorticoid first, then thyroxine, then sex steroids and growth hormone as indicated.** Surgery for mass effect.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Sick day rules, an emergency hydrocortisone supply, a steroid card and a medical alert** — see 0.3. Lifelong endocrine follow-up, fertility discussion, and bone protection.

## 0.2 Pituitary Tumours

> [!tip] Mass effect
> **· Bitemporal hemianopia** from compression of the decussating nasal fibres at the optic chiasm — **the visual field defect that is nearly pathognomonic of a suprasellar mass.**
> **· Cranial nerve palsies (III, IV, V1, V2, VI) with lateral extension into the cavernous sinus** — cross-refer [[D7]] 0.6.
> **· Headache · hypopituitarism · and hydrocephalus with very large lesions.**

> [!danger] Prolactinoma — check the simple things before ordering an MRI
> **Hyperprolactinaemia has many causes and most are not a tumour:**
> **· PREGNANCY — do a pregnancy test.**
> **· DRUGS — antipsychotics (particularly risperidone and amisulpride), metoclopramide and prochlorperazine, SSRIs, opioids, verapamil, and oestrogens.** **The drug chart explains a large proportion of raised prolactins.**
> **· PRIMARY HYPOTHYROIDISM** — TRH stimulates prolactin release. **Check TFTs**; the prolactin normalises with thyroxine.
> **· Stress, venepuncture itself, chest wall lesions, nipple stimulation, renal failure, cirrhosis.**
> **· Stalk compression by ANY sellar mass** — producing a modestly raised prolactin with other hormone deficiencies.
> **Two assay pitfalls worth knowing:**
> **· Macroprolactin** — a biologically inactive complexed form causing a spuriously raised result in an asymptomatic patient. Ask the laboratory to screen for it.
> **· The "hook effect"** — **in a very large macroprolactinoma the prolactin can be SO high that it saturates the assay and produces a FALSELY LOW or normal result.** **So a large pituitary mass with an unimpressive prolactin should prompt a request for serial dilution** — otherwise a prolactinoma is misclassified as a non-functioning adenoma and taken to surgery unnecessarily.

> [!warning] Prolactinoma is the pituitary tumour treated MEDICALLY first
> **Dopamine agonists (cabergoline, bromocriptine) are first-line even for large macroadenomas**, and they shrink the tumour as well as normalising prolactin — often dramatically, with recovery of visual fields.
> **This is the exception: every other pituitary tumour is treated surgically first.** Getting it the wrong way round means an unnecessary operation.
> Presentation differs by sex: **women present earlier with amenorrhoea, galactorrhoea and infertility from a microadenoma; men present later with a macroadenoma and mass effect**, because hypogonadal symptoms are attributed to other things.

> [!tip] Acromegaly — look at old photographs
> **Growth hormone excess after epiphyseal fusion.** Onset is so insidious that neither the patient nor their family notices — **comparing current appearance with photographs from ten and twenty years earlier is genuinely the most useful diagnostic manoeuvre**, and a driver's licence or wedding photo often makes the diagnosis in the room.
> **Features: coarsening facial features, prognathism with dental separation, macroglossia, enlarging hands and feet (increasing ring and shoe size — ask), excessive sweating, arthralgia and arthropathy, CARPAL TUNNEL SYNDROME, obstructive sleep apnoea, hypertension, diabetes, cardiomyopathy, and an increased risk of COLONIC POLYPS AND CARCINOMA.**
> **Cardiovascular disease is the main cause of excess mortality**, and it is largely reversible with treatment.
> **Diagnosis: a RANDOM growth hormone is useless because secretion is pulsatile.** **IGF-1 is the screening test** (an integrated measure), and **failure of GH to suppress during an oral glucose tolerance test is confirmatory.** `UNVERIFIED — thresholds and protocols.`
> Treatment: **transsphenoidal surgery first-line**, with somatostatin analogues, dopamine agonists, and the GH receptor antagonist pegvisomant as adjuncts.

> [!tip] The other tumours
> **· Cushing disease** — an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma, usually a microadenoma. See 0.4.
> **· Non-functioning adenoma** — the commonest macroadenoma, presenting with mass effect and hypopituitarism.
> **· Craniopharyngioma** — suprasellar, often calcified, presenting in children and again in older adults, with **a high rate of diabetes insipidus and hypothalamic dysfunction** including obesity and disordered thirst.
> **· TSH-secreting adenoma** — rare, producing the unusual pattern of **raised free T4 with a non-suppressed TSH.** Cross-refer [[I1]] 0.1.

**Ix:** **Prolactin, with pregnancy test, TFTs and medication review FIRST** (*why:* these explain most raised prolactins at no cost; *what:* the reversible cause). Request for macroprolactin screening or serial dilution where the result does not fit the picture (*why:* the two assay pitfalls above; *what:* true prolactin level). **IGF-1** (*why:* the screening test for acromegaly; *what:* elevation for age). **Oral glucose tolerance test with GH measurement** (*why:* confirms acromegaly; *what:* failure of GH suppression). **Full anterior pituitary profile** (*why:* co-existing hypopituitarism determines replacement needs; *what:* deficiencies). **MRI pituitary** (*why:* the imaging test; *what:* adenoma size and extension). **Formal visual field testing** (*why:* determines urgency of surgery; *what:* bitemporal defect). Colonoscopy in acromegaly (*why:* increased colonic neoplasia risk; *what:* polyps), and echocardiography and sleep study (*why:* cardiomyopathy and sleep apnoea are major contributors to morbidity; *what:* structural disease, apnoea-hypopnoea index).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Apoplexy per 0.1.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
**Dopamine agonist for prolactinoma; transsphenoidal surgery for everything else**, with medical and radiotherapy adjuncts.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Surveillance imaging and hormone assessment, replacement of deficiencies, and — after surgery — **monitoring for the triphasic sodium response** described in 0.1.

## 0.3 Adrenal Insufficiency

> [!danger] The commonest cause of adrenal insufficiency overall is exogenous corticosteroid
> **Not Addison disease. Not tuberculosis. Prescribed steroids.**
> Any patient on supraphysiological glucocorticoid for more than a few weeks — oral, but also **high-dose inhaled, topical, intra-articular and intranasal** — has a suppressed axis and **cannot mount a stress response.**
> **The consequences: abrupt cessation, or an intercurrent illness or operation without stress dosing, precipitates an adrenal crisis.**
> **Ask about steroids in every patient with unexplained hypotension, hyponatraemia, fatigue or collapse** — including the intra-articular injection they had three weeks ago and do not consider a medication.

> [!tip] Primary versus secondary — the discriminating features
> | | **Primary (Addison)** | **Secondary (pituitary / steroid withdrawal)** |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Pigmentation** | **PRESENT** (buccal mucosa, palmar creases, scars, pressure areas) | **Absent** |
> | **Potassium** | **HIGH** (aldosterone deficient) | **Normal** |
> | **Sodium** | Low | Low |
> | **Salt craving** | Present | Absent |
> | **Aldosterone** | Deficient — **needs fludrocortisone** | Preserved — **hydrocortisone alone** |
> | **ACTH** | **High** | Low or inappropriately normal |
> **Hyperkalaemia and pigmentation together are essentially diagnostic of primary adrenal failure.**

> [!warning] Causes of primary adrenal insufficiency
> **· Autoimmune adrenalitis — the commonest in Australia**, with 21-hydroxylase antibodies, often as part of an autoimmune polyglandular syndrome (with type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, coeliac disease, vitiligo, pernicious anaemia).
> **· Tuberculosis — the commonest cause worldwide**, and still relevant in migrant populations.
> **· Adrenal haemorrhage** — meningococcal sepsis (Waterhouse-Friderichsen), anticoagulation, antiphospholipid syndrome, trauma. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.9.
> **· Metastases** (lung, breast, melanoma) and lymphoma.
> **· Congenital adrenal hyperplasia** — see 0.6.
> **· Drugs** — ketoconazole, etomidate (a single induction dose suppresses steroidogenesis), and **immune checkpoint inhibitors.** Cross-refer [[A5]] 0.7.
> **· Adrenoleukodystrophy** in young males.

**S/Smx:** **Fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain (which can mimic an acute abdomen), postural dizziness, salt craving, myalgia and arthralgia, and hyperpigmentation in primary disease.** The onset is insidious and the diagnosis is frequently made only when a crisis occurs.

> [!danger] Sick day rules and the emergency kit — the OSCE-critical education
> **Every patient on long-term glucocorticoid replacement must know:**
> **1. "Double the dose" during febrile illness**, and continue until recovery. `UNVERIFIED — the exact sick day dosing rules.`
> **2. "If you cannot keep tablets down — vomiting or diarrhoea — you need an INJECTION."** They must have **injectable hydrocortisone at home, and they and a family member must be trained to give it.**
> **3. A steroid emergency card and a medical alert bracelet.**
> **4. Extra cover for surgery, dental procedures and major stress**, arranged in advance.
> **5. Seek urgent help for vomiting, diarrhoea, or feeling profoundly unwell.**
> **This education is what prevents death from adrenal crisis, and it is frequently given once at diagnosis and never revisited.** Check they still have in-date injectable hydrocortisone and can describe when to use it.

**Ix:** **Morning (8–9 am) cortisol** (*why:* the screening test, exploiting the diurnal peak — a clearly high value excludes and a clearly low value confirms, with intermediate values requiring dynamic testing; *what:* low, indeterminate or reassuring). **Short synacthen test** (*why:* the confirmatory test; *what:* failure of cortisol to rise adequately — noting it **may be falsely normal in very recent secondary insufficiency** before the adrenals atrophy). **ACTH, taken with the cortisol** (*why:* **distinguishes primary from secondary, which determines whether fludrocortisone is needed and whether the pituitary needs imaging**; *what:* high in primary, low or normal in secondary). **UEC** (*why:* the sodium and potassium pattern is itself diagnostic; *what:* hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia in primary). Glucose (*why:* hypoglycaemia; *what:* low glucose). **21-hydroxylase antibodies** (*why:* confirms autoimmune aetiology and prompts screening for associated autoimmune disease; *what:* positive). **Adrenal CT where antibodies are negative** (*why:* identifies haemorrhage, infiltration, metastases or tuberculosis; *what:* adrenal enlargement, calcification, mass). **MRI pituitary where secondary and not explained by steroid use** (*why:* pituitary pathology; *what:* lesion). Full pituitary profile in secondary disease (*why:* other axes are usually also affected; *what:* additional deficiencies).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
**Adrenal crisis: hydrocortisone and fluid resuscitation immediately, before confirmation. Take the cortisol first only if it costs no delay.** Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.6.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
**Hydrocortisone in divided doses mimicking the diurnal rhythm, plus FLUDROCORTISONE in PRIMARY disease only.** `UNVERIFIED — doses and regimens.`

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Sick day rules, emergency injectable hydrocortisone, steroid card, medical alert — revisited at every review.** Screening for associated autoimmune disease. Bone protection. Fertility and pregnancy planning (requirements increase in pregnancy).

## 0.4 Cushing Syndrome

> [!danger] Again — the commonest cause is prescribed glucocorticoid
> **Exogenous steroid vastly outnumbers all endogenous causes combined.** Establish it before embarking on any investigation, **including inhaled, topical, intranasal, intra-articular and complementary preparations** (some "herbal" arthritis remedies contain undeclared steroids).

> [!tip] Endogenous causes, split by ACTH
> **ACTH-DEPENDENT (ACTH raised or inappropriately normal):**
> **· Cushing DISEASE — a pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma. The commonest endogenous cause.**
> **· Ectopic ACTH — small cell lung carcinoma, bronchial and other neuroendocrine tumours.**
> **ACTH-INDEPENDENT (ACTH suppressed):**
> **· Adrenal adenoma, adrenal carcinoma, or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia.**
> **Note the terminology: "Cushing DISEASE" means the pituitary cause specifically; "Cushing SYNDROME" is the clinical state from any cause.**

> [!warning] The discriminating features — separating Cushing from simple obesity
> Central obesity, moon face and a dorsocervical fat pad occur in obesity generally. **The features with genuine discriminating value are those reflecting protein catabolism:**
> **· PROXIMAL MYOPATHY — difficulty rising from a chair without using the arms.**
> **· THIN SKIN and easy BRUISING** — sometimes with skin that tears.
> **· WIDE (over about 1 cm) PURPLE striae**, as opposed to the pale narrow striae of weight gain.
> **· Poor wound healing.**
> Alongside: hypertension, glucose intolerance or diabetes, osteoporosis and fragility fractures, immunosuppression with opportunistic infection, **psychiatric change (depression, psychosis, insomnia, emotional lability)**, and in women hirsutism, acne and oligomenorrhoea.

> [!tip] Ectopic ACTH looks different
> **Rapid onset · marked HYPOKALAEMIC METABOLIC ALKALOSIS (very high cortisol overwhelms the enzyme that protects the mineralocorticoid receptor) · pronounced PIGMENTATION from very high ACTH · muscle weakness · and WEIGHT LOSS rather than gain**, because the underlying malignancy dominates.
> **A patient with hypokalaemic alkalosis, pigmentation, weakness and weight loss does not look cushingoid** — and that is precisely the presentation that gets missed.

> [!warning] The diagnostic sequence, and pseudo-Cushing
> **Step 1 — establish cortisol excess** with at least two abnormal screening tests from: **24-hour urinary free cortisol, late-night salivary cortisol, and the overnight or low-dose dexamethasone suppression test.**
> **PSEUDO-CUSHING states cause false positives: harmful alcohol use, severe depression, obesity, poorly controlled diabetes, and physiological stress.** Testing during an acute illness or in an actively drinking patient produces uninterpretable results.
> **Step 2 — measure ACTH** to determine dependence.
> **Step 3 — localise**: pituitary MRI, adrenal CT, high-dose dexamethasone suppression or CRH testing, and — where the source remains unclear — **inferior petrosal sinus sampling**, which reliably distinguishes pituitary from ectopic ACTH.
> `UNVERIFIED — all test protocols and thresholds.`

**Ix:** **Careful medication history including all routes and complementary products** (*why:* it is the commonest cause and requires no further investigation; *what:* exogenous steroid). **Two concordant screening tests** as above (*why:* single tests have significant false positive and negative rates; *what:* cortisol excess). **ACTH** (*why:* the branch point between pituitary/ectopic and adrenal causes; *what:* raised or suppressed). Potassium and bicarbonate (*why:* hypokalaemic alkalosis suggests ectopic ACTH; *what:* the pattern). **MRI pituitary, CT adrenals, and CT chest and abdomen for an ectopic source** (*why:* localisation determines surgery; *what:* adenoma, adrenal mass, neuroendocrine tumour). Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (*why:* distinguishes pituitary from ectopic where imaging is equivocal, and pituitary microadenomas are frequently not visible on MRI; *what:* central-to-peripheral ACTH gradient). Assessment of complications — glucose, lipids, bone density, blood pressure (*why:* they drive morbidity and need treating in parallel; *what:* diabetes, osteoporosis, hypertension).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Not usually required, except for severe hypokalaemia, uncontrolled hyperglycaemia or psychosis.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
**Surgical — transsphenoidal for Cushing disease, adrenalectomy for adrenal causes, resection of an ectopic source.** Medical therapy (metyrapone, ketoconazole, osilodrostat) to control cortisol before surgery or where surgery is not possible.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**After successful treatment, the contralateral or residual axis is suppressed and glucocorticoid replacement is required — sometimes for a year or more — with sick day rules.** Patients feel dreadful during this period as they withdraw from long-standing hypercortisolism, and **warning them of this in advance prevents them concluding the operation failed.** Monitor for recurrence, and treat the persisting metabolic and bone complications.

## 0.5 Mineralocorticoid Excess and Phaeochromocytoma

> [!warning] Primary aldosteronism — commoner than taught, and specifically treatable
> Introduced in [[B2]] 0.4. The points that bear repeating:
> **· It accounts for a substantial minority of hypertension and a larger proportion of resistant hypertension.**
> **· HYPOKALAEMIA IS PRESENT IN ONLY A MINORITY — its absence does NOT exclude the diagnosis.** This is the single most consequential misconception.
> **· Screen with the aldosterone-to-renin ratio**, recognising that **beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics and especially mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists all interfere** — so the drugs the patient is already on are precisely the ones that confound the test.
> **· It carries cardiovascular and renal risk BEYOND that attributable to the blood pressure alone**, through direct aldosterone-mediated fibrosis — which is why identifying it matters even when the blood pressure is controlled.
> **· Confirmatory testing, then adrenal vein sampling to determine whether the source is unilateral (surgically curable by adrenalectomy) or bilateral (treated with a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist).** **CT alone cannot reliably lateralise**, because non-functioning adenomas are common and a visible nodule may not be the source.
> `UNVERIFIED — screening thresholds, drug withdrawal periods, and confirmatory protocols.`

> [!danger] Phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma
> Introduced in [[B2]] 0.4. Extended here:
> **· The classic triad is EPISODIC headache, palpitations and diaphoresis**, characteristically with **pallor rather than flushing**, on a background of sustained or labile hypertension. Attacks may be provoked by exertion, anaesthesia, palpation of the abdomen, or certain drugs.
> **· ALPHA BLOCKADE MUST PRECEDE BETA BLOCKADE.** Giving a beta-blocker first leaves unopposed alpha-mediated vasoconstriction and can precipitate a hypertensive crisis. This is the single most examined point.
> **· Screen with plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary metanephrines**, which are more sensitive than catecholamines. **False positives occur with tricyclics, levodopa, stimulants, and physiological stress**, so pre-test conditions matter.
> **· GENETIC TESTING IS NOW RECOMMENDED IN ESSENTIALLY ALL CASES** — a substantially higher proportion than historically appreciated carry a germline mutation (**SDHx, VHL, RET, NF1**), with implications for surveillance, for multifocal and malignant disease, and for family screening. **The old "rule of 10s" understates the hereditary proportion considerably.**
> **· Never biopsy an adrenal mass before excluding phaeochromocytoma** — see 0.7.

> [!tip] Other mineralocorticoid excess states
> **· Liquorice** — glycyrrhizic acid inhibits the enzyme that protects the mineralocorticoid receptor from cortisol, producing apparent mineralocorticoid excess with hypertension and hypokalaemia. **Ask about liquorice consumption in unexplained hypokalaemic hypertension** — it is a satisfying and entirely reversible diagnosis.
> **· Liddle syndrome** — an epithelial sodium channel mutation, with hypertension, hypokalaemia and **suppressed aldosterone AND renin.**
> **· Cushing syndrome** with very high cortisol — see 0.4.
> **· Congenital adrenal hyperplasia** variants — see 0.6.

**Ix:** **Aldosterone-to-renin ratio** with attention to interfering drugs (*why:* screens for primary aldosteronism; *what:* raised ratio requiring confirmation). **Plasma free or urinary metanephrines** (*why:* screens for phaeochromocytoma, and must be done before any adrenal intervention; *what:* elevated). **Genetic testing in confirmed phaeochromocytoma or paraganglioma** (*why:* a high proportion are hereditary, with surveillance and family implications; *what:* germline variant). Potassium, bicarbonate and blood pressure (*why:* the hypokalaemic alkalotic hypertensive pattern; *what:* the combination). CT or MRI adrenals (*why:* localisation; *what:* mass). **Adrenal vein sampling in confirmed primary aldosteronism being considered for surgery** (*why:* CT cannot reliably lateralise; *what:* lateralisation).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
**Alpha blockade before beta blockade in a phaeochromocytoma crisis.** Cross-refer [[B2]] 0.3.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Adrenalectomy after adequate alpha blockade for phaeochromocytoma; adrenalectomy or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist for primary aldosteronism.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Lifelong surveillance and family screening in hereditary phaeochromocytoma.** Continued cardiovascular risk management — the vascular damage accrued before diagnosis does not reverse.

## 0.6 Androgen Excess, Hirsutism and Virilisation

> [!tip] Define the problem precisely first
> **· HIRSUTISM — terminal (coarse, pigmented) hair in a MALE pattern in a woman: upper lip, chin, chest, upper abdomen, back, inner thighs. It is ANDROGEN-dependent.** Quantified with the Ferriman-Gallwey score.
> **· HYPERTRICHOSIS — generalised increase in fine vellus hair in a NON-androgenic distribution.** Caused by drugs (**phenytoin, ciclosporin, minoxidil, corticosteroids**), malnutrition, anorexia nervosa, porphyria and paraneoplastic states. **It is a different problem with a different differential and does not warrant an androgen workup.**
> **· VIRILISATION — clitoromegaly, deepening of the voice, male-pattern temporal balding, increased muscle bulk, breast atrophy.** **This is a different order of androgen excess.**

> [!danger] Rapid-onset hirsutism with virilisation is an androgen-secreting tumour until proven otherwise
> **Slowly progressive hirsutism from adolescence is almost always PCOS.**
> **Rapid onset over months, in an older woman, with virilisation, is an ovarian or adrenal androgen-secreting tumour** — and it requires urgent investigation with testosterone, DHEAS and imaging.
> **The tempo and the presence of virilisation are the two features that separate the benign from the sinister**, and asking "how quickly did this happen?" is the key question.

> [!tip] Polycystic ovary syndrome — the commonest cause by a wide margin
> **Diagnosed by the Rotterdam criteria — two of three: oligo- or anovulation · clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism · polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound** — **after excluding other causes**, which makes it partly a diagnosis of exclusion. `UNVERIFIED — the criteria and the ultrasound thresholds, and the modified criteria for adolescents, in whom polycystic morphology is common and normal.`
> **The metabolic dimension matters more than the cosmetic one**: insulin resistance, impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, dyslipidaemia and cardiovascular risk.
> **And the endometrial risk: chronic anovulation means unopposed oestrogen, which causes endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma.** **Women with PCOS and prolonged amenorrhoea need endometrial protection** — cyclical progestogen, a combined pill, or a hormonal IUD. **This is frequently overlooked when the focus is on hair and fertility.** Cross-refer [[O4]].

> [!warning] The other causes
> **· Non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency)** — presents in adolescence or adulthood with hirsutism and oligomenorrhoea, indistinguishable from PCOS clinically. **Screened for with an early-morning follicular-phase 17-hydroxyprogesterone**, and it matters because management and genetic counselling differ.
> **· Cushing syndrome** — see 0.4.
> **· Hyperprolactinaemia** and **acromegaly.**
> **· Drugs — anabolic steroids, testosterone (including partner's transdermal gel transferred by skin contact), danazol, and valproate.**
> **· Ovarian hyperthecosis** in postmenopausal women.
> **· Classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia** presenting in the neonate with ambiguous genitalia and salt-wasting crisis — a paediatric emergency. Cross-refer [[M3]].

> [!tip] Male hypogonadism, briefly
> **Primary (testicular) — LOW testosterone with HIGH LH and FSH.** Klinefelter syndrome, orchitis, trauma, chemotherapy, radiation.
> **Secondary (pituitary/hypothalamic) — LOW testosterone with LOW or inappropriately normal LH and FSH.** Pituitary disease, hyperprolactinaemia, **opioids (a major and under-recognised cause), anabolic steroid use, obesity, and systemic illness.**
> **Diagnosis requires a MORNING total testosterone on at least two occasions**, with SHBG where the total is borderline. **Do not diagnose it from a single afternoon sample or during acute illness.**
> **Testosterone replacement requires a genuine diagnosis, not just symptoms and a borderline level**, and carries risks including erythrocytosis, **suppression of spermatogenesis and infertility** (which must be discussed with men who may want children), and prostate considerations.

**Ix:** **Total testosterone, SHBG and calculated free androgen index** (*why:* quantifies androgen excess, and **a markedly elevated testosterone points to a tumour rather than PCOS**; *what:* degree of elevation). **DHEAS** (*why:* an adrenal androgen — **a markedly raised DHEAS points to an adrenal source**; *what:* elevation). **Early-morning 17-hydroxyprogesterone** (*why:* screens for non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia, which mimics PCOS; *what:* elevation). **Prolactin, TFTs, LH and FSH** (*why:* alternative endocrine causes of oligomenorrhoea; *what:* the pattern). Cortisol screening where cushingoid (*why:* Cushing syndrome; *what:* cortisol excess). **Pelvic ultrasound and adrenal imaging where testosterone or DHEAS is markedly raised or virilisation is present** (*why:* localises a tumour; *what:* ovarian or adrenal mass). **Metabolic screen in PCOS — OGTT or HbA1c, lipids, LFT, blood pressure** (*why:* the metabolic consequences are the ones that shorten life; *what:* impaired glucose tolerance, dyslipidaemia, MASLD).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Not applicable, except neonatal salt-wasting CAH.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
**PCOS: weight management (the single most effective intervention for both metabolic and reproductive outcomes), combined oral contraceptive for cycle regulation, endometrial protection and androgenic symptoms, antiandrogens (spironolactone, cyproterone) — with EFFECTIVE CONTRACEPTION, because they are teratogenic and feminise a male fetus — metformin for metabolic features, and ovulation induction for fertility.** Cosmetic measures including laser and electrolysis are legitimate and matter to patients.
**Tumour: surgical resection.**

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Metabolic surveillance and endometrial protection in PCOS.** Fertility counselling. **Antiandrogen effects take months to become apparent because of the hair growth cycle — warn the patient, or they will conclude the treatment failed and stop it.**

## 0.7 Adrenal and Pituitary Incidentalomas

> [!tip] Two questions for every adrenal incidentaloma
> **1. Is it FUNCTIONAL?**
> **2. Is it MALIGNANT?**
> Everything else follows from those.

> [!warning] The functional workup, which is required in ALL adrenal incidentalomas
> **· Overnight dexamethasone suppression test** — for autonomous cortisol secretion, which is common and, even when subclinical, is associated with hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis and cardiovascular events.
> **· Plasma free or urinary metanephrines** — for phaeochromocytoma. **Mandatory in every case, regardless of blood pressure, because a proportion are normotensive.**
> **· Aldosterone-to-renin ratio** — **if hypertensive or hypokalaemic.**
> **· Androgens (DHEAS, testosterone)** — **if there are virilising features**, which raise the possibility of adrenocortical carcinoma.
> `UNVERIFIED — the full recommended panel and thresholds.`

> [!danger] Never biopsy an adrenal mass before excluding phaeochromocytoma
> **Biopsying an unrecognised phaeochromocytoma can precipitate a catastrophic hypertensive crisis.**
> **Measure metanephrines first, always.**
> And note that **adrenal biopsy is rarely indicated at all** — it does not reliably distinguish adrenocortical adenoma from carcinoma, and its main role is confirming a suspected metastasis in a patient with a known extra-adrenal primary.

> [!tip] Imaging phenotype predicts benignity
> **A small, homogeneous, well-circumscribed lesion with LOW ATTENUATION on unenhanced CT (reflecting high lipid content) and RAPID CONTRAST WASHOUT is characteristic of a benign adenoma.**
> **Features raising concern: large size · irregular margins · heterogeneity · high unenhanced attenuation · slow washout · necrosis or calcification · growth on serial imaging.**
> **Size and imaging phenotype together determine whether surgery or surveillance is appropriate.** `UNVERIFIED — the attenuation and size thresholds.`
> **Bilateral adrenal masses** suggest metastases, infiltration, haemorrhage, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, or bilateral disease — and warrant assessment of adrenal FUNCTION for insufficiency as well as excess.

> [!tip] Pituitary incidentaloma
> The same two questions apply, plus a third:
> **1. Is it hypersecreting?** — prolactin, IGF-1, cortisol screening.
> **2. Is there hypopituitarism?** — full anterior pituitary profile.
> **3. Is there mass effect?** — **formal visual field testing if the lesion approaches or abuts the optic chiasm.**
> **Microadenomas that are non-functioning and not near the chiasm are generally monitored; macroadenomas require closer surveillance and often intervention.** `UNVERIFIED — surveillance intervals.`

**Ix:** As set out above. **Dedicated adrenal protocol CT or MRI** (*why:* the standard scan may not include the unenhanced and washout phases needed to characterise the lesion, and the patient may need re-imaging; *what:* attenuation, washout, morphology). Serial imaging where surveillance is chosen (*why:* growth changes the assessment; *what:* interval change). Assessment for an extra-adrenal primary where metastasis is plausible (*why:* an adrenal metastasis changes the diagnosis entirely; *what:* primary tumour).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Not applicable.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Adrenalectomy for functioning lesions, for those with concerning imaging features, and for large lesions. Surveillance for small non-functioning lesions with benign imaging characteristics.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**After unilateral adrenalectomy for a cortisol-secreting lesion, the contralateral gland is suppressed and glucocorticoid replacement with sick day rules is required until recovery** — the same principle as after treating Cushing syndrome in 0.4.
