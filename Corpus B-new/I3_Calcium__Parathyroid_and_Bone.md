## 0.1 Calcium Homeostasis and Interpreting the Result

**A/P:** Serum calcium is defended within a narrow range by three hormones acting on three organs.
**Parathyroid hormone** rises when ionised calcium falls, and it: **mobilises calcium from bone · increases renal calcium reabsorption while INCREASING phosphate EXCRETION · and activates renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase**, converting 25-hydroxyvitamin D to active **calcitriol**, which increases gut absorption of both calcium and phosphate.
**The reciprocal relationship between calcium and phosphate under PTH is the key to interpreting the biochemistry: PTH raises calcium and LOWERS phosphate.** So **high calcium with LOW phosphate suggests PTH excess**, whereas **high calcium with HIGH phosphate suggests a non-PTH mechanism** — malignancy, vitamin D excess, or renal failure.

> [!tip] Correct for albumin — but ionised calcium is what actually matters
> **Roughly half of circulating calcium is bound to albumin, and only the ionised fraction is physiologically active.**
> **A low albumin gives a low TOTAL calcium with a normal ionised calcium** — hence the correction formula, which is an approximation. `UNVERIFIED — the correction formula.`
> **Ionised calcium should be measured directly where accuracy matters** — critical illness, massive transfusion, dialysis, and where the corrected value does not fit the clinical picture.

> [!danger] Alkalosis causes symptomatic hypocalcaemia with a normal total calcium
> **Alkalosis increases the binding of calcium to albumin, reducing the ionised fraction.**
> **This is why hyperventilation causes perioral tingling and carpopedal spasm despite a completely normal total calcium** — and why a patient with a metabolic alkalosis from vomiting can develop tetany. Cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.7.
> **Correcting the alkalosis corrects the calcium.** Giving calcium to a hyperventilating patient treats the wrong problem.

> [!warning] The single most useful investigation in any calcium abnormality is the PTH
> **PTH is the branch point of the entire differential.**
> **· High or inappropriately NORMAL PTH with a high calcium = PTH-driven.** A "normal" PTH in the face of hypercalcaemia is inappropriate — it should be suppressed.
> **· Suppressed PTH with a high calcium = non-PTH-driven** — malignancy until proven otherwise.
> **· Low PTH with a low calcium = hypoparathyroidism.**
> **· High PTH with a low calcium = an appropriate response** — vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, or PTH resistance.
> **Send calcium, phosphate, albumin, PTH, magnesium, renal function, ALP and 25-hydroxyvitamin D together** — sending calcium alone and then chasing the rest sequentially wastes days.

## 0.2 Hypercalcaemia

> [!tip] The two-branch differential, decided by the PTH
> **PTH-MEDIATED (PTH high or inappropriately normal):**
> **· Primary hyperparathyroidism — the commonest cause in the community and in outpatients.**
> **· Tertiary hyperparathyroidism** — autonomous glands after prolonged secondary stimulation, typically in chronic kidney disease.
> **· Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH)** — see the danger box below.
> **· Lithium** — shifts the calcium set point.
> **NON-PTH-MEDIATED (PTH suppressed):**
> **· MALIGNANCY — the commonest cause in HOSPITAL inpatients**, by three mechanisms: **PTH-related peptide (humoral hypercalcaemia, classically squamous cell carcinoma, renal and breast) · direct osteolytic metastases (breast, myeloma) · and tumour production of calcitriol (lymphoma).**
> **· Granulomatous disease — sarcoidosis and tuberculosis** — where macrophages express 1-alpha-hydroxylase and produce calcitriol autonomously.
> **· Vitamin D toxicity**, including from supplements.
> **· Thyrotoxicosis · immobilisation · adrenal insufficiency · phaeochromocytoma · thiazide diuretics · vitamin A excess · milk-alkali syndrome (now usually calcium supplement excess).**

> [!danger] Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia — the diagnosis that prevents a pointless operation
> **An autosomal dominant, benign, lifelong condition caused by an inactivating calcium-sensing receptor mutation.** The set point is reset upward: calcium is mildly high, PTH is normal or mildly raised — **biochemically it looks exactly like mild primary hyperparathyroidism.**
> **The discriminator is the URINARY CALCIUM: it is LOW in FHH (the kidney avidly reabsorbs calcium) and normal or high in primary hyperparathyroidism.** A **24-hour urinary calcium or calcium-creatinine clearance ratio** distinguishes them. `UNVERIFIED — the ratio and its threshold.`
> **Why it matters: FHH requires NO treatment and NO surgery. Parathyroidectomy does not cure it** — and patients have undergone unnecessary neck exploration, sometimes repeatedly, because the urinary calcium was never measured.
> **Clues: mild hypercalcaemia present for years, from a young age, asymptomatic, with a family history and no complications.** **Measure urinary calcium before referring anyone for parathyroid surgery.**

> [!warning] Symptoms — "stones, bones, abdominal groans and psychic moans"
> **· Renal — polyuria and polydipsia (hypercalcaemia causes a nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, which is why these patients are VOLUME DEPLETED), nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, renal impairment.**
> **· Bone — pain, fractures, osteoporosis.**
> **· Gastrointestinal — anorexia, nausea, vomiting, constipation, and pancreatitis and peptic ulceration in severe cases.**
> **· Neuropsychiatric — fatigue, weakness, depression, confusion, and in severe hypercalcaemia drowsiness, coma.**
> **· Cardiac — SHORT QT interval, and arrhythmia and hypertension.**
> **Severity of symptoms depends on the RATE of rise as much as the absolute level** — a slowly rising calcium in primary hyperparathyroidism may be well tolerated at a level that would render a patient with malignancy comatose.

> [!danger] The treatment sequence — fluids first, and why
> **1. INTRAVENOUS SALINE, and generously.** These patients are **profoundly volume depleted** — hypercalcaemia impairs renal concentrating ability, causing polyuria, and the nausea and confusion reduce intake. **Rehydration alone lowers calcium substantially by restoring glomerular filtration and calcium excretion**, and it is the single most important initial step.
> **2. BISPHOSPHONATE** — the mainstay for hypercalcaemia of malignancy, but **it takes two to four days to work**, so it does not replace fluids. Dose-adjust in renal impairment.
> **3. CALCITONIN** — works within hours, useful as a bridge, but **tachyphylaxis develops within a couple of days**, so it buys time rather than providing definitive control.
> **4. DENOSUMAB** — effective, particularly in bisphosphonate-refractory disease and in renal impairment.
> **5. CORTICOSTEROIDS** — specifically for **granulomatous disease, lymphoma and vitamin D toxicity**, where the mechanism is excess calcitriol. **They do not work in hyperparathyroidism or in most solid tumour hypercalcaemia.**
> **6. Dialysis** in extremis or severe renal failure.
> **7. Treat the underlying cause**, and **stop the contributing drugs — thiazides, lithium, calcium and vitamin D supplements.**
> **Loop diuretics are NO LONGER routinely recommended** — the old "saline and frusemide" teaching has been abandoned, because it worsens volume depletion. **Use them only if the patient becomes fluid overloaded during rehydration.**
> `UNVERIFIED — all agents, doses, fluid rates and the severity thresholds triggering each step.`

> [!warning] Hypercalcaemia of malignancy carries a poor prognosis
> Its development in a patient with cancer is a marker of advanced disease and **short median survival.**
> **This should prompt a goals-of-care conversation** alongside treating the calcium — treating the biochemistry repeatedly without addressing the trajectory serves the patient poorly. Cross-refer [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.5 and [[J5_Oncological_Emergencies_and_Palliative_Care]].

**Ix:** **Corrected calcium (or ionised calcium), phosphate, albumin, PTH, magnesium, UEC, ALP and 25-hydroxyvitamin D — sent together** (*why:* the PTH determines the branch, and the phosphate and ALP refine it; *what:* the pattern). **24-hour urinary calcium or calcium-creatinine clearance ratio** (*why:* **excludes FHH before any consideration of surgery**; *what:* low urinary calcium in FHH). **PTH-related peptide** where malignancy is suspected with a suppressed PTH (*why:* confirms humoral hypercalcaemia of malignancy; *what:* elevated PTHrP). **Serum protein electrophoresis, free light chains and a skeletal survey or whole-body imaging** (*why:* **myeloma presents with hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, anaemia and bone pain — the CRAB features — and is missed if electrophoresis is not specifically requested**; *what:* paraprotein, lytic lesions — cross-refer [[J4_Paraproteins_and_Lymphoproliferative]]). ECG (*why:* short QT and arrhythmia; *what:* QT interval). Chest imaging and serum ACE where granulomatous disease is considered (*why:* sarcoidosis and tuberculosis; *what:* hilar lymphadenopathy). TFTs and morning cortisol (*why:* thyrotoxicosis and adrenal insufficiency both cause hypercalcaemia; *what:* abnormal results). Imaging for malignancy directed by the history (*why:* the commonest inpatient cause; *what:* primary tumour, metastases).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
**Intravenous saline.** Stop contributing drugs. Cardiac monitoring in severe hypercalcaemia. Bisphosphonate or denosumab where malignancy-related, calcitonin as a bridge if severe.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the cause — parathyroidectomy, oncological management, corticosteroids for granulomatous disease.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Monitoring, and — where the cause is malignancy — goals-of-care discussion.

## 0.3 Hypocalcaemia

> [!tip] Causes
> **· Post-surgical hypoparathyroidism — the commonest cause**, after total thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy or radical neck surgery. **It may be transient or permanent.**
> **· HYPOMAGNESAEMIA — see the danger box.**
> **· Vitamin D deficiency** — the commonest cause in the community.
> **· Chronic kidney disease** — reduced calcitriol production and phosphate retention.
> **· Autoimmune hypoparathyroidism**, including as part of autoimmune polyglandular syndrome.
> **· Acute pancreatitis** — calcium saponification by released lipase. Cross-refer [[C7_Pancreatobiliary_Disease]] 0.1.
> **· Rhabdomyolysis and tumour lysis syndrome** — hyperphosphataemia binding calcium.
> **· Massive transfusion** — citrate chelation. Cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.2.
> **· "Hungry bone syndrome"** — rapid skeletal uptake of calcium after parathyroidectomy for severe hyperparathyroidism.
> **· Drugs** — bisphosphonates, denosumab, proton pump inhibitors (via magnesium), foscarnet.
> **· Pseudohypoparathyroidism** — PTH resistance, with high PTH and low calcium, and characteristic skeletal features.
> **· Critical illness, sepsis and alkalosis.**

> [!danger] Correct the magnesium, or the calcium will not correct
> **Magnesium depletion causes a functional hypoparathyroidism: it impairs PTH SECRETION and causes PTH RESISTANCE at the target tissues.**
> **So hypocalcaemia due to hypomagnesaemia will NOT respond to calcium replacement until the magnesium is corrected.** The same mechanism makes hypokalaemia refractory in magnesium depletion.
> **Any refractory hypocalcaemia or hypokalaemia should prompt a magnesium level** — this is one of the highest-yield practical points in electrolyte management, and it is repeatedly missed while calcium and potassium are given without effect. See 0.6.

> [!warning] Symptoms and signs
> **Neuromuscular irritability is the theme: perioral and acral paraesthesia · muscle cramps · carpopedal spasm and TETANY · laryngospasm and bronchospasm · seizures.**
> **· Chvostek sign** — tapping the facial nerve produces facial twitching. **Present in a proportion of normal people, so it is sensitive but not specific.**
> **· Trousseau sign** — inflating a blood pressure cuff above systolic pressure for a few minutes induces carpal spasm. **More specific than Chvostek**, and the better bedside test.
> **· Cardiac — PROLONGED QT, with the risk of torsades de pointes, and impaired myocardial contractility.**
> **· Chronic hypocalcaemia — cataracts, basal ganglia calcification, dry skin, brittle nails, and papilloedema.**

> [!danger] Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcaemia — anticipate it
> **Any patient after total thyroidectomy or parathyroid surgery needs calcium monitoring**, because the parathyroids may be bruised, devascularised or removed.
> **Symptoms typically appear within the first day or two**, and the patient should be asked specifically about **perioral tingling and hand cramps** rather than waiting for tetany.
> **Warn the patient before discharge what to look for and when to return** — this is a specific and examinable discharge instruction.

> [!tip] Treatment — and the reason calcitriol is used in hypoparathyroidism
> **Severe or symptomatic hypocalcaemia: intravenous calcium with cardiac monitoring.** **Calcium gluconate is preferred peripherally; calcium chloride is more concentrated but far more irritant and causes tissue necrosis on extravasation, so it is given centrally.**
> **Correct magnesium simultaneously.**
> **Maintenance: oral calcium plus vitamin D — and in HYPOPARATHYROIDISM specifically, ACTIVE vitamin D (calcitriol or alfacalcidol) is required, not cholecalciferol.**
> **The reason: the conversion of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to active calcitriol requires renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase, and that enzyme is PTH-DEPENDENT.** Without PTH, giving plain vitamin D does not produce active hormone. **The same applies in chronic kidney disease**, where the enzyme is lost with renal mass.
> `UNVERIFIED — all doses, infusion rates and monitoring.`

**Ix:** **Corrected or ionised calcium, MAGNESIUM, phosphate, PTH, UEC, ALP and 25-hydroxyvitamin D** (*why:* magnesium in particular, because without it the calcium will not correct; *what:* the pattern). **ECG** (*why:* prolonged QT with risk of torsades; *what:* QT interval). Lipase where pancreatitis is possible (*why:* a cause of hypocalcaemia and a severity marker within it; *what:* elevation). CK and phosphate where rhabdomyolysis or tumour lysis is suspected (*why:* hyperphosphataemia binding calcium; *what:* raised CK and phosphate). Coeliac serology and malabsorption workup where vitamin D deficiency is unexplained (*why:* malabsorption is a treatable underlying cause; *what:* coeliac serology).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
**Intravenous calcium with cardiac monitoring for tetany, seizures, laryngospasm or a markedly prolonged QT. Correct magnesium.**

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Oral calcium with **active** vitamin D in hypoparathyroidism, cholecalciferol in simple deficiency, and treatment of the underlying cause.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Monitoring of calcium, phosphate, renal function and **urinary calcium** in treated hypoparathyroidism — **because treatment increases urinary calcium excretion and risks nephrocalcinosis and stones**, so the target calcium is deliberately kept at the lower end of normal rather than fully normalised.

## 0.4 Parathyroid Disorders

> [!tip] Primary hyperparathyroidism
> **Autonomous PTH secretion — a single adenoma in the large majority, four-gland hyperplasia in a minority, and parathyroid carcinoma rarely.**
> **Associated with MEN1 and MEN2A**, so **a young patient, multi-gland disease, or a family history should prompt genetic consideration.** Cross-refer [[I4_Pituitary__Adrenal_and_Sex_Hormone]].
> **Most cases are now detected incidentally on biochemistry in an asymptomatic patient**, rather than presenting with symptomatic disease.
> **Complications that determine whether to operate: osteoporosis (characteristically affecting CORTICAL bone, so the DISTAL RADIUS is the site to measure — a site frequently omitted from a standard DXA), nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis, declining renal function, and neurocognitive symptoms.**

> [!danger] Localisation imaging is for surgical planning, not for diagnosis
> **The diagnosis of primary hyperparathyroidism is BIOCHEMICAL — hypercalcaemia with an inappropriately normal or raised PTH, after excluding FHH.**
> **Sestamibi scanning, ultrasound and 4D-CT are performed to guide a minimally invasive operation. A negative scan does NOT exclude the diagnosis**, and it does not mean the patient should not be operated on — it means the surgeon may need a bilateral exploration.
> **Ordering imaging to "confirm" hyperparathyroidism is a category error**, and a normal scan in a patient with clear biochemistry is a common source of inappropriate reassurance.

> [!tip] Who gets surgery
> **Parathyroidectomy is curative.** Indications are based on **age, the degree of hypercalcaemia, renal function and urinary calcium, the presence of stones, bone density, and symptoms** — with younger patients and those with complications favoured for surgery, and older asymptomatic patients with mild disease often monitored. `UNVERIFIED — the specific criteria.`
> **Cinacalcet** (a calcimimetic) lowers calcium where surgery is not appropriate, but does not improve bone density.
> **Post-operatively, anticipate hypocalcaemia** — both transient and from hungry bone syndrome. See 0.3.

> [!info] Secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism
> **· Secondary** — an APPROPRIATE PTH response to a low or normal calcium. **Chronic kidney disease (reduced calcitriol, phosphate retention) and vitamin D deficiency.** **PTH high, calcium low or normal.** Treat the cause.
> **· Tertiary** — after prolonged secondary stimulation the glands become AUTONOMOUS. **PTH high AND calcium high**, typically in long-standing chronic kidney disease or after transplantation.
> **The distinction is made by the calcium: appropriate response (low/normal calcium) versus autonomy (high calcium).**

> [!warning] Hypoparathyroidism
> Most commonly **post-surgical.** Also autoimmune, congenital (**DiGeorge syndrome**), infiltrative, and functional from magnesium depletion.
> **Low calcium, HIGH phosphate (because PTH normally promotes phosphate excretion), and low or inappropriately normal PTH.**
> **Pseudohypoparathyroidism** — end-organ resistance, so **PTH is HIGH with a low calcium**, and there may be characteristic skeletal features including short fourth metacarpals.

**Ix:** As per 0.2 and 0.3. **24-hour urinary calcium before surgical referral** (*why:* excludes FHH; *what:* low urinary calcium). **DXA including the DISTAL RADIUS** (*why:* primary hyperparathyroidism preferentially affects cortical bone and the radius may be the only abnormal site; *what:* cortical bone loss). Renal imaging (*why:* nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis are surgical indications and are often asymptomatic; *what:* stones, calcification). **Sestamibi, ultrasound or 4D-CT — only once the diagnosis is biochemically established and surgery is planned** (*why:* localisation for a targeted operation; *what:* adenoma location). Genetic assessment where young, multi-gland, or a family history (*why:* MEN syndromes have implications for the patient and family; *what:* pathogenic variant).

## 0.5 Metabolic Bone Disease

> [!tip] Osteomalacia and rickets — defective MINERALISATION
> **Osteoporosis is too little normal bone. Osteomalacia is normal quantity, poorly mineralised bone.** The distinction determines the treatment entirely.
> **Cause: vitamin D deficiency predominantly**, and also phosphate depletion and rare disorders of alkaline phosphatase.
> **Presentation: bone pain (often diffuse and mistaken for fibromyalgia), PROXIMAL MYOPATHY with a waddling gait and difficulty rising from a chair or climbing stairs, and fractures.** **Looser zones (pseudofractures)** on imaging.
> **The biochemistry: low or low-normal calcium, LOW PHOSPHATE, HIGH ALKALINE PHOSPHATASE, high PTH, and low 25-hydroxyvitamin D.** **The raised ALP with proximal weakness and bone pain is the pattern to recognise** — it is a treatable cause of weakness that gets attributed to age, deconditioning or fibromyalgia. Cross-refer [[B6_Oedema__Fatigue__Weakness_and_Undifferentiated_Presentations]] 0.6 and [[L3_Muscle_Symptoms_and_Widespread_Pain]].
> **Rickets** is the childhood equivalent, affecting the growth plates: **bowing of the legs, widened wrists and ankles, rachitic rosary, craniotabes, delayed fontanelle closure and growth failure.** Cross-refer [[M4_Growth_and_Development]].

> [!warning] Vitamin D deficiency in Australia — who is at risk
> Despite the climate: **people with darker skin · those who veil or otherwise cover for cultural or religious reasons · housebound, institutionalised and residential aged care residents · those who avoid sun for medical reasons or because of skin cancer risk · malabsorption (coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, bariatric surgery) · obesity (sequestration in adipose tissue) · anticonvulsants and other enzyme inducers · chronic kidney and liver disease · and exclusively breastfed infants of deficient mothers.**
> **There is a genuine tension between sun exposure for vitamin D and skin cancer prevention in Australia**, given the highest melanoma rates in the world — which is why **supplementation rather than deliberate sun exposure is the usual advice for at-risk groups.** `UNVERIFIED — current supplementation recommendations and target levels.`

> [!tip] Paget disease of bone
> **Disordered, accelerated bone remodelling producing structurally abnormal bone.**
> **Most cases are ASYMPTOMATIC and detected incidentally as an ISOLATED RAISED ALKALINE PHOSPHATASE with a NORMAL calcium and phosphate** — that combination in an older person should prompt the thought.
> When symptomatic: **bone pain, deformity (bowing of long bones, increasing skull size — "my hat doesn't fit"), pathological fracture, secondary osteoarthritis, and DEAFNESS from compression of the eighth nerve as the skull base remodels.** Rarely, high-output cardiac failure, and **osteosarcoma, which is rare but is the reason for investigating new severe pain in a Pagetic bone.**
> **Diagnosis: raised ALP, characteristic radiographic appearances, and a bone scan to define the extent.** **Confirm the ALP is of bone origin — a raised ALP with a normal GGT points away from the liver.** Cross-refer [[C3_Jaundice_and_Liver_Disease]] 0.1.
> Treated with **bisphosphonates** where symptomatic or at sites of complication risk.

> [!tip] Chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder
> A combined disturbance of **calcium, phosphate, PTH and vitamin D**, with **bone abnormality and vascular calcification.**
> **Mechanism: falling GFR → phosphate retention and reduced calcitriol production → hypocalcaemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism**, with FGF23 elevation early in the sequence.
> **The consequences that matter clinically are vascular — the calcium-phosphate product drives arterial and valvular calcification, and cardiovascular death far exceeds bone complications as the outcome.**
> **Calciphylaxis** — painful necrotic skin lesions from cutaneous arteriolar calcification, in dialysis patients, with high mortality. **It is misdiagnosed as cellulitis or vasculitis.** Cross-refer [[K2_Skin_and_Soft_Tissue_Infection]] 0.1 and [[H3_Acute_Kidney_Injury_and_CKD]].
> Management involves **phosphate binders, dietary phosphate restriction, active vitamin D analogues and calcimimetics**, under nephrology direction.

**Ix:** **Calcium, phosphate, ALP, PTH and 25-hydroxyvitamin D together** (*why:* the pattern distinguishes osteomalacia, hyperparathyroidism, Paget disease and renal bone disease; *what:* the combination). **GGT alongside ALP** (*why:* confirms a raised ALP is of bone rather than hepatic origin; *what:* normal GGT with raised ALP indicates bone). Radiographs (*why:* Looser zones in osteomalacia, and the characteristic lytic and sclerotic changes of Paget disease; *what:* the appearances). **Bone scan in Paget disease** (*why:* defines the extent and identifies asymptomatic sites at risk; *what:* focal increased uptake). DXA (*why:* coexisting osteoporosis; *what:* bone density — cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.6). Coeliac serology and malabsorption assessment (*why:* a treatable cause of vitamin D deficiency; *what:* positive serology). Renal function (*why:* CKD-MBD; *what:* eGFR).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Not usually required.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
**Vitamin D and calcium replacement for osteomalacia — with a dramatic response in the proximal myopathy**, which is one of the more satisfying treatments in medicine. Bisphosphonates for symptomatic Paget disease. Phosphate binders and specialist management in CKD-MBD.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Maintenance vitamin D, treatment of the underlying malabsorption, hearing assessment in skull-base Paget disease, and falls and fracture prevention. Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.5–0.6.

## 0.6 Phosphate and Magnesium

> [!danger] Hypophosphataemia — the causes that matter in hospital
> **· REFEEDING SYNDROME — the classic and most important. Reintroducing nutrition after prolonged starvation drives insulin release and a rapid intracellular shift of phosphate, potassium and magnesium.** Cross-refer [[C2_Nausea_and_Vomiting]] 0.7 and [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.3.
> **· Treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis** — insulin drives phosphate intracellularly.
> **· Alcohol use disorder** — through poor intake, magnesium depletion and renal losses.
> **· Respiratory alkalosis.**
> **· Hyperparathyroidism, Fanconi syndrome, and tumour-induced osteomalacia (FGF23-mediated).**
> **· Certain intravenous IRON preparations cause FGF23-mediated renal phosphate wasting and clinically significant hypophosphataemia** — an increasingly recognised and under-appreciated cause, relevant given how widely IV iron is now used.
> **The consequences are under-recognised: muscle weakness including DIAPHRAGMATIC weakness and FAILURE TO WEAN from ventilation · rhabdomyolysis · haemolysis · impaired myocardial contractility · confusion, seizures and coma.**
> **A patient who will not wean from a ventilator deserves a phosphate level.**

> [!tip] Hyperphosphataemia
> **· Chronic kidney disease — by far the commonest.**
> **· Tumour lysis syndrome and rhabdomyolysis** — massive cellular release.
> **· Hypoparathyroidism.**
> **· Phosphate-containing enemas and laxatives** — which can cause severe, occasionally fatal, hyperphosphataemia and hypocalcaemia, **particularly in older people and those with renal impairment**, and are a genuine iatrogenic hazard.
> Consequences: hypocalcaemia by precipitation, and **vascular and soft tissue calcification** with chronicity.

> [!danger] Hypomagnesaemia — the electrolyte that makes the others refractory
> **· Proton pump inhibitors — an under-recognised and increasingly common cause with long-term use, and it resolves on stopping the drug.**
> **· Diuretics (loop and thiazide) · alcohol · diarrhoea and malabsorption · refeeding · poorly controlled diabetes · and drugs — aminoglycosides, amphotericin, cisplatin, calcineurin inhibitors, and cetuximab.**
> **The critical clinical point: magnesium depletion causes REFRACTORY HYPOKALAEMIA and REFRACTORY HYPOCALCAEMIA.**
> **Low magnesium promotes renal potassium wasting and impairs both PTH secretion and PTH action — so potassium and calcium will not correct until the magnesium is replaced, no matter how much you give.**
> **Any potassium or calcium that will not correct despite adequate replacement should prompt a magnesium level.** This is among the most practically useful facts in electrolyte management and it is repeatedly overlooked while replacement is escalated fruitlessly.
> **Consequences: arrhythmia including TORSADES DE POINTES (cross-refer [[B3_Arrhythmia__Bradycardia_and_Cardiac_Devices]] 0.3), tetany, tremor, weakness, seizures and personality change.**

> [!warning] Hypermagnesaemia
> Almost always **renal impairment combined with a magnesium load** — magnesium-containing antacids and laxatives, or **therapeutic magnesium in obstetrics for pre-eclampsia and eclampsia.**
> **In obstetric magnesium therapy, LOSS OF DEEP TENDON REFLEXES is the early warning sign of toxicity and precedes respiratory depression and cardiac arrest — which is why reflexes are monitored during infusion.** **Calcium gluconate is the antidote.** Cross-refer [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]].
> Progression: loss of reflexes → hypotension and bradycardia → respiratory depression → cardiac arrest.

**Ix:** **Phosphate and magnesium measured alongside calcium and potassium** (*why:* they interact, and measuring one in isolation leads to fruitless replacement of another; *what:* the full electrolyte picture). **Magnesium in any refractory hypokalaemia or hypocalcaemia** (*why:* the replacement will not work until it is corrected; *what:* low magnesium). Renal function (*why:* determines both clearance and the safety of replacement; *what:* eGFR). **Urinary phosphate and magnesium** where the cause is unclear (*why:* **distinguishes renal wasting from gastrointestinal loss or redistribution**, which have different causes and management; *what:* fractional excretion). Medication review (*why:* PPIs, diuretics, IV iron and chemotherapy agents are the commonest reversible causes; *what:* culprit drug). ECG (*why:* torsades risk with hypomagnesaemia, and conduction effects with hypermagnesaemia; *what:* QT, conduction). **Deep tendon reflexes during therapeutic magnesium infusion** (*why:* the earliest clinical sign of toxicity; *what:* reflex loss).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Intravenous replacement for severe or symptomatic deficiency with cardiac monitoring. **Calcium gluconate for magnesium toxicity.** Stop the causative agent.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the cause. **Anticipate and prevent refeeding syndrome in at-risk patients rather than treating it after it occurs.**

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Review long-term PPI and diuretic use. Monitor in ongoing losses and in chronic kidney disease.
