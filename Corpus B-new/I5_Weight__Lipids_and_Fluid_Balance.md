## 0.1 Obesity

> [!warning] BMI is a population tool with real individual limitations
> **It does not distinguish fat from muscle**, so it misclassifies muscular individuals, and it **underestimates adiposity in older people** who have lost muscle.
> **Cut-offs differ by ethnicity: the thresholds for increased metabolic risk are LOWER in South and East Asian populations**, which matters in Australia's population — a person of South Asian background can have substantial metabolic risk at a BMI considered normal by European criteria. `UNVERIFIED — the ethnicity-specific thresholds.`
> **Central adiposity predicts metabolic and cardiovascular risk better than BMI**, so **waist circumference adds information that BMI does not** and is worth measuring.

> [!danger] Weight stigma is a clinical problem, not a courtesy issue
> **People with obesity report avoiding or delaying healthcare because of previous experiences of judgement**, and symptoms are attributed to weight without assessment — which is how serious diagnoses get missed in larger patients.
> **Practical points that change the encounter:**
> **· Ask permission before discussing weight** — "would it be alright if we talked about weight today?" — and accept a no.
> **· Use person-first, neutral language** — "a person with obesity" rather than "an obese person", "weight" rather than "fatness".
> **· Do not attribute a symptom to weight without evaluating it** as you would in anyone else.
> **· Ensure the practical environment is appropriate** — correctly sized blood pressure cuffs, gowns, chairs and equipment. Their absence communicates something.
> **This is directly examinable in an OSCE and it materially affects whether the person returns.**

> [!tip] It is not simply energy balance — look for contributors
> **· Drugs — a genuinely major and reversible contributor.** **Antipsychotics (olanzapine and clozapine most), sodium valproate, mirtazapine, tricyclics, lithium, insulin and sulfonylureas, corticosteroids, gabapentinoids, and some beta-blockers.** **Review the drug chart before intensifying lifestyle advice.**
> **· Endocrine** — hypothyroidism, Cushing syndrome, PCOS, hypothalamic damage.
> **· Obstructive sleep apnoea and sleep deprivation**, which are both consequence and cause.
> **· Reduced mobility and chronic pain.**
> **· Social determinants** — food cost and availability, housing, shift work, and time.
> **· Psychological** — depression, trauma, binge eating disorder. Cross-refer [[N8_Eating_Disorders]].

> [!info] Management, and what the evidence supports
> **· Lifestyle** — **adherence matters more than dietary composition**; any sustainable energy deficit works, and the best diet is the one the person can maintain. **Physical activity has health benefits independent of weight loss** and is essential for maintaining loss, though it is a weak tool for producing it.
> **· Realistic goals** — **5 to 10% weight loss produces meaningful improvement in blood pressure, glycaemia, lipids and sleep apnoea**, and framing that as the target is more useful and more achievable than a "normal BMI".
> **· Pharmacotherapy** — **GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual agonists have substantially changed what is achievable medically.** Also orlistat, phentermine, and naltrexone-bupropion.
> **The essential counselling point: weight is regained when the medication stops.** These are treatments for a chronic condition, not a course. Availability, cost and PBS status are significant practical barriers in Australia. `UNVERIFIED — agents, doses, and current PBS and supply status, which is changing.`
> **· Bariatric and metabolic surgery** — the most effective durable intervention, with **remission of type 2 diabetes in a substantial proportion.** `UNVERIFIED — BMI and comorbidity eligibility thresholds.`

> [!danger] Post-bariatric surgery — two things every doctor should know
> **1. LIFELONG nutritional supplementation and monitoring is required** — **vitamin B12, iron, folate, thiamine, calcium and vitamin D, and copper and zinc.** **Deficiencies present years later as anaemia, neuropathy, osteomalacia, and — in the case of thiamine — Wernicke encephalopathy, which occurs after bariatric surgery particularly with vomiting.** Cross-refer [[C2_Nausea_and_Vomiting]] 0.7 and [[D2_Altered_Consciousness_and_Cognition]] 0.5.
> **2. ABDOMINAL PAIN AFTER GASTRIC BYPASS IS AN INTERNAL HERNIA UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE.** It can cause closed-loop obstruction and bowel infarction, **CT is frequently normal or equivocal**, and the correct response to significant unexplained abdominal pain in a bypass patient is **urgent surgical review by the bariatric team, not observation.** This is a genuine cause of preventable death and is missed by clinicians unfamiliar with the anatomy.
> Also: **dumping syndrome, post-bariatric hypoglycaemia (late postprandial), increased alcohol sensitivity and risk of alcohol use disorder, and a recommendation to delay pregnancy for a period after surgery** because of the rapid weight loss and nutritional risk.

**S/Smx:** Assess the weight history, previous attempts, eating pattern, physical activity, sleep, mood, medications, and the complications below.

**Ix:** **HbA1c or fasting glucose, lipids, LFT, blood pressure** (*why:* the metabolic complications, several of which are asymptomatic; *what:* diabetes, dyslipidaemia, MASLD, hypertension). TFTs (*why:* hypothyroidism as a contributor, though it rarely explains substantial obesity; *what:* raised TSH). **Sleep study where symptoms suggest obstructive sleep apnoea** (*why:* very common, treatable, and it worsens metabolic control and cardiovascular risk; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index). Liver fibrosis assessment where MASLD is present (*why:* fibrosis stage determines prognosis; *what:* elastography or fibrosis score — cross-refer [[C3_Jaundice_and_Liver_Disease]] 0.5). Screening for Cushing syndrome **only where there are discriminating features** (*why:* untargeted testing generates false positives, and obesity itself is a pseudo-Cushing state; *what:* proximal myopathy, thin skin, bruising, wide purple striae — cross-refer [[I4_Pituitary__Adrenal_and_Sex_Hormone]] 0.4).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Not applicable.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Multicomponent lifestyle intervention, review of obesogenic medications, pharmacotherapy, and surgical referral where indicated.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Treat obesity as a chronic relapsing condition requiring ongoing management, not a one-off intervention.** Lifelong nutritional monitoring after surgery. Address the complications independently.

## 0.2 Unintentional Weight Loss

> [!danger] Unintentional weight loss is always significant
> `UNVERIFIED — the percentage and timeframe that define clinically significant loss.`
> **First, confirm it is real** — documented weights, or a change in clothing or belt size, rather than an impression. **Self-reported weight loss without corroboration is unreliable in both directions.**

> [!tip] The single most useful discriminating question: what has happened to the appetite?
> **· Weight loss with REDUCED appetite** — malignancy, chronic infection, chronic organ failure, depression, medications, dementia, social factors.
> **· Weight loss with PRESERVED OR INCREASED appetite** — **a much shorter list: thyrotoxicosis, uncontrolled diabetes, malabsorption (coeliac disease, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, inflammatory bowel disease), and phaeochromocytoma.**
> That one question narrows the differential immediately, and it is frequently not asked.

> [!warning] The differential
> **· Malignancy** — accounts for a substantial proportion, and may be occult.
> **· Gastrointestinal** — coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic pancreatitis, peptic ulcer disease, dysphagia, chronic mesenteric ischaemia (**postprandial pain and food fear**).
> **· Endocrine** — thyrotoxicosis (**including apathetic thyrotoxicosis in the elderly** — cross-refer [[I1_Thyroid_Disease]] 0.3), diabetes, adrenal insufficiency, phaeochromocytoma.
> **· Infection** — tuberculosis, HIV, infective endocarditis, chronic abscess. Cross-refer [[K1_Fever_Workup]] 0.3.
> **· Psychiatric — DEPRESSION is a leading and treatable cause, particularly in older people**, alongside eating disorders and substance use.
> **· Chronic organ failure** — cardiac cachexia, COPD, chronic kidney disease, cirrhosis.
> **· Neurological** — dementia, Parkinson disease, motor neurone disease, stroke — largely through **dysphagia and reduced intake.**
> **· Medications** — a long list, and worth reviewing.
> **· SOCIAL AND ORAL** — **poverty, isolation, bereavement, inability to shop or cook, and poor dentition or ill-fitting dentures.** **These are common, correctable and consistently under-considered**, particularly in older people. Cross-refer [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.3.

**Ix:** **Targeted history and full examination first, including the mouth and dentition, and a mood assessment** (*why:* a large proportion of causes are identified clinically, and untargeted imaging in a patient with an obvious social or psychiatric cause is unhelpful; *what:* the likely cause). **FBC, ESR and CRP, UEC, LFT, calcium, glucose or HbA1c, TFTs, and coeliac serology** (*why:* the baseline that covers the metabolic, inflammatory, endocrine and malabsorptive causes; *what:* the abnormality). **HIV testing** (*why:* under-offered, treatable, and a recognised cause; *what:* positive). CXR and urinalysis (*why:* malignancy, tuberculosis, infection; *what:* findings). **Age-appropriate cancer screening brought up to date, and faecal occult blood or direct colonic investigation where indicated** (*why:* occult gastrointestinal malignancy; *what:* neoplasia — cross-refer [[C4_Gastrointestinal_Bleeding]] 0.5). **Directed imaging based on findings rather than a whole-body scan** (*why:* untargeted imaging has low yield and a high incidentaloma rate; *what:* the suspected lesion).

> [!tip] When the workup is negative
> **A meaningful proportion of unintentional weight loss never receives a diagnosis**, and a negative initial evaluation in a patient without red flags is reasonably reassuring.
> **The correct response is structured follow-up with repeat weighing and reassessment over months, rather than escalating imaging** — a cancer that was occult at presentation usually declares itself within that period, and repeated scanning in the interim finds incidentalomas rather than answers.

Rarely required.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Nutritional support and dietitian involvement alongside investigating the cause** — the malnutrition itself worsens outcomes and should be treated in parallel, not after the diagnosis. Cross-refer [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.3.

## 0.3 Lipid Disorders

**A/P:** **LDL cholesterol is causally related to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease** — this is established by genetic, epidemiological and randomised evidence, and it underpins the entire treatment strategy: **lowering LDL reduces events, and the benefit is proportional to the absolute reduction achieved.**
**In hypertriglyceridaemia and diabetes, LDL underestimates risk**, and **non-HDL cholesterol or apolipoprotein B** better capture the total atherogenic particle burden.

> [!danger] Familial hypercholesterolaemia — common, dangerous, and massively underdiagnosed
> **An autosomal dominant disorder of LDL receptor function, affecting roughly 1 in 250 people — making it one of the commonest serious inherited conditions — and the great majority of affected Australians are undiagnosed.**
> **Untreated, it causes markedly elevated LDL from birth and premature coronary disease**, sometimes in the fourth or fifth decade.
> **Clues: a very high LDL · a personal or family history of PREMATURE cardiovascular disease (under about 55 in men, 60 in women) · TENDON XANTHOMATA (Achilles, extensor tendons of the hands — feel for them, they are specific) · corneal arcus under about 45 · and xanthelasma.**
> **Diagnosis uses clinical criteria such as the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network score, with genetic testing available.** `UNVERIFIED — the criteria and LDL thresholds.`
> **CASCADE SCREENING OF FIRST-DEGREE RELATIVES IS THE HIGHEST-VALUE ACTION** — each first-degree relative has a one-in-two chance of being affected, and identifying them young allows treatment before disease develops. **Diagnosing one index case should generate a family.**

> [!warning] Always exclude secondary causes before labelling a primary lipid disorder
> **· HYPOTHYROIDISM — check the TSH in every dyslipidaemia. It is common, it causes marked hypercholesterolaemia, it resolves with thyroxine, and treating the lipids without treating the thyroid is both futile and increases statin myopathy risk.**
> **· Diabetes and insulin resistance** — typically a raised triglyceride with low HDL.
> **· Nephrotic syndrome** — marked hypercholesterolaemia.
> **· Chronic kidney disease · cholestatic liver disease · alcohol (particularly raising triglycerides) · obesity · pregnancy.**
> **· Drugs — thiazides, corticosteroids, antipsychotics, retinoids (isotretinoin markedly raises triglycerides), immunosuppressants, oestrogens, and some antiretrovirals.**

> [!danger] Very high triglycerides — the priority is preventing pancreatitis, not cardiovascular disease
> Above a threshold, hypertriglyceridaemia causes **acute pancreatitis**, and that becomes the immediate concern.
> **Management: alcohol abstinence, strict dietary fat restriction, glycaemic control, fibrates and omega-3 fatty acids, and cessation of contributing drugs.** Cross-refer [[C7_Pancreatobiliary_Disease]] 0.1.
> **And remember the assay trap: in hypertriglyceridaemia-induced pancreatitis, the AMYLASE may be falsely NORMAL** because the lipaemic serum interferes — so measure lipase and triglycerides. `UNVERIFIED — the triglyceride threshold at which pancreatitis risk becomes significant.`

> [!tip] Treatment — driven by absolute risk, not by the number alone
> Australian practice uses **absolute cardiovascular risk assessment** rather than treating a lipid threshold in isolation — cross-refer [[B2_Hypertension_Spectrum]] 0.1 and [[GER3_Preventive_and_Occupational_Health]].
> **Exceptions treated regardless of calculated risk: established cardiovascular disease, familial hypercholesterolaemia, diabetes with certain features, chronic kidney disease, and very high individual risk factor levels.**
> **· Statins — first-line**, with high-intensity therapy for established disease.
> **· Ezetimibe** — added where statin alone is insufficient or not tolerated.
> **· PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran** — for familial hypercholesterolaemia and very high risk, with restricted access.
> **· Fibrates and omega-3** — for hypertriglyceridaemia.
> `UNVERIFIED — agents, intensities, targets and PBS authority criteria.`

> [!warning] Statin-associated muscle symptoms — mostly not caused by the statin
> **Muscle symptoms are reported commonly, but blinded rechallenge studies show that the majority are NOT attributable to the drug — a substantial nocebo effect.**
> **This matters because statin discontinuation after a myocardial infarction increases mortality**, and patients are frequently taken off them permanently on the basis of aches that were not caused by them.
> **A reasonable approach: check CK and TSH, stop temporarily to see if symptoms resolve, then REchallenge — with the same statin, a lower dose, an alternate-day regimen, or a different statin.** Most patients tolerate something.
> **Genuine myopathy with a markedly raised CK, and rhabdomyolysis, are rare but real** — and **statin-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy CONTINUES to progress after the drug is stopped and requires immunosuppression.** Cross-refer [[D4_Weakness__Neuropathy_and_Radiculopathy]] 0.6.
> **Check the TSH** — untreated hypothyroidism substantially increases statin myopathy risk.

**Ix:** **Fasting or non-fasting lipid profile** (*why:* non-fasting is acceptable for screening in most circumstances, which improves uptake; *what:* total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides, non-HDL). **TSH, glucose or HbA1c, UEC, LFT, and urine protein** (*why:* the secondary causes, all of which change management; *what:* hypothyroidism, diabetes, renal disease, cholestasis). **Absolute cardiovascular risk calculation** (*why:* it, not the cholesterol number, determines treatment in primary prevention; *what:* risk category). **Family history of premature cardiovascular disease, and examination for tendon xanthomata and corneal arcus** (*why:* identifies familial hypercholesterolaemia, which is otherwise missed; *what:* the clinical criteria). Lipoprotein(a) in selected patients (*why:* an independent genetically determined risk factor, measured once, that refines risk in those with premature or familial disease; *what:* elevation). CK where muscle symptoms (*why:* distinguishes true myopathy from myalgia; *what:* elevation).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Only for hypertriglyceridaemic pancreatitis.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Lifestyle plus lipid-lowering therapy determined by absolute risk, with treatment of secondary causes first.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Adherence support — persistence with statins is poor and it is the main determinant of benefit.** **Cascade screening of relatives in familial hypercholesterolaemia.**

## 0.4 Metabolic Syndrome and Integrated Risk

> [!tip] The clustering, and a caveat about its usefulness
> **Central obesity plus a combination of raised triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose**, unified by **insulin resistance.** `UNVERIFIED — the diagnostic criteria, which differ between definitions.`
> **The clustering is real and predicts type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease — but the label itself adds limited value**, because **each component requires treatment on its own merits regardless of whether the patient meets the criteria.** A patient with hypertension and dyslipidaemia is treated for both whether or not they qualify as having "metabolic syndrome".
> **Its clinical utility is as a prompt: finding one component should trigger assessment for the others**, and it identifies a phenotype that responds particularly well to weight loss and physical activity.

> [!warning] Look for the associated conditions that are not in the definition
> **· Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)** — extremely common in this group, and can progress to fibrosis and cirrhosis. Cross-refer [[C3_Jaundice_and_Liver_Disease]] 0.5.
> **· Obstructive sleep apnoea** — common, bidirectional, and treatable.
> **· Polycystic ovary syndrome** — cross-refer [[I4_Pituitary__Adrenal_and_Sex_Hormone]] 0.6.
> **· Hyperuricaemia and gout.**
> **· Chronic kidney disease.**

**Ix:** Waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting lipids and glucose or HbA1c (*why:* the components; *what:* the cluster). LFT and liver fibrosis assessment (*why:* MASLD; *what:* steatosis, fibrosis). Sleep study where indicated (*why:* OSA; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index). **Absolute cardiovascular risk calculation** (*why:* integrates the components into a treatment decision; *what:* risk category).

### 0.4.1–0.4.3 Mx
As per the individual components, with weight loss and physical activity addressing several simultaneously.

## 0.5 Sodium and Water Balance

> [!danger] Hyponatraemia is a water problem, not a salt problem
> **Serum sodium reflects the ratio of sodium to water, not the total body sodium.** A patient can be hyponatraemic while total body sodium is high (heart failure), normal (SIADH) or low (diuretics, vomiting).
> **This is why the treatment is determined by volume status and by the cause, not by "giving salt".**

> [!tip] The four-step approach
> **1. Is it TRUE hyponatraemia?**
> **· Pseudohyponatraemia** — a laboratory artefact from **severe hyperlipidaemia or paraproteinaemia** displacing plasma water. **Serum osmolality is NORMAL.**
> **· Hypertonic (translocational) hyponatraemia** — **hyperglycaemia** or mannitol drawing water out of cells. **Serum osmolality is HIGH.** **In marked hyperglycaemia, the sodium must be corrected for the glucose before it is interpreted**, and it will rise as the glucose is treated.
> **2. Measure SERUM OSMOLALITY** — this distinguishes the above from true hypotonic hyponatraemia.
> **3. Assess VOLUME STATUS clinically** — hypovolaemic, euvolaemic or hypervolaemic. This is the step that is done poorly and it drives the whole diagnosis.
> **4. Measure URINE SODIUM and URINE OSMOLALITY** — which refine what the clinical assessment cannot.

> [!info] The differential by volume status
> **HYPOVOLAEMIC** — total body water and sodium both reduced, sodium more so.
> **· Renal losses (urine sodium HIGH): diuretics — especially THIAZIDES, which are the commonest drug cause · adrenal insufficiency · cerebral salt wasting · osmotic diuresis.**
> **· Extrarenal losses (urine sodium LOW, as the kidney appropriately conserves): vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, third-spacing, pancreatitis.**
> **EUVOLAEMIC**
> **· SIADH — the commonest, and a diagnosis of exclusion requiring euvolaemia, low serum osmolality, INAPPROPRIATELY CONCENTRATED urine, urine sodium not low, and normal thyroid and adrenal function.** Causes: **malignancy (classically small cell lung carcinoma) · CNS disease · pulmonary disease · drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, antipsychotics, PPIs, chemotherapy) · pain, nausea and the post-operative state**, which are extremely common and often overlooked.
> **· Hypothyroidism and secondary adrenal insufficiency** — **which is why TSH and cortisol are part of the hyponatraemia workup, not an afterthought.**
> **· Primary polydipsia and low solute intake ("beer potomania", "tea and toast")** — where urine osmolality is LOW because the kidney is appropriately excreting water.
> **HYPERVOLAEMIC** — total body water increased more than sodium.
> **· Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, advanced chronic kidney disease.** Urine sodium is low in the first three (avid retention) and higher in renal failure.

> [!danger] The correction rate is the dangerous part, and it cuts both ways
> **· ACUTE hyponatraemia (developing over less than about 48 hours) causes CEREBRAL OEDEMA** — headache, vomiting, confusion, seizures, respiratory arrest — because the brain has not had time to adapt. **This is the immediate threat.**
> **· CHRONIC hyponatraemia is better tolerated because the brain extrudes osmolytes to adapt — but that adaptation is exactly why RAPID CORRECTION causes OSMOTIC DEMYELINATION SYNDROME**, with delayed, often irreversible, and sometimes devastating neurological injury (quadriparesis, dysarthria, dysphagia, locked-in syndrome) appearing days after the sodium was "successfully" corrected.
> **`UNVERIFIED — the maximum permitted rate of sodium rise per 24 hours, and the more conservative limit in high-risk patients, are DELIBERATELY OMITTED from this file. Obtain them from eTG and your health network's protocol. This is the single figure in electrolyte medicine least suitable for recall.`**
> **Risk factors for osmotic demyelination: chronicity, sodium very low at presentation, alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, liver disease, hypokalaemia, and advanced age.**
> **Severe symptoms — seizures or coma — warrant hypertonic saline regardless of chronicity**, because cerebral oedema will kill sooner than demyelination will. But the total rise over 24 hours must still be limited.
> **Watch for AUTOCORRECTION.** When the cause is removed — desmopressin stopped, hypovolaemia corrected, cortisol replaced — **ADH switches off and a large water diuresis follows, overshooting the target rapidly.** Monitor the sodium frequently, and be prepared to give free water or desmopressin to slow it down.

> [!warning] Hypernatraemia is nearly always a problem of ACCESS TO WATER
> **Hypernatraemia means a water deficit.** In a conscious person with an intact thirst mechanism and access to fluid, it is almost impossible to become hypernatraemic — the thirst is overwhelming.
> **So hypernatraemia essentially always means: an infant, an older or cognitively impaired person who cannot ask for or reach a drink, an intubated or unconscious patient, or someone with diabetes insipidus or an impaired thirst mechanism.**
> **In an aged care or hospital setting, hypernatraemia is frequently a marker of inadequate care** — a person who was not offered enough to drink — and it should prompt that reflection rather than only a fluid prescription.
> **Correct slowly** — rapid correction of chronic hypernatraemia causes cerebral oedema. `UNVERIFIED — the correction rate.`

**Ix:** **Serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium — taken together and BEFORE giving fluids** (*why:* fluid administration changes all three and makes the results uninterpretable, which is why the samples must be collected at presentation; *what:* the pattern that classifies the hyponatraemia). **Clinical volume assessment** (*why:* it determines the entire management; *what:* hypo-, eu- or hypervolaemia). **TSH and morning cortisol** (*why:* hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency both cause hyponatraemia, both are treatable, and both are missed if not specifically sought — **and SIADH cannot be diagnosed until they are excluded**; *what:* abnormal results). Glucose and lipids (*why:* the causes of pseudo- and hypertonic hyponatraemia; *what:* hyperglycaemia, lipaemia). **Full medication review** (*why:* thiazides, SSRIs, carbamazepine, PPIs and antipsychotics are among the commonest causes and are reversible; *what:* culprit drug). CXR and imaging where SIADH is confirmed and unexplained (*why:* **small cell lung carcinoma and CNS pathology are important causes of SIADH**; *what:* malignancy). **Frequent repeat sodium during correction** (*why:* both under- and over-correction cause harm, and autocorrection can be rapid; *what:* rate of rise).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
**Hypertonic saline for seizures or coma.** Otherwise, treat the cause and the volume state — **fluid restriction in SIADH, saline in hypovolaemia, diuresis and treatment of the underlying disease in hypervolaemia.** **Monitor the sodium frequently.**

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Stop the offending drug. Treat the malignancy or endocrine cause.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Review of diuretics and other contributing drugs, and — in recurrent hyponatraemia — fluid advice and monitoring.

## 0.6 Potassium Disorders

> [!danger] Hyperkalaemia — treat the ECG, not the number
> **Causes: renal impairment (the commonest) · DRUGS — ACE inhibitors and ARBs, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, NSAIDs, TRIMETHOPRIM, heparin, beta-blockers, digoxin, calcineurin inhibitors · acidosis · tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, haemolysis, burns) · adrenal insufficiency · stored blood transfusion.**
> **The drug combination that causes it repeatedly: an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist plus an NSAID, in a patient with declining renal function** — and trimethoprim added for a urinary infection is a classic tipping point.

> [!warning] Pseudohyperkalaemia — exclude it in a well patient with a normal ECG
> **A haemolysed sample · prolonged tourniquet time or fist clenching · delayed sample processing · marked thrombocytosis or leucocytosis.**
> **In a well patient with no ECG changes and no explanation, repeat the sample properly before treating.**
> **But if there are ECG changes, treat immediately and repeat afterwards** — the risk of waiting exceeds the risk of treating a spurious result.

> [!tip] The ECG progression
> **Peaked (tall, narrow, symmetrical) T waves → flattening and loss of P waves with PR prolongation → widening QRS → sine wave → asystole or ventricular fibrillation.**
> **The ECG changes correlate poorly with the absolute level and better with the RATE of rise**, so a chronically dialysed patient may tolerate a level that would arrest someone else. **The ECG determines urgency, not the number.**

> [!info] Treatment — three separate jobs, and calcium does not do the second or third
> **1. STABILISE the myocardium — intravenous CALCIUM (gluconate or chloride).** **It does NOT lower the potassium at all** — it raises the threshold potential and buys time. **Its effect is short-lived and it may need repeating.** Give it whenever there are ECG changes.
> **2. SHIFT potassium into cells — INSULIN with DEXTROSE**, and **nebulised salbutamol** as an adjunct. **Monitor for rebound hypoglycaemia after insulin-dextrose, which is common and is a recognised iatrogenic harm — check the glucose afterwards.** Sodium bicarbonate has a limited role, mainly in acidosis.
> **3. REMOVE potassium from the body — the only step that actually lowers total body potassium.** Potassium binders, diuretics if the patient is fluid-replete with adequate renal function, and **dialysis**, which is definitive.
> **The error to avoid: giving calcium and insulin-dextrose, seeing the potassium fall, and stopping there. The potassium is still in the body and will re-emerge.** Removal, or correction of the cause, is required.
> `UNVERIFIED — all agents, doses and the potassium thresholds triggering each step.`

> [!danger] Hypokalaemia — and the reason it will not correct
> **Causes: gastrointestinal loss (vomiting, diarrhoea, high-output stoma, laxative misuse) · renal loss (DIURETICS, hyperaldosteronism, Cushing syndrome, renal tubular acidosis, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, amphotericin) · intracellular SHIFT (insulin, beta-agonists including salbutamol nebulisers, ALKALOSIS, refeeding, periodic paralysis) · and poor intake.**
> **CHECK THE MAGNESIUM. Refractory hypokalaemia is hypomagnesaemia until proven otherwise** — magnesium depletion causes renal potassium wasting that cannot be overcome by potassium replacement alone. Cross-refer [[I3_Calcium__Parathyroid_and_Bone]] 0.6.
> **ECG: flattened T waves, ST depression, prominent U waves, and a prolonged QT with the risk of TORSADES DE POINTES** — cross-refer [[B3_Arrhythmia__Bradycardia_and_Cardiac_Devices]] 0.3. **Hypokalaemia also potentiates digoxin toxicity.**
> **Replacement: oral where possible.** Intravenous potassium has **strict rate limits, requires cardiac monitoring above a certain concentration, and concentrated solutions must be given centrally** — **rapid intravenous potassium causes cardiac arrest, and it is a recognised cause of inpatient death.** `UNVERIFIED — all concentrations, rates and monitoring requirements.`

**Ix:** **Repeat sample with proper technique in an unexplained abnormal result in a well patient** (*why:* pseudohyperkalaemia is common; *what:* true value). **ECG immediately in hyperkalaemia** (*why:* it determines urgency and is faster than the laboratory; *what:* peaked T waves, QRS widening). UEC and eGFR (*why:* renal impairment is the commonest cause and determines management; *what:* renal function). **MAGNESIUM** (*why:* refractory hypokalaemia; *what:* low magnesium). VBG (*why:* acid-base disturbance drives transcellular shift, and gives a rapid potassium; *what:* pH, potassium). CK (*why:* rhabdomyolysis; *what:* elevation). **Full medication review** (*why:* drugs are the commonest reversible cause in both directions; *what:* culprit). **Morning cortisol and aldosterone-renin ratio where unexplained** (*why:* adrenal insufficiency causes hyperkalaemia and primary aldosteronism causes hypokalaemia, and both are diagnosable and treatable; *what:* the endocrine cause — cross-refer [[I4_Pituitary__Adrenal_and_Sex_Hormone]] 0.3, 0.5). **Urine potassium** where the cause is unclear (*why:* **distinguishes renal wasting from gastrointestinal loss or shift**, which have entirely different differentials; *what:* fractional excretion).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
**Hyperkalaemia with ECG changes: calcium, then insulin-dextrose and salbutamol, then removal.** Check glucose after insulin. **Hypokalaemia with arrhythmia: replace with cardiac monitoring, and correct magnesium.**

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the cause and remove the offending drugs.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Review the combination of renin-angiotensin blockade, mineralocorticoid antagonists, NSAIDs and trimethoprim in patients with renal impairment**, and monitor potassium after any change. Dietary advice where recurrent.
