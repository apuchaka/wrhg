## 0.1 Definition, Classification and Pathophysiology

**D:** A clinical syndrome of symptoms and signs caused by a structural or functional cardiac abnormality, resulting in **reduced cardiac output and/or elevated intracardiac pressures** at rest or during stress.

> [!tip] Classification by ejection fraction — because it determines the treatment
> **· HFrEF — heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.** The group with the strongest evidence base and the largest treatment effect.
> **· HFmrEF — mildly reduced ejection fraction.** An intermediate category; increasingly treated like HFrEF.
> **· HFpEF — preserved ejection fraction.** Requires objective evidence of diastolic dysfunction or raised filling pressures — a normal EF alone is not enough, and symptoms with a normal echo are not automatically HFpEF.
> `UNVERIFIED — the EF cut-offs defining each category.`
>
> **Also classified by:**
> **· NYHA class I–IV** — current symptom severity, which **fluctuates** and is reassessed at every visit.
> **· ACC/AHA stages A–D** — structural progression, which **only moves in one direction.** A patient who improves from NYHA III to NYHA I remains in the same structural stage.

> [!info] The neurohormonal model — and why it explains the whole treatment strategy
> An index injury reduces cardiac output → the body responds as though to haemorrhage → **activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, the sympathetic nervous system, and vasopressin.**
> **These responses are adaptive acutely and maladaptive chronically:** salt and water retention increases preload and congestion; vasoconstriction increases afterload and further reduces output; and chronic catecholamine and aldosterone exposure drives **adverse ventricular remodelling — myocyte hypertrophy, apoptosis and interstitial fibrosis** — which worsens function further, in a self-reinforcing spiral.
>
> **This single concept explains the two most counterintuitive facts in heart failure:**
> **1. Beta-blockers improve survival despite being negative inotropes**, because blocking chronic sympathetic drive prevents remodelling — even though they may transiently worsen symptoms on initiation.
> **2. Inotropes make people feel better and die sooner.** Increasing contractility increases myocardial oxygen demand and arrhythmia without addressing the underlying process.
> **Every disease-modifying drug in heart failure blocks a neurohormonal pathway. Diuretics, which do not, relieve symptoms without improving survival.**

> [!tip] Aetiology — always look for a cause, because several are reversible
> **· Ischaemic heart disease — the commonest cause in Australia.**
> **· Hypertension.**
> **· Valvular disease** — cross-refer [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.1.
> **· Arrhythmia** — **tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy from persistent uncontrolled AF or another tachyarrhythmia is REVERSIBLE with rate or rhythm control**, and is missed when the AF is assumed to be a consequence rather than the cause.
> **· Alcohol** — dose-related, and **substantially reversible with abstinence.**
> **· Cardiotoxic drugs** — anthracyclines, trastuzumab, **immune checkpoint inhibitors (myocarditis — cross-refer [[A5_Toxicology_II_-_Poisoned_Patient__ADRs_and_Immunotherapy]] 0.7)**, and stimulants including methamphetamine, which is an increasingly recognised cause of cardiomyopathy in younger Australians.
> **· Myocarditis** — viral, autoimmune.
> **· Infiltrative** — **cardiac amyloidosis (increasingly recognised, and ATTR amyloidosis is now treatable, which makes diagnosing it worthwhile)**, sarcoidosis, haemochromatosis.
> **· Peripartum cardiomyopathy** — cross-refer [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.3.
> **· Familial dilated cardiomyopathy** — take a family history and consider screening relatives.
> **· Rheumatic heart disease** — with disproportionate burden in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.10 and [[AU1_Australian_Health_Context_and_ATSI_Health]].
> **· High-output failure** — **severe anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, arteriovenous fistula, thiamine deficiency (beriberi), Paget disease.** Treat the cause, not the heart.

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, ankle swelling, abdominal distension, early satiety, nocturia, and weight gain. Signs: raised JVP, displaced apex, third heart sound, basal crackles, peripheral or sacral oedema, hepatomegaly, ascites, and a narrow pulse pressure with cool peripheries in low-output states.

**Ix:** See 0.2.

## 0.2 Diagnosis and Assessment

> [!warning] Individual symptoms and signs perform poorly — the diagnosis needs objective evidence
> **Orthopnoea, PND and a raised JVP are relatively specific but insensitive. Ankle oedema and basal crackles are neither.** Crackles in an older patient are frequently atelectasis or lung disease, and ankle oedema is more often venous, drug-induced or dependent — cross-refer [[B6_Oedema__Fatigue__Weakness_and_Undifferentiated_Presentations]] 0.2.
> **The diagnosis of heart failure requires objective cardiac assessment**, and labelling a breathless oedematous patient with "heart failure" without an echocardiogram commits them to lifelong therapy that may be inappropriate.

> [!tip] Natriuretic peptides — use them to rule OUT
> **BNP and NT-proBNP have high negative predictive value.** A low level in an untreated patient makes heart failure unlikely and should redirect the workup.
> **A raised level is far less specific.** It rises with **age, atrial fibrillation, renal impairment, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, right heart strain, and critical illness generally.**
> **And it is LOWERED by obesity**, which is a genuine pitfall — an obese patient with heart failure may have a deceptively low level.
> `UNVERIFIED — the rule-out thresholds, which differ between BNP and NT-proBNP, between acute and non-acute presentations, and by age.`

> [!tip] Echocardiography is the essential test
> It provides: **ejection fraction, chamber dimensions, regional wall motion (suggesting ischaemic aetiology), valvular function, diastolic function and estimated filling pressures, right ventricular function and pulmonary pressures, and pericardial assessment.**
> **A completely normal 12-lead ECG makes HFrEF unlikely** — a useful and cheap negative.

> [!warning] Then investigate the cause — this step is frequently skipped
> Establishing "heart failure" and starting the drugs without determining **why** misses reversible and specifically treatable causes.
> **· Ischaemia** — the commonest cause, and revascularisation may be indicated. Functional testing, CT coronary angiography, or invasive angiography.
> **· Iron deficiency** — **very common in heart failure, and it worsens symptoms and exercise capacity independently of anaemia.** **Intravenous iron improves symptoms and quality of life in iron-deficient HFrEF even when the haemoglobin is normal**, so **ferritin and transferrin saturation should be checked in everyone** — and the thresholds used in heart failure are different from those used for anaemia generally.
> **· Thyroid disease · diabetes · alcohol · sleep apnoea.**
> **· Cardiac MRI** — increasingly central for aetiology, distinguishing ischaemic from non-ischaemic scar and identifying myocarditis, infiltration and amyloid.
> **· Cardiac amyloidosis** — suspect with **left ventricular hypertrophy out of proportion to blood pressure, low ECG voltages despite thick walls (a striking discordance), bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome preceding the cardiac presentation by years, spinal stenosis, and intolerance of standard heart failure medications.** **Bone scintigraphy identifies ATTR amyloidosis non-invasively, and treatment now exists** — which is why this diagnosis has moved from academic to actionable.

**Ix:** **Echocardiography** (*why:* the essential test — it establishes the diagnosis, categorises by EF, and identifies aetiology; *what:* EF, chamber size, wall motion, valves, diastolic parameters, RV function). **BNP or NT-proBNP** (*why:* rule-out value in the undifferentiated breathless patient; *what:* level, interpreted with the caveats above). ECG (*why:* prior infarction, LVH, bundle branch block (which affects device eligibility), AF, and low voltages suggesting amyloid; *what:* the trace). CXR (*why:* congestion, cardiomegaly, and alternative pulmonary causes of breathlessness; *what:* upper lobe diversion, Kerley B lines, effusions). **FBC and iron studies including ferritin and transferrin saturation** (*why:* anaemia and iron deficiency are both common, both worsen symptoms, and iron deficiency is specifically treatable in HF; *what:* anaemia, low ferritin or transferrin saturation). UEC, eGFR and electrolytes (*why:* baseline before RAAS blockade, and cardiorenal interaction; *what:* renal function, potassium, sodium). LFT (*why:* hepatic congestion; *what:* congestive pattern). TFTs (*why:* both hypo- and hyperthyroidism cause or worsen heart failure; *what:* abnormal TSH). HbA1c and lipids (*why:* risk factor management; *what:* glycaemia, lipids). **Assessment for ischaemia** (*why:* the commonest cause and potentially revascularisable; *what:* inducible ischaemia or coronary disease). **Cardiac MRI in non-ischaemic or unexplained cardiomyopathy** (*why:* identifies myocarditis, infiltration, amyloid and scar pattern, which changes management and prognosis; *what:* late gadolinium enhancement pattern). Bone scintigraphy where ATTR amyloid is suspected (*why:* non-invasive diagnosis of a now-treatable condition; *what:* cardiac tracer uptake). Sleep study where indicated (*why:* sleep-disordered breathing is common in HF and treating it improves symptoms; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index). Genetic testing and family screening in dilated cardiomyopathy (*why:* relatives may be affected and screenable; *what:* pathogenic variant).

## 0.3 HFrEF Management — The Four Pillars

> [!danger] The modern approach: four drug classes, started early, titrated in parallel
> **Each of the four independently reduces mortality, and their benefits are additive.** The older approach — start one, titrate to maximum over months, then add the next — leaves patients unprotected for a long period.
> **Current practice is to initiate all four at low dose as early as tolerated, then uptitrate**, because the mortality benefit appears within weeks and a patient on four low doses does better than one on a single maximal dose.
>
> **1. ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan), or an ACE inhibitor / ARB** if ARNI is not available or tolerated.
> **2. Beta-blocker** — **only the evidence-based agents: bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol extended-release, nebivolol.** **This is a class where the specific agent matters — do not substitute another beta-blocker and assume equivalent benefit.**
> **3. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist** — spironolactone or eplerenone.
> **4. SGLT2 inhibitor** — dapagliflozin or empagliflozin. **The benefit is independent of whether the patient has diabetes**, which surprises people, and it is now a core heart failure drug rather than a diabetes drug.
> `UNVERIFIED — all agents, starting doses, target doses, titration intervals and PBS authority criteria.`

> [!warning] Diuretics relieve symptoms and do not prolong life
> **Loop diuretics treat congestion. They have no mortality benefit and they activate the renin-angiotensin system.**
> **Use the lowest dose that maintains euvolaemia**, and **reduce or stop them if the patient becomes dry** — a patient over-diuresed into hypovolaemia will not tolerate the drugs that actually improve survival, and diuretic dose is frequently escalated and never revisited.
> Diuretic dose is titrated to weight and symptoms, ideally with the patient adjusting it themselves under an action plan — see 0.7.

> [!tip] The other agents
> **· Ivabradine** — for patients in sinus rhythm with a persistently elevated heart rate despite a maximally tolerated beta-blocker. It does not work in AF.
> **· Hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate** — where RAAS blockade cannot be used, and with specific evidence in some populations.
> **· Digoxin** — reduces hospitalisation, **no mortality benefit**, useful for symptoms and for rate control in AF.
> **· Vericiguat** and other newer agents in selected patients.

> [!danger] Practical prescribing points that cause the most trouble
> **· Expect the creatinine to rise and the potassium to increase when starting RAAS blockade or an MRA.** A modest, stable rise is **expected and acceptable** and reflects the drug working — it is **not** a reason to stop. Stopping reflexively at the first creatinine bump denies the patient the most beneficial drugs they will receive. **Recheck electrolytes and renal function after initiation and after each uptitration**, and know the thresholds at which action is genuinely required. `UNVERIFIED — the acceptable creatinine rise and the potassium thresholds for dose reduction or cessation.`
> **· Start beta-blockers when the patient is euvolaemic, not while congested.** "Start low, go slow." **Warn the patient that symptoms may transiently worsen for a few weeks before improving** — otherwise they stop the drug and conclude it made them worse.
> **· SGLT2 inhibitors cause euglycaemic ketoacidosis**, particularly with intercurrent illness, fasting or surgery — **sick day rules and perioperative withholding must be explained.** Cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.3.
> **· ARNI must not be co-administered with an ACE inhibitor, and requires a washout period after stopping one**, because of angioedema risk. `UNVERIFIED — the washout interval.`
> **· MRAs cause gynaecomastia (spironolactone more than eplerenone) and hyperkalaemia.**

> [!danger] Drugs to avoid or use with great caution in HFrEF
> **· NSAIDs** — sodium retention, renal impairment, and they antagonise diuretics and RAAS blockade. **A very common cause of decompensation, and frequently bought over the counter without being mentioned.**
> **· Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem)** — negatively inotropic and contraindicated in HFrEF. **Dihydropyridines (amlodipine) are haemodynamically neutral and may be used** — this distinction matters and is examined. Cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.7.
> **· Thiazolidinediones (pioglitazone)** — fluid retention.
> **· Class I antiarrhythmics such as flecainide** — pro-arrhythmic in structural heart disease.
> **· High-dose corticosteroids** — fluid retention.
> **· Some complementary preparations** and high-sodium effervescent formulations, which are an overlooked sodium load.

> [!warning] Cardiac rehabilitation is under-referred
> **Exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation improves symptoms, quality of life and hospitalisation in heart failure**, and it is offered to a fraction of eligible patients. **Refer.**

**Ix:** UEC and potassium before and after each initiation and uptitration (*why:* the expected and the dangerous changes both occur here; *what:* creatinine, potassium). Blood pressure and heart rate at each visit (*why:* determines titration headroom; *what:* the limiting parameter). **Repeat echocardiography after a period of optimised therapy** (*why:* function frequently improves, which changes prognosis and device eligibility — **and a patient whose EF has recovered on treatment should generally continue it, because withdrawal causes relapse**; *what:* EF change). Iron studies (*why:* IV iron improves symptoms; *what:* ferritin, transferrin saturation).

## 0.4 HFpEF Management

**A/P:** Impaired ventricular relaxation and increased passive stiffness → **elevated filling pressures at normal or near-normal volumes** → congestion and exertional dyspnoea with a preserved ejection fraction. Driven by hypertension, obesity, ageing, diabetes, atrial fibrillation and chronic kidney disease, with systemic inflammation and microvascular dysfunction implicated.

> [!tip] The typical patient
> **Older, more often female, with hypertension, obesity, atrial fibrillation, diabetes and chronic kidney disease.** Exertional dyspnoea and fatigue with a preserved EF on echocardiography.
> **The diagnosis requires objective evidence of raised filling pressures or diastolic dysfunction**, not simply symptoms plus a normal EF — because "breathless with a normal echo" has a wide differential including deconditioning, obesity, lung disease, anaemia and ischaemia.

> [!danger] Exclude the mimics before settling on HFpEF
> **· Cardiac amyloidosis** — see 0.2. Increasingly recognised as a substantial minority of what was called HFpEF, and ATTR is treatable.
> **· Constrictive pericarditis** — potentially curable surgically, and distinguishing it from restrictive physiology is a specific echocardiographic and haemodynamic question.
> **· Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.**
> **· Valvular disease**, particularly severe aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation.
> **· High-output states** — anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, arteriovenous fistula.
> **Labelling a patient as HFpEF closes the diagnostic process**, so it is worth being deliberate about it.

> [!warning] The treatment position has changed
> **For years the accurate statement was that no therapy improved outcomes in HFpEF. That is no longer true — SGLT2 inhibitors now have evidence of benefit in HFpEF**, and this represents the first substantial change in the field.
> The remainder of management is:
> **· Diuretics for congestion** — symptom relief only, and these patients are **preload-dependent and over-diurese easily**, so titrate carefully.
> **· Aggressive blood pressure control** — the single most important preventive and therapeutic measure.
> **· Rate and rhythm control of atrial fibrillation**, which is poorly tolerated because atrial contribution to filling matters more in a stiff ventricle. **Restoring sinus rhythm can produce marked symptomatic improvement.**
> **· Weight loss and exercise training**, both of which have evidence in HFpEF.
> **· Treatment of comorbidities** — sleep apnoea, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, anaemia and iron deficiency.
> `UNVERIFIED — agents, doses and current Australian PBS eligibility for SGLT2 inhibitors in HFpEF, which may lag the evidence.`

**Ix:** As per 0.2, with particular attention to **diastolic parameters and estimated filling pressures on echocardiography** (*why:* required for the diagnosis; *what:* E/e′, atrial size, pulmonary pressures). **Assessment for amyloid where the phenotype fits** (*why:* treatable and increasingly recognised; *what:* voltage-mass discordance, bone scintigraphy, cardiac MRI). Exercise or stress echocardiography where resting studies are equivocal (*why:* filling pressures may only rise on exertion, so a normal resting study does not exclude HFpEF; *what:* exercise-induced rise in filling pressures).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Cautious diuresis for congestion — **these patients tip into hypovolaemia and prerenal failure more readily than HFrEF patients.**

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
SGLT2 inhibitor, blood pressure control, AF management, weight loss, exercise, comorbidity treatment.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
See 0.7.

## 0.5 Decompensation — Precipitants and the Chronic-Acute Interface

> [!tip] Always look for the precipitant — decompensation rarely happens spontaneously
> **· Non-adherence** — to medication, fluid restriction or dietary sodium. **The commonest precipitant, and it is best explored non-judgementally: cost, side effects, complexity, health literacy, and simply running out.**
> **· Infection** — respiratory or urinary.
> **· Ischaemia or infarction** — which may be silent.
> **· Arrhythmia, particularly new atrial fibrillation** — the loss of atrial contribution and the rapid rate together decompensate a marginal ventricle.
> **· Anaemia and iron deficiency.**
> **· Drugs — NSAIDs above all, plus corticosteroids, calcium channel blockers, and newly started chemotherapy.**
> **· Uncontrolled hypertension.**
> **· Thyroid dysfunction.**
> **· Pulmonary embolism.**
> **· Excess salt, fluid or alcohol** — a holiday, a funeral, a restaurant meal.
> **· Worsening renal function.**
> **Treating the decompensation without identifying the precipitant guarantees readmission.**

> [!tip] The wet-dry / warm-cold framework
> A practical bedside classification that directs treatment:
> | | **Warm (adequately perfused)** | **Cold (hypoperfused)** |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Dry (no congestion)** | Compensated — optimise oral therapy | Hypovolaemic or low output — cautious fluid, consider inotrope |
> | **Wet (congested)** | **The commonest presentation — diurese** | **Cardiogenic shock — inotropes, ICU** — cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.3 |
> **Congestion** is assessed by JVP, orthopnoea, oedema, hepatojugular reflux, weight gain and **bendopnoea** (breathlessness on bending forward to tie shoes, a useful and under-asked symptom of raised filling pressures).
> **Perfusion** is assessed by peripheral temperature, pulse pressure, mental state, and urine output.

> [!warning] Diuresis, and diuretic resistance
> Intravenous loop diuretic, with **daily weights, strict fluid balance, and monitoring of renal function and potassium.**
> **Diuretic resistance** — inadequate response despite escalating loop diuretic — is managed by **sequential nephron blockade: adding a thiazide-type diuretic to block distal compensatory sodium reabsorption.** This is effective and requires close electrolyte monitoring because it can cause profound hypokalaemia and hyponatraemia. `UNVERIFIED — agents, doses and monitoring frequency.`
> **Do not stop the disease-modifying drugs during decompensation unless there is a specific reason** (hypotension, hyperkalaemia, acute kidney injury). Beta-blockers in particular are frequently stopped and never restarted, and **continuing them where possible is associated with better outcomes.**

> [!danger] The post-discharge period is the highest-risk time
> **Readmission rates after a heart failure admission are high, and the first weeks are the most vulnerable.**
> **Early review — within about a week of discharge — reduces readmission**, and should be arranged before the patient leaves, not left to them to organise.
> Ensure at discharge: **the precipitant has been identified and addressed · the medications are optimised and the changes explained · a follow-up appointment exists · weights and an action plan are in place · and the GP has an accurate discharge summary stating what changed and why.**

Acute pulmonary oedema management is in [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.3 and is not repeated here — the key points being **sit up, oxygen, non-invasive ventilation and nitrates**, with diuretics playing a smaller role than commonly assumed in the hypertensive "SCAPE" presentation.

**Ix:** **Daily weight and strict fluid balance** (*why:* the most reliable measure of diuretic response, and more reliable than fluid charts, which are notoriously inaccurate; *what:* weight trend). UEC and potassium daily during active diuresis (*why:* acute kidney injury and electrolyte derangement; *what:* creatinine, potassium, sodium). **ECG and troponin** (*why:* ischaemia and arrhythmia as precipitants; *what:* ischaemic change, AF). FBC, iron studies, TFTs, CRP (*why:* anaemia, iron deficiency, thyroid dysfunction and infection as precipitants; *what:* the cause). CXR (*why:* congestion and infection; *what:* oedema, consolidation). **Medication history including over-the-counter NSAIDs** (*why:* a common and entirely reversible precipitant that patients do not report unless asked specifically; *what:* NSAID use).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen, sit up, IV diuretic, identify and treat the precipitant, and NIV plus nitrates in acute pulmonary oedema.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Optimise the four pillars before discharge, and address the precipitant.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Early follow-up, action plan, and heart failure service involvement.

## 0.6 Comorbidities, Devices and Advanced Heart Failure

> [!tip] The comorbidities that change outcomes
> **· Atrial fibrillation** — very common and mutually reinforcing. **Catheter ablation in selected patients with HFrEF and AF improves outcomes** and is worth considering rather than accepting permanent rate control. Cross-refer [[B3_Arrhythmia__Bradycardia_and_Cardiac_Devices]] 0.4.
> **· Iron deficiency** — treat with **intravenous** iron; oral absorption is poor in heart failure.
> **· Chronic kidney disease** — cardiorenal syndrome, where each worsens the other. The temptation to stop RAAS blockade to protect the kidneys usually harms both.
> **· Sleep-disordered breathing** — common, and treating obstructive sleep apnoea helps.
> **· Depression** — common, under-treated, and associated with worse adherence and outcomes. Ask about it.
> **· Diabetes, COPD, obesity, frailty and cognitive impairment**, each of which complicates management and self-care.

> [!info] Devices
> **· Implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD)** — for **primary prevention of sudden cardiac death in HFrEF with a persistently low ejection fraction despite an adequate period of optimal medical therapy.** The key point is **"despite optimal therapy for an adequate period"** — EF frequently improves on the four pillars, and assessing eligibility too early leads to unnecessary implantation.
> **· Cardiac resynchronisation therapy (CRT)** — biventricular pacing for patients with **left bundle branch block and a broad QRS**, which resynchronises a dyssynchronous ventricle. It improves symptoms, ejection fraction and survival in appropriately selected patients.
> `UNVERIFIED — EF thresholds, QRS duration criteria, the required duration of optimal medical therapy, and NYHA class requirements for both devices.`
> **· ICD deactivation must be discussed as part of end-of-life planning** — an unsuspended device will shock a dying patient repeatedly. Cross-refer [[B3_Arrhythmia__Bradycardia_and_Cardiac_Devices]] 0.6 and [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.5.

> [!warning] Recognising advanced heart failure and when to refer
> Features suggesting advanced disease: **recurrent hospitalisations · persistent NYHA III–IV symptoms despite optimal therapy · inability to tolerate the disease-modifying drugs because of hypotension or renal impairment · escalating diuretic requirements · progressive renal dysfunction · hyponatraemia · weight loss and cardiac cachexia · frequent ICD shocks · and declining functional capacity.**
> **These patients should be referred to a specialist heart failure service for consideration of advanced therapies — mechanical circulatory support, transplantation, or a palliative approach — and the referral is frequently made too late.**
> Advanced therapies include **left ventricular assist devices** and **cardiac transplantation**, with strict eligibility criteria and limited availability. **Continuous inotrope infusion has a role as a palliative measure** for symptom control in patients not eligible for advanced therapies, with the explicit understanding that it does not prolong life.

**Ix:** Repeat echocardiography after optimisation (*why:* determines device eligibility; *what:* EF). ECG for QRS duration and morphology (*why:* CRT eligibility; *what:* LBBB, QRS width). Right heart catheterisation in advanced disease (*why:* haemodynamic assessment for transplant and device candidacy; *what:* filling pressures, cardiac output, pulmonary vascular resistance). Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (*why:* objective functional assessment and transplant prognostication; *what:* peak VO₂). Frailty and cognitive assessment (*why:* determines suitability for burdensome therapies; *what:* frailty grade — cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.2).

Not applicable.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive

## 0.7 Self-Management, Prognosis and Palliative Care

> [!tip] The self-management package
> **· Daily weights, at the same time, on the same scales, recorded** — with **a written action plan specifying what weight gain over what period should trigger what action** (an extra diuretic dose, or contacting the heart failure nurse or GP). **This is the single most practical self-management tool and it prevents admissions.** `UNVERIFIED — the weight gain thresholds used in Australian action plans.`
> **· Sodium restriction** — sensible moderation rather than severe restriction; the evidence for aggressive restriction is weaker than traditionally taught and it can worsen appetite and nutrition.
> **· Fluid restriction** — **individualised, not universal.** Blanket fluid restriction for all heart failure patients is not supported and causes thirst, poor adherence and hypovolaemia. It has a role in hyponatraemia and refractory congestion.
> **· Medication adherence** — explore cost, complexity, side effects and understanding.
> **· Exercise and cardiac rehabilitation.**
> **· Vaccination** — influenza, pneumococcal and COVID-19, which reduce decompensation from infection.
> **· Alcohol moderation or abstinence**, and smoking cessation.
> **· Recognising deterioration** — increasing breathlessness, orthopnoea, weight gain, oedema, and reduced exercise tolerance.

> [!info] Multidisciplinary heart failure programs work
> **Nurse-led heart failure programs and multidisciplinary disease management reduce readmission and mortality**, through education, titration support, early detection of decompensation and rapid access.
> They are established in Australia and are **under-referred.** Referral to a heart failure service or program is a concrete action with good evidence behind it.

> [!danger] The prognosis is worse than many cancers, and patients are rarely told
> **Mortality after a heart failure hospitalisation is substantial, and five-year survival in advanced heart failure compares unfavourably with several common cancers.**
> **Patients and families consistently overestimate prognosis**, in part because the illness trajectory is one of exacerbation and partial recovery, which reads as "getting better again" each time.
> **This is why advance care planning in heart failure is systematically neglected** — there is never an obviously terminal moment. Cross-refer [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.5 for the organ-failure trajectory.

> [!tip] Palliative care in heart failure
> **Early integration alongside active treatment**, not as a terminal handover.
> Symptom priorities: **breathlessness (opioids are effective and under-used in refractory breathlessness in heart failure)**, fatigue, pain, depression and anxiety.
> **Practical end-of-life issues specific to heart failure: ICD deactivation, rationalising the medication burden, deciding about further hospitalisation, and preferred place of care.**
> **The conversations to have while the patient is well: what matters most to them, what trade-offs they would accept, who speaks for them, and what they would not want.** Cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.3 and [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.6.

**Ix:** Regular review of weight, symptoms, NYHA class, renal function, electrolytes and adherence (*why:* the ongoing measures that detect deterioration and permit titration; *what:* trends). **Depression screening** (*why:* common, under-treated, worsens outcomes; *what:* depressive symptoms). Frailty and functional assessment in older patients (*why:* informs the intensity of therapy and the goals of care; *what:* frailty and function).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Not applicable.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Structured self-management education, action plan, and heart failure program referral.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Regular review, honest prognostic discussion, advance care planning, and palliative care integration as the disease advances.
