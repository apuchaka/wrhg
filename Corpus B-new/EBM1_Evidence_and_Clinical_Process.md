## 0.1 Critical Appraisal in Practice

> [!tip] Evidence-based medicine is three things, not one
> **BEST AVAILABLE EVIDENCE + CLINICAL EXPERTISE + PATIENT VALUES AND CIRCUMSTANCES.**
> **All three are required.** Evidence without expertise produces protocol-driven care that ignores the patient in front of you; expertise without evidence produces eminence-based practice; and both without the patient's values produce technically correct decisions that the person did not want.
> **The commonest misuse of the term is to mean "the guideline says", which is only one of the three.**

> [!warning] Start with pre-appraised resources, not with PubMed
> **For a clinical question on the ward, the efficient order is:**
> **1. POINT-OF-CARE SUMMARIES — UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, DynaMed.**
> **2. AUSTRALIAN THERAPEUTIC SOURCES — eTG (Therapeutic Guidelines) and the Australian Medicines Handbook, which are the standard for prescribing here and are what your seniors and pharmacists will expect you to have used.**
> **3. SYSTEMATIC REVIEWS — Cochrane and equivalents.**
> **4. PRIMARY LITERATURE — last, and only when the question is genuinely not answered above.**
> **Going straight to a database search for a bedside question wastes time and produces a less reliable answer than a resource that has already been appraised.**
> **Structure the question first — PICO: POPULATION · INTERVENTION · COMPARATOR · OUTCOME.** A vague question returns a vague answer.

> [!tip] Appraising a therapy paper — the questions that matter
> **VALIDITY (can I believe it?):**
> **· Was it randomised, and was ALLOCATION CONCEALED?**
> **· Were the groups similar at baseline?**
> **· Was FOLLOW-UP complete, and were losses balanced and explained?**
> **· Was the analysis by INTENTION TO TREAT?**
> **· Was blinding adequate — particularly of outcome assessors for subjective outcomes?**
> **RESULTS (what does it show?):**
> **· What is the ABSOLUTE effect and the NNT — not the relative risk reduction?** Cross-refer [[PH1_Population_Health_and_Research_Literacy]] 0.4.
> **· How PRECISE is it — how wide is the confidence interval?**
> **APPLICABILITY (does it apply to my patient?):**
> **· Are the trial participants like the person in front of me? **Trial populations are systematically YOUNGER, HEALTHIER, LESS COMORBID, less often female, less often from minority backgrounds, and less often frail than real patients.**
> **· Was the OUTCOME one the patient would care about, or a surrogate?**
> **· Are the HARMS reported as thoroughly as the benefits — and were they actively sought?**
> **· Is the treatment feasible, affordable and acceptable here?**

> [!danger] Harms are systematically under-reported, and abstracts are systematically spun
> **Adverse effects receive far less space, less rigorous ascertainment and less prominent reporting than benefits in most trial publications.** **So an apparent absence of harm frequently means harm was not measured well, not that it did not occur.**
> **"SPIN" — where the abstract's conclusion is more favourable than the results justify — is common:**
> **· A non-significant primary outcome presented as a "trend" or "promising signal".**
> **· Emphasis on a SECONDARY or SUBGROUP finding when the primary outcome was negative.**
> **· A COMPOSITE endpoint driven entirely by its least important component.**
> **· A SURROGATE outcome presented as though it were a clinical one.**
> **READ THE RESULTS AND DECIDE FOR YOURSELF WHAT THEY SHOW BEFORE READING THE CONCLUSION.**
> **Also check: TRIAL REGISTRATION (was the primary outcome pre-specified, or changed after the data were seen?) · FUNDING SOURCE and author conflicts, since industry-sponsored trials more often report favourable results · and whether the comparator was a fair one (an inadequate dose of the competing drug, or placebo where an active comparator existed).**

## 0.2 Using Guidelines Well

> [!tip] Guidelines are aids to decision-making, not rules
> **A good guideline distinguishes the STRENGTH OF A RECOMMENDATION from the QUALITY OF THE EVIDENCE behind it — and these are not the same.** A strong recommendation can rest on weak evidence when the harms of not acting are severe, and vice versa. `UNVERIFIED — the GRADE terminology and its categories.`
> **THE AUSTRALIAN SOURCES you will actually use: eTG · the Australian Medicines Handbook · the RACGP Red Book for preventive activities · NHMRC guidelines · Cancer Council Australia guidelines · and the specialty society guidelines (RANZCOG, CSANZ, ANZAED and others).**
> **And LOCAL protocols — which take precedence for operational matters (which antibiotic, which form, which pathway), because they reflect local resistance patterns, formularies and services.**

> [!danger] Guidelines are written for single diseases, and your patient has five
> **This is the central problem in applying guidelines to real patients.**
> **Each guideline is developed for one condition, usually excluding the comorbid, elderly and frail from its evidence base — and applying five single-disease guidelines simultaneously to one multimorbid patient produces POLYPHARMACY, contradictory advice, an unmanageable treatment burden, and interactions nobody anticipated.**
> **A patient with heart failure, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, osteoarthritis and depression, managed strictly by guideline, ends up on a dozen medications with conflicting instructions and a full-time job attending appointments.**
> **THE RESPONSE IS NOT TO IGNORE GUIDELINES BUT TO PRIORITISE: what matters most to this person · what has the largest absolute benefit given their competing risks and life expectancy · what has the greatest treatment burden · and what can be stopped.** Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.2, 0.4.
> **DEVIATING FROM A GUIDELINE IS ENTIRELY LEGITIMATE where there is a reason — and the requirement is that the reasoning is EXPLICIT AND DOCUMENTED.** **"Guideline recommends X; not used here because Y" is defensible. Silent deviation is not.**
> **CHOOSING WISELY AUSTRALIA publishes specialty-generated lists of low-value tests and treatments** — a useful counterweight to the assumption that more intervention is better. `UNVERIFIED — current recommendations.`

## 0.3 Clinical Reasoning and Diagnostic Error

> [!tip] Two systems, and both are necessary
> **SYSTEM 1 — fast, intuitive, pattern-recognition.** It is how experienced clinicians work most of the time, it is efficient, and it is usually right. It is also where bias operates.
> **SYSTEM 2 — slow, deliberate, analytical.** Accurate but effortful and slow, and it degrades with fatigue, interruption and time pressure.
> **The goal is not to abolish System 1 — that is neither possible nor desirable — but to recognise the situations where it fails and deliberately engage System 2.**
> **DIAGNOSTIC ERROR IS COMMON AND IS A LEADING CAUSE OF PREVENTABLE PATIENT HARM**, and it is under-recognised compared with medication and procedural errors because it is harder to see and rarely reported.

> [!danger] The biases that actually cause harm, with what they look like
> **· PREMATURE CLOSURE — accepting a diagnosis before it is fully verified, and stopping the search.** **This is the single largest contributor to diagnostic error.** *(The patient with "gastroenteritis" who has appendicitis; the "musculoskeletal chest pain" that is a dissection.)*
> **· ANCHORING — fixing on the initial impression and failing to adjust as new information arrives.** *(The triage note said "?anxiety" and the pulmonary embolism was never considered.)*
> **· CONFIRMATION BIAS — seeking and weighting evidence that supports the working diagnosis while discounting what contradicts it.**
> **· AVAILABILITY — over-estimating what comes readily to mind, usually because it was recent or memorable.** *(Diagnosing the condition you saw last week, or missing the one you have never seen.)*
> **· DIAGNOSTIC MOMENTUM — a label attached early, often by someone junior or by a paramedic, becomes progressively more certain as it is repeated through handovers, until nobody questions it.** *(The "seizure" that was a syncope, carried through three shifts.)*
> **· ATTRIBUTION AND DIAGNOSTIC OVERSHADOWING — attributing new symptoms to an existing condition or to a characteristic of the patient.** **This disproportionately harms people with mental illness, intellectual disability, obesity, substance use disorders and chronic pain — and it kills them younger.** Cross-refer [[N6_Functional__Dissociative_and_Personality_Disorders]] 0.5 and [[AU1_Australian_Health_Context_and_ATSI_Health]] 0.5.
> **· SEARCH SATISFICING — stopping after the first positive finding.** *(The second fracture on the trauma series; the second drug interaction.)*
> **· FRAMING — being led by how the problem was presented to you.**
> **· OUTCOME AND HINDSIGHT BIAS — judging the quality of a decision by how it turned out, which distorts learning at morbidity and mortality meetings.**

> [!warning] The debiasing strategies that work — and two questions worth asking aloud
> **· A DIAGNOSTIC TIME-OUT before committing: pause, and ask deliberately.**
> **· "WHAT ELSE COULD THIS BE?" — force an explicit differential rather than a single label.**
> **· "WHAT WOULD I BE EMBARRASSED TO MISS?" — this prompts the dangerous alternatives rather than the likely ones, and it is the more useful of the two.**
> **· SEEK DISCONFIRMING EVIDENCE, not more confirming evidence.**
> **· RECONSIDER WHEN THE PATIENT IS NOT BEHAVING AS THE DIAGNOSIS PREDICTS.** **"Not improving as expected" is diagnostic information, not a reason to escalate the same treatment.**
> **· A RE-PRESENTATION IS A RED FLAG.** **A patient returning within a short period has either been misdiagnosed or has progressed — and the second visit deserves a fresh assessment, not a repeat of the first one with more reassurance.** **Re-presentation is one of the strongest signals available and it is routinely treated as an inconvenience.**
> **· ASK SOMEONE ELSE. A second opinion is a debiasing tool, not an admission of inadequacy.**
> **· KNOW THE HIGH-RISK CIRCUMSTANCES: fatigue and night shift · frequent interruption · high workload and time pressure · handover boundaries · and the patient you find difficult.**
> **THAT LAST ONE IS WORTH STATING PLAINLY: A NEGATIVE EMOTIONAL RESPONSE TO A PATIENT MEASURABLY INCREASES DIAGNOSTIC ERROR.** **Noticing "I don't like this patient" or "this patient is being difficult" should function as a prompt to be MORE careful, not less.**

## 0.4 Documentation

> [!tip] What the notes are for
> **Continuity of care · communication between clinicians · a record of reasoning · a medicolegal document · and a source for audit and quality improvement.**
> **The first two are the reasons that matter day to day, and they are the ones served by good documentation.** **The medicolegal function follows automatically from doing the first two well — writing "defensively" produces worse notes than writing clearly for the next clinician.**

> [!warning] The principles
> **· CONTEMPORANEOUS — written at the time or as soon as practicable.**
> **· DATED, TIMED, SIGNED, with your NAME and DESIGNATION printed, and legible.**
> **· FACTUAL and OBJECTIVE — describe what you observed and were told, and attribute reported information to its source.**
> **· DOCUMENT THE POSITIVES AND THE RELEVANT NEGATIVES.** **"No red flags" is not documentation; naming the red flags you asked about and excluded is.**
> **· DOCUMENT YOUR REASONING, not just your conclusion — the differential considered, why you favoured one, and what would change your mind. This is what makes the note useful to the next person and defensible later.**
> **· DOCUMENT WHAT YOU TOLD THE PATIENT, including the SAFETY NETTING — the specific return criteria — and what they understood.**
> **· DOCUMENT WHO YOU DISCUSSED IT WITH, by name and time.**
> **· CORRECTIONS: strike through with a single line so the original remains legible, then date, time and initial the correction. NEVER obliterate, overwrite, use correction fluid, or backdate.** **An honest correction is unremarkable; an altered record destroys credibility entirely, including for the parts that were true.**

> [!danger] Three documentation habits that cause harm
> **1. COPY-AND-PASTE (and "copy-forward" in electronic records).** **It propagates errors indefinitely, inflates notes with stale information, obscures what actually changed today, and creates entries describing examinations that were not performed.** **An error copied forward for a week acquires a false authority through repetition.**
> **2. PEJORATIVE OR JUDGEMENTAL LANGUAGE.** **"Non-compliant", "difficult", "drug-seeking", "poor historian", "frequent flyer".** **Patients read their records, and this language demonstrably changes how subsequent clinicians treat them — priming them to take symptoms less seriously.** **Write what happened instead: "did not attend — reports no transport", "declined admission; concerns explored were X and Y".** Cross-refer [[AU1_Australian_Health_Context_and_ATSI_Health]] 0.3.
> **3. EMPTY ENTRIES.** **"Reviewed. Plan as per team." "Obs stable." "For ward round."** These occupy space, satisfy an audit of documentation frequency, and convey nothing — and they are indistinguishable from not having seen the patient.
> **Also: unexplained abbreviations, speculation about other clinicians' decisions, and using the record to conduct a disagreement.**

## 0.5 Handover and Transitions of Care

> [!danger] Communication failure is the commonest contributor to serious harm
> **Handover is where information is lost, and transitions of care — emergency department to ward, ward to ward, shift to shift, hospital to general practice — are the highest-risk moments in a patient's admission.**
> **Each transfer loses information, and the losses compound.**
> **ISBAR is the structure to use: IDENTIFY (yourself, the patient) · SITUATION (what is happening now) · BACKGROUND (relevant history) · ASSESSMENT (what you think is going on) · RECOMMENDATION (what you want, and by when).**
> **The "R" is the part most often omitted, and it is the point of the conversation.** **Make the ask explicit: "I need you to review her in the next hour", not "I just wanted to let you know".**

> [!warning] What good handover requires
> **· A PROTECTED TIME AND PLACE, with interruptions minimised.**
> **· SICKEST PATIENTS FIRST, not alphabetically or by bed number.**
> **· A WRITTEN LIST as well as the verbal handover — verbal alone does not survive.**
> **· EXPLICIT TRANSFER OF RESPONSIBILITY — the receiving clinician acknowledges that they now own these patients. Ambiguity about who is responsible is how patients fall between teams.**
> **· TASKS WITH AN OWNER AND A TIME — "chase the potassium" is not a task; "I need you to check the potassium at 2200 and act on it if above X" is.**
> **· AN OPPORTUNITY TO ASK QUESTIONS — handover delivered as a monologue transfers less than half of what is intended.**
> **· AND COMMUNICATE THE SOFT CONCERNS.** **"I'm worried about the man in bed 12 and I can't tell you exactly why" is legitimate and important clinical information.** **Clinician gut feeling has measurable predictive value for deterioration, and it is exactly what gets omitted from structured handover because it does not fit a field.** **Say it anyway.**
> **· And be alert to DIAGNOSTIC MOMENTUM travelling with the patient — hand over the uncertainty, not just the label.**

> [!danger] The discharge summary is the most consequential document you write
> **It is the handover to the person who will look after the patient for the next decade, and it is routinely done last, quickly, and badly.**
> **WHAT IT MUST CONTAIN:**
> **· The DIAGNOSIS, and what actually happened.**
> **· MEDICATION CHANGES — every drug STARTED, STOPPED or CHANGED, WITH THE REASON.** **A list of discharge medications without indicating what changed and why is nearly useless, and it is how patients end up back on a drug that was stopped for a good reason, or permanently off one that was only held temporarily.**
> **· ANYTHING HELD OR WITHHELD, with WHO should restart it, WHEN, and with what MONITORING.** **"ACE inhibitor withheld" with no plan produces either a patient permanently off a beneficial drug, or one restarted blindly into another acute kidney injury.** Cross-refer [[H3_Acute_Kidney_Injury_and_CKD]] 0.3.
> **· OUTSTANDING RESULTS, and NAMED responsibility for following them up.** **A pending culture, biopsy or scan with nobody assigned is a well-documented mechanism of serious missed diagnoses.**
> **· FOLLOW-UP — arranged with whom, when, and whether the appointment exists or the GP is expected to arrange it.**
> **· WHAT TO WATCH FOR, and what should prompt re-presentation.**
> **· WHAT THE PATIENT AND FAMILY WERE TOLD — including prognosis and goals of care discussions, which the general practitioner otherwise learns about from the patient.**
> **AND IT MUST BE TIMELY. A summary that arrives after the patient has already seen their GP has failed, however good it is.**

## 0.6 Error, Incident Reporting and Open Disclosure

> [!tip] Systems thinking, and what a "just culture" means
> **THE SWISS CHEESE MODEL: serious harm rarely results from one person's failure. It results from an ACTIVE FAILURE (the error at the sharp end) passing through gaps in multiple LATENT conditions — staffing, design, fatigue, training, equipment, protocols — that were already there.**
> **Asking "who did this?" finds a person. Asking "how did the system allow this?" finds the fix.**
> **A JUST CULTURE distinguishes between:**
> **· HUMAN ERROR — an inadvertent slip or lapse. The response is to CONSOLE and to redesign the system that allowed it.**
> **· AT-RISK BEHAVIOUR — drifting into unsafe shortcuts, usually because the safe way is impractical. The response is to COACH and remove the incentive.**
> **· RECKLESS BEHAVIOUR — conscious disregard of substantial risk. The response is accountability.**
> **A PURELY PUNITIVE CULTURE DOES NOT REDUCE ERROR — IT DRIVES REPORTING UNDERGROUND**, so the organisation stops learning while the same latent conditions persist.
> **REPORT INCIDENTS AND NEAR MISSES.** **Near misses are the most valuable reports, because the learning is free — the harm did not occur.** **Under-reporting is the main limitation of every incident system, and it is worst among junior staff who fear blame.**

> [!danger] Open disclosure — apologising is not admitting liability
> **OPEN DISCLOSURE IS A NATIONAL STANDARD IN AUSTRALIA, not an optional courtesy.** `UNVERIFIED — the ACSQHC Australian Open Disclosure Framework and your local policy.`
> **THE ELEMENTS:**
> **1. ACKNOWLEDGE that something has gone wrong, EARLY — do not wait for an investigation to conclude.**
> **2. APOLOGISE or express REGRET, sincerely, using the word "sorry".** **"I am sorry this happened to you" is an expression of empathy, NOT an admission of legal liability, and apology protections exist in Australian law.** **The fear of apologising is largely misplaced, and withholding an apology causes disproportionate harm.**
> **3. EXPLAIN factually what happened and what is known — WITHOUT SPECULATING about cause or blaming individuals before the facts are established.** "We don't yet know why, and we will find out and tell you" is an acceptable and honest answer.
> **4. Explain what will be done for the patient NOW.**
> **5. Explain what is being done to PREVENT RECURRENCE — and follow up to tell them what was found.**
> **6. Provide ONGOING contact and support.**
> **WHAT PATIENTS AND FAMILIES CONSISTENTLY SAY THEY WANT: to be TOLD, honestly and promptly · a genuine APOLOGY · an EXPLANATION · and an assurance that it will not happen to someone else.**
> **FAILING TO DISCLOSE DAMAGES TRUST FAR MORE THAN THE ERROR ITSELF, and concealment — or the perception of it — is one of the strongest drivers of complaints and litigation.** **Openness reduces both.**
> **As an intern: you are rarely the person who leads a formal open disclosure conversation, but you are frequently the person who first realises something has gone wrong. ESCALATE IT PROMPTLY, document factually, and do not attempt to manage it alone or to reassure the family with an explanation you cannot support.**

> [!warning] The second victim, and speaking up
> **THE SECOND VICTIM: clinicians involved in a serious adverse event experience substantial distress — guilt, shame, loss of confidence, intrusive rumination, sleep disturbance and, in some, lasting career impact and suicidal ideation.**
> **This is a normal response to having harmed someone you were trying to help, and it is not a sign of weakness or of unsuitability for medicine.**
> **Support — from colleagues, supervisors, employee assistance programs and, where needed, professional help — is legitimate and necessary, and the culture of not discussing these events makes them worse.** **If it happens to you, tell someone.** Cross-refer [[N1_Mental_State_Examination_and_Risk]] 0.3.
> **SPEAKING UP ABOUT SAFETY — the hierarchy is a genuine barrier, and juniors reliably under-communicate concern to seniors.**
> **GRADED ASSERTIVENESS gives you an escalating script, and the "CUS" formulation is easy to remember under pressure:**
> **· "I am CONCERNED about..."**
> **· "I am UNCOMFORTABLE with..."**
> **· "This is a SAFETY issue — we need to stop."**
> **Escalating explicitly through those three levels is more effective than hinting, and it gives you permission to be direct.** **If you remain unheard and a patient is at risk, escalate around or above the person — that is a legitimate use of the chain of escalation and it is what it exists for.**
> **Other structures worth knowing: MORBIDITY AND MORTALITY meetings (which should examine systems, not perform blame) · ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS for serious incidents · the CLINICAL AUDIT CYCLE (standard → measure → change → re-measure, and the re-measurement is the step routinely skipped) · sentinel event reporting · coronial reporting obligations · and mandatory notification to AHPRA where a practitioner poses a risk to the public.** Cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.4–0.5.

**Ix:** Not applicable — but the analogous discipline for this file: **before ordering a test, ask what you will do with each possible result** (*why:* a test that cannot change management should not be done, and this single question prevents a large volume of low-value investigation; *what:* a decision). **Before accepting a diagnosis, ask "what else could this be?" and "what would I be embarrassed to miss?"** (*why:* premature closure is the largest single contributor to diagnostic error; *what:* the dangerous alternative). **Before discharging, ask "what is outstanding, and who owns it?"** (*why:* unfollowed results at transitions of care are a documented mechanism of serious harm; *what:* a named person and a plan). **Before handing over, ask "what am I worried about that isn't in the notes?"** (*why:* clinician concern predicts deterioration and is exactly what structured handover omits; *what:* the soft signal).
