## 0.1 The Australian Health System

> [!tip] The two-tier funding structure, and why it fragments care
> **· MEDICARE — universal insurance funding medical services through the Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS), on a fee-for-service basis.** Practitioners may **bulk bill** (accepting the rebate as full payment) or charge a **gap.**
> **· PHARMACEUTICAL BENEFITS SCHEME (PBS) — subsidised medicines, with a co-payment, a concessional rate, and a SAFETY NET once annual spending passes a threshold.** Some medicines require **authority** approval against specified criteria.
> **· PUBLIC HOSPITALS — state-operated, jointly funded by the states and the Commonwealth.**
> **· PRIVATE HOSPITALS AND INSURANCE — with policy levers (Lifetime Health Cover loading, Medicare Levy Surcharge) designed to encourage private cover.**
> **The Commonwealth funds primary care and medicines; the states run hospitals.** **This split is the structural origin of much of the fragmentation patients experience** — discharge from a state hospital into Commonwealth-funded primary care, aged care and disability systems, with no single accountable party.
> `UNVERIFIED — all co-payment amounts, thresholds and current arrangements.`

> [!info] Primary care and the items that fund longitudinal care
> **General practice is the entry point and the referral gateway** — specialist attendance generally requires a referral for a Medicare rebate.
> **MBS items exist to fund structured chronic and preventive care**, and knowing they exist matters even if you never bill them:
> **· GP Management Plans and Team Care Arrangements** — funding multidisciplinary chronic disease care and allied health access.
> **· Health assessments** for defined groups.
> **· The Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Assessment (commonly called the "715")** — a comprehensive annual health check that unlocks follow-up items and allied health access. **Recommending it is a concrete thing to do.**
> **· Mental health treatment plans** funding psychological therapy.
> `UNVERIFIED — item numbers, eligibility, frequency and current structure; these change and I would not rely on the detail here.`

> [!danger] Cost is a clinical variable — ask about it
> **Out-of-pocket costs cause Australians to delay or avoid care, skip filling prescriptions, and take medicines less often than prescribed to make them last.**
> **Patients rarely volunteer this.** They do not say "I can't afford it" — they say the tablets didn't suit them, or they simply do not return.
> **Ask directly and without embarrassment: "Is the cost of these medicines manageable?"**
> **What you can do: prescribe generics · consider PBS-listed alternatives · check concession card status and the SAFETY NET · use the CTG PBS Co-payment Programme for eligible Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients (see 0.6) · consolidate the number of medicines · and consider whether every drug on the list is still needed.** Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.4.

> [!tip] The other systems you will interact with
> **· Aged care** — My Aged Care as the entry point, with the system currently in reform. Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.3.
> **· NDIS** — for people under 65 with permanent and significant disability.
> **· DVA** — veterans, with separate and often better-resourced access. **Ask about veteran status.**
> **· My Health Record, electronic prescribing and telehealth.**
> **· Notifiable disease surveillance through state public health units.**
> **· AHPRA and the Medical Board** — registration, CPD, and **mandatory notification obligations.** Cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]].
> **· The National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standards** — including a specific standard addressing **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health**, plus open disclosure, incident reporting and clinical governance. `UNVERIFIED — the standards and their current content.`

## 0.2 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health — Context

> [!danger] The framing is not optional decoration — it determines whether the clinical reasoning is correct
> **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples represent the oldest continuous living cultures on earth**, with sophisticated systems of law, kinship, land management, knowledge and healing sustained over tens of thousands of years, and with enormous diversity — **hundreds of distinct nations and language groups, living across urban, regional and remote Australia.** **They are not a homogeneous group, and a patient in Adelaide may have entirely different circumstances from one in the APY Lands.**
> **The health disparities that exist are the consequence of COLONISATION and its continuing effects** — dispossession from Country, the forced removal of children (the Stolen Generations), suppression of language and culture, exclusion from services and citizenship rights, systemic and interpersonal racism, and the social and economic conditions that followed.
> **They are NOT a consequence of culture, of biology, or of individual choices** — and framing them that way is both factually wrong and clinically harmful, because it directs attention away from the causes that can actually be addressed.
> **Culture is a PROTECTIVE factor.** Connection to Country, family, community and cultural practice is associated with better health and wellbeing, and supporting it is part of good care.

> [!info] Social and emotional wellbeing — a broader model of health
> **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander concepts of health are HOLISTIC**, encompassing the physical, social, emotional, cultural and spiritual wellbeing of the individual AND the community.
> The **social and emotional wellbeing (SEWB)** framework describes connections to: **body · mind and emotions · family and kinship · community · culture · Country · spirituality and ancestors.**
> **The practical implication: a presentation cannot be understood in isolation from family, community and connection to Country.** A patient's non-attendance, distress or apparent disengagement frequently has an explanation in one of those domains, and asking about them is clinically useful rather than merely respectful.
> **It also means Western mental health categories map imperfectly onto this framework**, and grief, cultural obligation and the effects of racism should not be reflexively pathologised.

> [!warning] The social determinants that generate the clinical burden
> **· HOUSING AND OVERCROWDING — with direct, traceable links to skin infections and scabies, acute rheumatic fever, otitis media, gastroenteritis and respiratory infection.** **This is the clearest example of a determinant producing specific diseases**, and it explains why treating the individual infection without addressing the environment produces recurrence.
> **· FOOD SECURITY AND COST — fresh food in remote communities can cost multiples of the urban price**, which makes dietary advice meaningless without acknowledging it.
> **· Education, employment and income.**
> **· ACCESS — distance, transport, workforce shortage, and the cost of getting to care.**
> **· INCARCERATION — Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people are grossly over-represented in the justice system, with major health consequences and disruption of care.**
> **· RACISM — both interpersonal and systemic — is itself a determinant of health.** Experiences of racism in healthcare settings are common, are associated with worse health, and directly drive avoidance of services and self-discharge.
> `UNVERIFIED — all statistics; specific figures are deliberately omitted here.`

> [!tip] Closing the Gap and the community-controlled sector
> **The National Agreement on Closing the Gap** commits governments to socioeconomic targets and, importantly, to **Priority Reforms** including **shared decision-making with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, building the community-controlled sector, transforming government organisations, and shared access to data.**
> **The reframing matters: the earlier approach set targets FOR Aboriginal people; the current agreement is built around partnership and self-determination.**
> **ABORIGINAL COMMUNITY CONTROLLED HEALTH ORGANISATIONS (ACCHOs)** are primary health care services **initiated and governed by the local Aboriginal community**, delivering holistic, comprehensive and culturally safe care. **NACCHO** is the national peak body.
> **They consistently outperform mainstream services on engagement and outcomes for Aboriginal patients** — which is why **referring to, liaising with and discharging back to an ACCHO is good clinical practice, not a courtesy.** **Find out which ACCHO serves your area.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the current targets, progress and program structures.`

## 0.3 Cultural Safety in Practice

> [!danger] Cultural safety is defined by the PATIENT, not by the clinician
> **This is the distinction that matters and it is examinable.**
> **"Cultural awareness" and "cultural competence" are things the clinician acquires and can claim to have.**
> **CULTURAL SAFETY is determined by the person RECEIVING the care — it is their experience of feeling safe, respected and able to be themselves.** **You do not get to decide that you have provided it.**
> **It requires REFLEXIVITY: examining your own culture, assumptions, biases and the power you hold in the interaction — rather than treating the patient as the one with the culture.**
> **Cultural safety is now embedded in the Medical Board's professional standards and in the NSQHS Standards.** `UNVERIFIED — the current definitions and requirements.`

> [!warning] Communication — the practical points that change the interaction
> **· SILENCE is comfortable and meaningful in many Aboriginal cultures.** **Do not rush to fill it.** Waiting is often what allows the person to answer properly.
> **· DIRECT SUSTAINED EYE CONTACT may be experienced as confronting or disrespectful.** Follow the person's lead rather than insisting on it, and do not interpret its absence as evasion or low mood.
> **· GRATUITOUS CONCURRENCE — agreeing with a question to be polite, to end an uncomfortable interaction, or because the question format invites yes.** **This is a well-described phenomenon and it means that "Do you understand?" and "Is that alright?" produce unreliable answers.**
> **Instead: avoid strings of closed questions · use open questions · and use TEACH-BACK — "just so I know I've explained it properly, can you tell me what you'll do when you get home?"**
> **· Avoid jargon and rapid-fire questioning**, which can feel like interrogation.
> **· MEN'S AND WOMEN'S BUSINESS — some health matters are appropriately discussed only with a clinician of the same gender.** **Ask whether the person would prefer that, rather than assuming either way.**
> **· FAMILY AND KINSHIP structures are broader than the nuclear family.** Ask **who the person wants involved** in discussions and decisions, and recognise that the appropriate decision-maker may not be the next of kin as recorded.

> [!danger] Engage Aboriginal Liaison Officers and Health Workers EARLY
> **Aboriginal Liaison Officers, Aboriginal Health Workers and Aboriginal Health Practitioners are skilled professionals with clinical, cultural and advocacy roles.**
> **The common failure is calling them at the point of crisis — when a patient wants to self-discharge, or when communication has already broken down.**
> **Engage them at ADMISSION, as a routine part of care**, so that the relationship exists before it is needed. **They can identify barriers you cannot see, facilitate communication, support the patient and family, and coordinate with the person's community and ACCHO.**
> **Use INTERPRETERS for Aboriginal languages where needed** — Aboriginal language interpreter services exist and are substantially under-used, and the assumption that a person speaking some English does not need one is frequently wrong for complex medical discussions.

> [!danger] "Discharge against medical advice" is usually a signal about the service, not the patient
> **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients self-discharge from hospital at higher rates, and the reasons are almost always identifiable and often addressable:**
> **· Feeling unsafe, unwelcome, judged or racially discriminated against.**
> **· Not understanding what is happening or why.**
> **· Family and cultural obligations — including SORRY BUSINESS (cultural obligations surrounding death and mourning), which may take precedence over an admission and should be accommodated rather than resisted.**
> **· Being far from Country, family and community, sometimes thousands of kilometres.**
> **· Care of children or other family members at home.**
> **· Previous negative experiences with the health system.**
> **The reframe: treat self-discharge as a SYSTEM problem to be diagnosed, not a patient behaviour to be documented and dismissed.**
> **Practically: ask what is making them want to leave · involve the Aboriginal Liaison Officer · explore whether the barrier can be solved (a phone call home, a family member accommodated, a treatment plan adjusted, a leave pass) · negotiate what care is achievable rather than presenting it as all-or-nothing · and ALWAYS make it explicitly clear they are welcome to return, and arrange follow-up regardless.**

> [!warning] Distance, and the practicalities of being far from home
> **A patient in a tertiary hospital may be thousands of kilometres from Country, family and community, with no local support and no easy way home.**
> **Consider: Patient Assistance Transport Schemes and funding for ESCORTS — a patient travelling alone for major treatment is a substantial and avoidable stressor · accommodation for family · phone and video contact · and coordinating discharge so that medications, follow-up and transport actually work at the other end.**
> **A discharge plan that assumes a pharmacy, a GP and a car is not a plan for a patient returning to a remote community.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the SA patient transport scheme name, eligibility and application process.`

> [!tip] Language in the notes matters
> **Avoid deficit framing — "non-compliant", "poor historian", "failed to attend", "refused".**
> **Prefer descriptive and neutral language — "did not attend, transport unavailable", "we were not able to arrange an interpreter", "declined admission; barriers explored were X and Y".**
> **What you write is read by the next clinician and shapes how the patient is treated before they walk in the door.** **Documenting strengths, supports and the person's own goals is legitimate and useful.**

## 0.4 Conditions With Disproportionate Burden

> [!danger] Acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease
> **Australia has among the highest reported rates of ARF and RHD in the world among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in remote communities** — a condition essentially eliminated in most high-income settings.
> **The chain is: crowded housing and limited access to washing facilities → GROUP A STREPTOCOCCAL SKIN AND THROAT INFECTION → ARF → RHD → heart failure, stroke, endocarditis, pregnancy complications and premature death.**
> **The skin route matters as much as the throat**, which is why scabies and impetigo control is cardiac prevention. Cross-refer [[K2_Skin_and_Soft_Tissue_Infection]] 0.6.
> **The single most effective intervention is SECONDARY PROPHYLAXIS — regular long-acting benzathine penicillin injections for years — and adherence is the whole game.** **Every missed injection increases the risk of recurrence and further valve damage.**
> **Register-based control programs and echocardiographic screening exist.**
> **The intern-level point: know that ARF is notifiable, know that a patient on prophylaxis must not miss doses, and check whether an admitted patient with RHD is on a register and up to date with injections.** Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.10 and [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.1. `UNVERIFIED — diagnostic criteria (Jones, with Australian modifications), prophylaxis regimens and duration.`

> [!warning] Chronic kidney disease and end-stage kidney disease
> **Rates of end-stage kidney disease are dramatically higher**, driven by diabetes, hypertension, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, low birth weight (with reduced nephron endowment), and the social determinants above.
> **The consequence that dominates people's lives: DIALYSIS OFTEN REQUIRES RELOCATION AWAY FROM COUNTRY, family and community, sometimes permanently and sometimes thousands of kilometres.**
> **This is why some patients decline or discontinue dialysis, and it is a rational decision about a whole life rather than non-compliance.**
> **Models supporting dialysis on or closer to Country exist and matter enormously.** Cross-refer [[H3_Acute_Kidney_Injury_and_CKD]] and [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.6.

> [!warning] The other conditions with markedly higher burden
> **· TYPE 2 DIABETES — higher prevalence, EARLIER ONSET (including in adolescence), more complications, and higher rates of gestational diabetes.** **Screening therefore begins at a younger age.** Cross-refer [[I2_Diabetes_and_Glucose_Disorders]] 0.1.
> **· CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASE — earlier onset and higher mortality, so absolute risk assessment begins at a younger age.** Cross-refer [[B2_Hypertension_Spectrum]] 0.1 and [[GER3_Preventive_and_Occupational_Health]].
> **· OTITIS MEDIA — earlier onset, higher prevalence, more persistent and more often suppurative, with a chain of consequences that is under-appreciated: CONDUCTIVE HEARING LOSS → impaired language development → educational disadvantage → employment disadvantage → and demonstrated over-representation in the justice system.** **Detecting and treating middle ear disease in Aboriginal children is one of the highest-leverage interventions in Australian paediatrics.** Cross-refer [[F1_Ear]] and [[M2_Fever_in_Children]].
> **· SKIN INFECTIONS, SCABIES AND CRUSTED SCABIES** — cross-refer [[K2_Skin_and_Soft_Tissue_Infection]] 0.6.
> **· BRONCHIECTASIS AND CHRONIC SUPPURATIVE LUNG DISEASE — frequently beginning in childhood after severe or recurrent infection.** **A wet cough lasting more than four weeks in an Aboriginal child should not be dismissed.** Cross-refer [[RESP-X_Occupational_and_Chronic_Lung_Disease]] 0.4 and [[M2_Fever_in_Children]].
> **· TRACHOMA — Australia has been the only high-income country with endemic trachoma**, in remote central and northern communities, managed through the SAFE strategy (Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, Environmental improvement). Cross-refer [[E1_Red_and_Painful_Eye]].
> **· SEXUALLY TRANSMISSIBLE INFECTIONS, and the sustained INFECTIOUS SYPHILIS OUTBREAK across northern and central Australia with congenital syphilis cases.** Cross-refer [[O6_Sexual_and_Reproductive_Health]] 0.1.
> **· GOUT — higher prevalence, earlier onset, and markedly under-treated with urate-lowering therapy.** Cross-refer [[L1_Hot_and_Swollen_Joint]] 0.3.
> **· CANCER — later stage at diagnosis, lower screening participation, and poorer survival**, with access, cultural safety and competing priorities all contributing.
> **· EYE HEALTH — diabetic retinopathy, cataract, and higher rates of avoidable blindness with lower rates of treatment.**
> **· MATERNAL AND INFANT HEALTH — higher rates of preterm birth and low birth weight, with **Birthing on Country** and community-controlled maternity models showing meaningful improvements.**
> **· Region-specific infections: MELIOIDOSIS, STRONGYLOIDES and HTLV-1** in northern and central Australia. Cross-refer [[K1_Fever_Workup]] 0.4 and [[K3_Exposure__Tuberculosis__HIV_and_Immunodeficiency]] 0.5.

> [!danger] Social and emotional wellbeing, and suicide
> **Rates of psychological distress, self-harm and suicide are substantially higher, particularly among young people, and this is a source of profound grief in communities.**
> **Approach with great care:**
> **· The causes are grounded in intergenerational trauma, dispossession, racism and social disadvantage — not in individual pathology alone.**
> **· Do not pathologise grief or cultural expressions of distress.**
> **· CONNECTION TO CULTURE, COUNTRY, FAMILY AND COMMUNITY IS PROTECTIVE**, and supporting it is a legitimate part of a management plan.
> **· Involve Aboriginal mental health workers and SEWB services, and culturally appropriate crisis supports, rather than defaulting to mainstream pathways alone.**
> **· Be aware that a death in a community affects many people through kinship networks, and Sorry Business obligations follow.**
> Cross-refer [[N1_Mental_State_Examination_and_Risk]] and [[N4_Mood_Disorders]]. `UNVERIFIED — services, statistics and current strategies.`

## 0.5 Rural, Remote and Other Priority Populations

> [!tip] Rural and remote practice
> **Distance changes clinical decision-making.** With limited diagnostics, no immediate specialist support, and retrieval measured in hours, **the threshold for treating empirically, transferring early, and consulting by telehealth all shift.**
> **· Retrieval services — including the Royal Flying Doctor Service and state retrieval services — and knowing how to activate them.**
> **· Telehealth** — now embedded, and particularly valuable for specialist access.
> **· Delayed presentation and more advanced disease at diagnosis.**
> **· Higher rates of injury and trauma, with longer times to definitive care.**
> **· Workforce shortage and the rural generalist model**, with practitioners holding broader scopes than metropolitan equivalents.
> **The Modified Monash Model (MMM)** classifies geographic remoteness and underpins many workforce and funding programs. `UNVERIFIED — the classification and its applications.`

> [!danger] Culturally and linguistically diverse patients — use professional interpreters
> **Use a PROFESSIONAL interpreter, in person or by phone, for any clinically significant conversation where English proficiency is limited.**
> **DO NOT use family members**, and **NEVER use children.** Family members filter, soften, omit and editorialise; they may have their own interests in the conversation; and using a child as an interpreter for their parent's medical care is harmful to the child and unreliable for the clinician.
> **The excuses are always the same — it will take too long, the family member speaks good English, it's only a quick question — and they are how consent becomes invalid and diagnoses get missed.**
> **REFUGEE AND ASYLUM SEEKER HEALTH:** a comprehensive **refugee health assessment** is warranted, covering **catch-up immunisation, screening for tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C, HIV, syphilis, parasites including strongyloides and schistosomiasis, nutritional deficiencies (iron, vitamin D, B12), dental and vision, and mental health including experiences of torture and trauma.**
> **Some asylum seekers are NOT eligible for Medicare**, which is a substantial practical barrier — know which local services can help. `UNVERIFIED — current eligibility rules and refugee health services in SA.`

> [!warning] The other priority populations
> **· PEOPLE EXPERIENCING HOMELESSNESS — very high burden of chronic disease, mental illness, substance use and injury; the barriers are practical (storage of medicines, refrigeration, appointment times, identification, phone contact) and the discharge plan has to account for them.**
> **· PEOPLE IN PRISON AND RECENTLY RELEASED — high rates of chronic disease, blood-borne viruses, mental illness and substance use, with continuity of care disrupted at both entry and release. The period immediately after release carries a very high mortality risk, particularly from overdose.**
> **· LGBTIQA+ PEOPLE — cross-refer [[O6_Sexual_and_Reproductive_Health]] 0.6.**
> **· PEOPLE WITH DISABILITY — DIAGNOSTIC OVERSHADOWING, where new symptoms are attributed to the person's existing disability rather than investigated, is a documented and serious cause of missed diagnosis and premature death, particularly in intellectual disability.** **Investigate the symptom on its merits, seek collateral about what is different from baseline, and use accessible communication.**
> **· OLDER PEOPLE — cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] and [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]].**
> **· VETERANS — ask, because DVA access is separate and often better resourced.**

> [!tip] Health literacy — use teach-back
> **A large proportion of Australian adults have health literacy below the level needed to manage their own health confidently**, and it is not predictable from education or articulacy.
> **TEACH-BACK: "I want to make sure I've explained this clearly — can you tell me in your own words what you're going to do?"**
> **This places the responsibility on the explanation rather than the patient**, which is both more accurate and less shaming than "do you understand?" — a question to which the answer is almost always yes.

## 0.6 Practical Intern-Level Competencies

> [!danger] Ask the identification question — every patient, every time
> **"Are you of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander origin?"**
> **Ask it of EVERYONE, routinely, as a standard demographic question — not selectively based on appearance, which is both offensive and inaccurate.**
> **Why it matters practically:**
> **· It enables access to the CTG PBS Co-payment Programme, the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Assessment, and other specific programs and services.**
> **· It allows Aboriginal Liaison Officers and Health Workers to be involved.**
> **· It informs clinical thresholds — earlier screening for diabetes and cardiovascular risk, consideration of ARF and RHD, and other conditions with different epidemiology.**
> **· Accurate data underpins service planning and the Closing the Gap measurement framework.**
> **UNDER-IDENTIFICATION is a well-documented problem**, and it happens because clinicians feel awkward asking or assume they can tell. **Identification is based on descent, self-identification and community acceptance — not on appearance.**

> [!tip] The CTG PBS Co-payment Programme
> **Eligible Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients can receive PBS medicines at a reduced or nil co-payment**, which removes a genuine and significant barrier to adherence.
> **It requires the patient to be registered and the prescription to be annotated appropriately.**
> **Knowing this exists, checking whether your patient is registered, and annotating discharge prescriptions correctly is a concrete, high-value action that costs nothing.** `UNVERIFIED — current eligibility, registration process and annotation requirements.`

> [!warning] A social history that is actually useful
> Beyond occupation and smoking, ask about:
> **· HOUSING — where they live, who with, how many people, and whether it is stable and adequate.**
> **· FOOD SECURITY — "do you ever run out of food before you can buy more?"**
> **· TRANSPORT — how they got here, and how they will get to follow-up.**
> **· COST — of medicines, of appointments, of getting to them.**
> **· WHO IS AT HOME — dependants, carers, and who cares for them.**
> **· CONNECTION — family, community, and for Aboriginal patients, Country.**
> **These determine whether the plan you write is achievable, and a plan that is not achievable is not a plan.**

> [!tip] Discharge planning that accounts for reality
> **· Will they be able to GET the medicines — cost, pharmacy access, refrigeration, dose administration aids?**
> **· Will the FOLLOW-UP happen — is it at a service they can reach, and has the ACCHO or GP been informed?**
> **· TRANSPORT home and to appointments.**
> **· Communication — has the discharge summary gone to the right service, and does the patient have something they understand?**
> **· If they are returning to a remote community, has someone there been contacted?**
> **A discharge summary sent to a GP the patient does not see, prescribing medicines they cannot afford, with follow-up at a clinic they cannot reach, is a documented plan and no plan at all.**

> [!warning] Reflect on your own practice
> **· Notice your assumptions** — about who is likely to be adherent, who is likely to be drug-seeking, whose pain to believe. **Implicit bias is measurable, universal, and affects clinical decisions**, and the first defence is knowing you have it.
> **· Notice when you are documenting deficit rather than description.**
> **· Notice who you spend less time with, and why.**
> **· Ask for help from those who know more** — Aboriginal Health Workers and Liaison Officers, interpreters, and colleagues from the communities you serve.
> **· Undertake cultural safety training seriously rather than as a compliance exercise**, and recognise that it is ongoing rather than a certificate.
