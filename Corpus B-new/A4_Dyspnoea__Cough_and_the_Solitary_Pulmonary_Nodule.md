## 0.1 Acute Dyspnoea

**D:** Subjective awareness of uncomfortable breathing developing over minutes to days.

**A/P:** Dyspnoea arises from a mismatch between the respiratory drive generated centrally and the ventilation actually achieved — sensed through chemoreceptors, pulmonary stretch and irritant receptors, and chest wall mechanoreceptors. **This is why it is not a purely respiratory symptom**: metabolic acidosis, anaemia and anxiety all generate it through the same final pathway.

> [!tip] Time course narrows the differential faster than anything else
> **Seconds:** pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, inhaled foreign body, arrhythmia.
> **Minutes to hours:** asthma, acute pulmonary oedema, anaphylaxis, COPD exacerbation, aspiration.
> **Hours to days:** pneumonia, pleural effusion, decompensating heart failure, anaemia from bleeding.
> **Days to weeks:** malignancy, interstitial lung disease, chronic anaemia, progressive neuromuscular disease.
> Ask "what were you doing when it started, and how long did it take to reach this?" before anything else.

> [!tip] Differential by system, so nothing gets missed
> **Airway:** obstruction, foreign body, anaphylaxis — cross-refer [[A2_Airway_Compromise__Stridor_and_Tracheostomy_Emergencies]].
> **Lung:** pneumonia, PE, pneumothorax, effusion, asthma, COPD, ARDS, haemorrhage.
> **Cardiac:** acute pulmonary oedema, myocardial infarction (dyspnoea may be the anginal equivalent in diabetics, women and the elderly), tamponade, arrhythmia, valvular failure.
> **Metabolic:** any metabolic acidosis — DKA, sepsis, renal failure, salicylate. Cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.1.
> **Haematological:** acute anaemia, carbon monoxide poisoning.
> **Neuromuscular:** Guillain–Barré, myasthenic crisis — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.5.
> **Psychogenic:** a diagnosis of exclusion, never a first move.

> [!danger] Dyspnoea with a clear chest
> Normal auscultation and a normal chest radiograph do **not** reassure. **Pulmonary embolism, metabolic acidosis, anaemia, myocardial ischaemia and early neuromuscular failure all present with breathlessness and clear lungs.** If the chest is clear and the patient is breathless, widen the differential rather than narrowing it.

**S/Smx:** Increased respiratory rate and effort, accessory muscle use, inability to speak in full sentences, positional preference, chest pain, cough, wheeze, stridor, fever, leg swelling, and associated features that point at the system involved.

**Ix:** Oxygen saturation with inspired oxygen concentration recorded (*why:* a saturation without the FiO₂ alongside it cannot be interpreted; *what:* saturation with FiO₂). ABG or VBG with lactate (*why:* separates hypoxaemic from hypercapnic from metabolic causes, which is the first branch of the differential; *what:* PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, lactate). ECG (*why:* ischaemia, arrhythmia and right heart strain, and dyspnoea may be the only presentation of infarction in diabetics and the elderly; *what:* ischaemic change, arrhythmia, S1Q3T3 or strain pattern). CXR (*why:* the highest-yield single image — consolidation, oedema, pneumothorax, effusion; *what:* findings). Bedside lung ultrasound where available (*why:* faster and more sensitive than radiography at the bedside for pneumothorax, effusion and interstitial oedema, and it does not require moving the patient; *what:* absent lung sliding, B-lines, effusion, consolidation). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* separates cardiogenic from non-cardiogenic and identifies RV strain from PE; *what:* LV function, RV dilatation, pericardial fluid). FBC (*why:* anaemia causes dyspnoea with normal saturation; *what:* haemoglobin). BNP or NT-proBNP where the cardiac-versus-respiratory question is genuinely open (*why:* a normal value makes heart failure unlikely; *what:* elevation — `UNVERIFIED — cut-offs, and the effect of renal impairment, obesity and AF on them`). D-dimer or CTPA per pre-test probability (*why:* PE is the most frequently missed cause of acute dyspnoea; *what:* filling defect). Bedside glucose and ketones (*why:* DKA presents as breathlessness, sometimes with no other respiratory complaint; *what:* hyperglycaemia with ketosis).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E, oxygen to target, sit upright, monitoring, IV access. Treat the identified cause. Decompress a tension pneumothorax on clinical grounds without waiting for imaging.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] and [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]].

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Address the underlying condition and its preventer or maintenance therapy.

## 0.2 Chronic and Chronic Progressive Dyspnoea

**D:** Breathlessness present over weeks to months, either stable or worsening.

**A/P:** Chronic dyspnoea reflects a persistent limitation in the oxygen delivery chain — ventilation, gas exchange, oxygen carriage, or cardiac output — or an increase in the work required to achieve it. **Deconditioning and obesity are genuine and extremely common contributors** and should be identified rather than assumed once organic causes are addressed.

> [!tip] Cardiac versus respiratory — the discriminating features
> | | Points cardiac | Points respiratory |
> |---|---|---|
> | Orthopnoea, PND | Strongly | Occasionally (COPD, large effusion) |
> | Wheeze | "Cardiac asthma" possible | Common |
> | Sputum | Pink frothy in acute oedema | Chronic, purulent, or large-volume |
> | Ankle oedema | Common | Only with cor pulmonale |
> | Exercise pattern | Fatigue and breathlessness together | Breathlessness with preserved leg strength |
> | Response to diuretic | Improves | Does not |
> The two coexist frequently in older patients, and identifying one does not exclude the other.

> [!warning] Chronic *progressive* dyspnoea deserves a different threshold
> Relentless worsening over months, particularly with a dry cough and clubbing, suggests **interstitial lung disease**. Progressive dyspnoea with a normal chest radiograph and normal spirometry suggests **pulmonary hypertension**, which is characteristically diagnosed late because early investigation is unremarkable. Progressive dyspnoea with weight loss suggests malignancy. Progressive dyspnoea with limb or bulbar weakness suggests neuromuscular disease — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.5.

> [!info] Grading it
> The MRC or modified MRC dyspnoea scale grades functional limitation and is used in COPD assessment and pulmonary rehabilitation referral. `UNVERIFIED — the grade descriptors and the version used in Australian practice require verification.` A practical alternative in any consultation: ask what the patient can no longer do that they could a year ago, and how many stairs or metres they manage now.

> [!tip] Platypnoea-orthodeoxia — the reverse pattern
> Dyspnoea and desaturation **worse when upright and better lying flat**. Rare, and almost always significant — hepatopulmonary syndrome, intracardiac shunt through a patent foramen ovale, or basal arteriovenous malformations. Worth knowing because the pattern is so distinctive that recognising it essentially makes the diagnosis.

**S/Smx:** Exertional breathlessness with declining exercise tolerance, cough, wheeze, chest pain, orthopnoea, ankle swelling, weight loss, fatigue, and on examination clubbing, crackles, wheeze, raised JVP, murmurs, or signs of anaemia.

**Ix:** Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility (*why:* separates obstructive from restrictive physiology and identifies reversible airflow limitation, and it is the single most useful outpatient test; *what:* FEV1/FVC ratio, reversibility). Full pulmonary function tests with lung volumes and diffusing capacity (*why:* DLCO is reduced in emphysema, ILD and pulmonary vascular disease and preserved in asthma and chest wall disease, which discriminates when spirometry alone is equivocal; *what:* reduced DLCO, restrictive volumes). CXR (*why:* first-line structural assessment; *what:* interstitial change, mass, effusion, cardiomegaly). High-resolution CT chest where ILD is suspected (*why:* the pattern of fibrosis is diagnostic in itself in many cases and CXR is insensitive early; *what:* reticulation, honeycombing, ground glass, distribution). Echocardiography (*why:* assesses systolic and diastolic function, valves, and estimates pulmonary artery pressure — the screening test for pulmonary hypertension; *what:* ejection fraction, diastolic parameters, estimated PA pressure). FBC and iron studies (*why:* anaemia is a common and eminently treatable cause; *what:* haemoglobin, ferritin). TFTs (*why:* both hypo- and hyperthyroidism cause exertional breathlessness; *what:* TSH). BNP (*why:* screening for heart failure in the undifferentiated patient; *what:* elevation). ECG (*why:* ischaemia, hypertrophy, right heart strain; *what:* findings). Six-minute walk test with oximetry (*why:* demonstrates exertional desaturation that resting saturation entirely misses, and quantifies functional capacity objectively; *what:* distance, desaturation on exertion).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Rarely needed unless acutely decompensated.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Respiratory or cardiology referral where the diagnosis is unclear after first-line investigation.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Pulmonary rehabilitation** for chronic respiratory disease — it improves exercise capacity and quality of life more reliably than most drugs and is under-referred. Smoking cessation, weight management, treatment of anaemia, vaccination, and management of breathlessness itself in advanced disease including the palliative use of opioids and handheld fans.

## 0.3 Orthopnoea and Paroxysmal Nocturnal Dyspnoea

**D:** Breathlessness on lying flat (orthopnoea), and breathlessness waking the patient from sleep (PND).

**A/P:** Lying flat redistributes blood from the splanchnic circulation and lower limbs into the thorax → increased venous return → raised left atrial and pulmonary capillary pressure → in a failing ventricle that cannot accommodate the extra preload, interstitial pulmonary oedema develops → dyspnoea. Supine posture also raises the diaphragm and reduces functional residual capacity, which is why diaphragmatic weakness, massive ascites and severe obesity produce the same symptom by an entirely mechanical route.
**PND** classically occurs one to two hours after falling asleep — reabsorption of interstitial fluid continues after lying down, and nocturnal reduction in sympathetic tone reduces ventricular support. The patient wakes gasping, sits or stands at a window, and improves over roughly half an hour, which distinguishes it from the near-instantaneous relief of sitting up in orthopnoea.

> [!tip] Quantify it crudely but usefully
> **Ask how many pillows**, and more importantly whether that number has changed. "Three pillows, same as always" is far less informative than "two for years, four for the last fortnight." Ask whether they now sleep in a chair — that is severe orthopnoea and is frequently not volunteered.

> [!warning] Not all orthopnoea is heart failure
> **Large bilateral pleural effusions · massive ascites · severe obesity · bilateral diaphragmatic palsy** (breathless immediately on lying flat, with paradoxical abdominal movement) · **severe COPD** · **large goitre or mediastinal mass.** Nocturnal breathlessness also occurs in asthma (with wheeze and cough, typically in the early hours), obstructive sleep apnoea (with snoring and witnessed apnoeas, and the patient usually settles quickly), and gastro-oesophageal reflux (with acid taste and cough).

> [!tip] Distinguishing nocturnal breathlessness
> | | Timing | Associated | Relief |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **PND** | 1–2 h after sleep onset | Cough, froth, palpitations | Sitting or standing, over ~30 min |
> | **Asthma** | Early hours | Wheeze, dry cough | Bronchodilator |
> | **OSA** | Repeatedly, any time | Snoring, witnessed apnoea, choking | Settles in seconds |
> | **Reflux** | After late meals, on lying | Acid taste, cough, hoarseness | Sitting, antacid |

**S/Smx:** As above, with the associated features of the underlying cause — raised JVP, third heart sound, basal crackles and peripheral oedema in heart failure.

**Ix:** Echocardiography (*why:* the definitive assessment of the ventricular and valvular function that produces cardiac orthopnoea, and it distinguishes systolic from diastolic failure; *what:* ejection fraction, diastolic parameters, valve lesions). BNP or NT-proBNP (*why:* a normal value substantially reduces the likelihood of heart failure in an undifferentiated patient; *what:* elevation). CXR (*why:* identifies pulmonary congestion, effusions, cardiomegaly and the raised hemidiaphragm of phrenic palsy; *what:* upper lobe diversion, effusions, elevated diaphragm). ECG (*why:* ischaemia, AF and hypertrophy; *what:* findings). Supine and erect spirometry (*why:* a marked fall in FVC on lying flat is characteristic of diaphragmatic weakness and is otherwise easily missed; *what:* postural fall in FVC). Sleep study where OSA is suspected (*why:* confirms and grades, and CPAP is highly effective; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index). UEC, FBC, TFTs (*why:* renal failure, anaemia and thyroid disease all contribute; *what:* derangement). Weight and fluid balance charting (*why:* documents the fluid accumulation that drives symptoms and tracks response; *what:* weight trend).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Sit upright, oxygen if hypoxaemic, and manage as acute pulmonary oedema if that is the presentation — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.3.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Diuresis and guideline-directed therapy for heart failure. Drainage of large effusions. CPAP for OSA.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Daily weights with a written action plan, fluid and salt advice, and medication adherence review. A patient who knows to act on a two-kilogram weight gain prevents an admission.

## 0.4 Acute Cough

**D:** Cough of less than about three weeks' duration. `UNVERIFIED — the duration boundaries separating acute, subacute and chronic cough require verification.`

**R/Causes:** Viral upper respiratory tract infection is by far the commonest. Also: acute bronchitis, pneumonia, asthma exacerbation, COPD exacerbation, **pertussis**, aspiration, inhaled foreign body, pulmonary embolism, acute pulmonary oedema, and irritant or smoke inhalation.

**A/P:** Irritant receptors in the larynx, trachea and large bronchi are stimulated by inflammation, secretions, foreign material or mechanical distortion → afferent vagal signalling to the brainstem cough centre → the reflex sequence of inspiration, glottic closure, expiratory effort and explosive release.

> [!warning] Pertussis is under-diagnosed in adults and it matters
> **Paroxysms of coughing, post-tussive vomiting, and an inspiratory whoop** — though adults frequently have paroxysms and vomiting without a whoop, and often present as "a cough that won't go away" at several weeks. **Adults and adolescents are the reservoir that infects unimmunised infants**, in whom pertussis causes apnoea and death. It is a **notifiable disease** in Australia, and household contact management including infant prophylaxis matters. `UNVERIFIED — diagnostic method by stage of illness (PCR versus serology), antibiotic regimens, exclusion periods and contact prophylaxis all require verification against eTG and SA Health guidance.`

> [!tip] Post-infectious cough
> A dry cough persisting for weeks after a viral illness resolves is common, benign and self-limiting, caused by transient airway hyperresponsiveness. It needs explanation and reassurance rather than repeated antibiotics — but only once the red flags below are absent.

> [!danger] Red flags in acute cough
> Haemoptysis · breathlessness at rest · pleuritic chest pain · fever with focal chest signs · unilateral wheeze (foreign body) · choking episode · weight loss · immunosuppression · stridor.

**S/Smx:** Cough with or without sputum, coryza, sore throat, fever, wheeze, chest pain, and the paroxysmal pattern of pertussis.

**Ix:** Usually none in an otherwise well patient with a clear viral picture (*why:* investigating uncomplicated viral cough adds cost and radiation without changing management; *what:* not indicated). CXR where red flags are present, focal signs are found, or the patient is systemically unwell (*why:* identifies pneumonia, malignancy, effusion and foreign body complications; *what:* consolidation, mass, collapse). Nasopharyngeal swab for respiratory viral and pertussis PCR (*why:* confirms pertussis, which triggers notification and contact management, and identifies influenza and COVID-19 where antivirals or isolation apply; *what:* pathogen). Oxygen saturation (*why:* separates uncomplicated cough from lower respiratory tract involvement; *what:* hypoxia). Sputum culture where purulent and the patient is unwell or has chronic lung disease (*why:* directs therapy and identifies resistant organisms in bronchiectasis; *what:* organism). Spirometry after resolution where asthma is suspected (*why:* cough may be the sole asthma symptom; *what:* reversible obstruction).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Symptomatic measures. Antibiotics only where bacterial infection is likely — **most acute cough is viral and antibiotics do not shorten it.** Treat asthma or COPD exacerbation where that is the cause.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Notify and manage contacts for pertussis.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Immunisation, including adult pertussis boosters in those around infants — pregnant women and household contacts. Smoking cessation.

## 0.5 Chronic Cough

**D:** Cough persisting beyond about eight weeks in an adult. `UNVERIFIED — the duration threshold and the paediatric definition require verification.`

> [!tip] The big three in a non-smoker with a normal chest radiograph
> **Upper airway cough syndrome** (formerly post-nasal drip) — throat clearing, sensation of dripping, cobblestone posterior pharynx.
> **Asthma, including cough-variant asthma** — cough may be the *only* symptom, with no wheeze; nocturnal and exercise-related, and diagnosed by demonstrating variable airflow obstruction or airway hyperresponsiveness.
> **Gastro-oesophageal reflux** — may be entirely "silent", with no heartburn at all, presenting only as cough and hoarseness.
> These three account for the large majority, they frequently coexist, and treatment is often sequential empirical trials.

> [!danger] Check the medication list before anything else
> **ACE inhibitor cough** occurs in a substantial minority, is dry and tickly, and can begin **months to years** after starting the drug — which is why patients and clinicians both fail to connect the two. It resolves after cessation, though this can take weeks. Ceasing the ACE inhibitor is a free diagnostic test and should be done before an extensive workup. An ARB is the usual substitute.

> [!warning] Red flags mandating imaging and referral
> Haemoptysis · weight loss · smoking history, particularly over 40 · persistent hoarseness · dysphagia · recurrent pneumonia in the same location · clubbing · focal chest signs · night sweats and fever (tuberculosis) · immunosuppression · **large-volume daily sputum production** (bronchiectasis) · occupational dust or asbestos exposure.

**R/Causes beyond the big three:** Smoking and chronic bronchitis · bronchiectasis · lung cancer · interstitial lung disease · tuberculosis · non-asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis · obstructive sleep apnoea · chronic aspiration · habit or tic cough in children · and chronic cough hypersensitivity syndrome, where the reflex itself is sensitised.

**S/Smx:** Character (dry versus productive), timing (nocturnal, on waking, exertional), triggers, associated throat clearing, heartburn, wheeze, and systemic features.

**Ix:** CXR in all cases of genuinely chronic cough (*why:* the baseline test that excludes malignancy, tuberculosis and interstitial disease, and its normality directs you toward the big three; *what:* mass, infiltrate, bronchiectatic change). Spirometry with reversibility (*why:* identifies the asthma that may present as cough alone; *what:* obstruction with reversibility). Bronchial provocation testing where spirometry is normal and asthma is still suspected (*why:* cough-variant asthma has normal baseline spirometry by definition and is otherwise undiagnosable; *what:* airway hyperresponsiveness). Medication review, specifically for ACE inhibitors (*why:* free, instant, and a common answer; *what:* ACE inhibitor on the list). ENT assessment and nasendoscopy (*why:* identifies upper airway cough syndrome and laryngeal pathology; *what:* post-nasal secretions, laryngeal changes). High-resolution CT chest (*why:* the diagnostic test for bronchiectasis and interstitial lung disease, both of which CXR misses; *what:* bronchial dilatation, tree-in-bud, reticulation). Sputum for culture, cytology and mycobacterial studies where indicated (*why:* tuberculosis and non-tuberculous mycobacteria present exactly this way and require specific testing; *what:* acid-fast bacilli, culture). Induced sputum eosinophil count where available (*why:* identifies eosinophilic bronchitis, which responds to inhaled steroids despite normal spirometry; *what:* sputum eosinophilia). Trial of proton pump inhibitor and reflux measures (*why:* a therapeutic trial is often more practical than pH studies; *what:* symptomatic response over weeks).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Rarely required. Stop the ACE inhibitor if present.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Sequential empirical treatment of the big three where the chest radiograph is normal, with each trial given adequate duration before moving on. Specialist referral where red flags are present or treatment fails.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Smoking cessation. Speech pathology for cough suppression therapy in refractory cough hypersensitivity, which has reasonable evidence and is under-used. Airway clearance and specialist follow-up in bronchiectasis.

## 0.6 Productive Cough and Sputum Character

**D:** Cough producing sputum, where the volume, character and pattern narrow the differential considerably.

**A/P:** Airway inflammation increases mucus production from goblet cells and submucosal glands, while impaired mucociliary clearance — from smoking, infection, structural damage or ciliary dysfunction — prevents its removal. **The combination produces the retention that both causes cough and predisposes to further infection**, which is the vicious cycle underlying bronchiectasis.

> [!tip] Sputum character and what it suggests
> | Appearance | Suggests |
> |---|---|
> | Clear or white mucoid | Viral, chronic bronchitis, asthma |
> | Yellow or green purulent | Bacterial infection, or simply neutrophils — **purulence alone does not prove bacterial infection** |
> | **Large volume, daily, worse on waking or with posture** | **Bronchiectasis** |
> | Rust-coloured | Classically pneumococcal pneumonia |
> | "Currant jelly" | Classically *Klebsiella* — memorable but unreliable in practice |
> | Foul-smelling, putrid | Anaerobic infection — lung abscess, aspiration |
> | Pink and frothy | Acute pulmonary oedema |
> | Black | Coal dust, smoke inhalation, injecting drug use |
> | Frank blood | See haemoptysis — always investigate |

> [!danger] Chronic daily large-volume sputum is bronchiectasis until proven otherwise
> This is the discriminator most often missed. A patient describing daily sputum production for years, recurrent chest infections, and sputum that is worse in certain positions has bronchiectasis, and **the diagnosis requires HRCT — chest radiography is not sensitive enough.** Once diagnosed, airway clearance, exacerbation plans and a search for an underlying cause (cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, prior severe infection, aspiration) change management substantially.

> [!warning] Haemoptysis is never a symptom to observe
> Distinguish it from haematemesis (dark, acidic, food particles, preceding nausea) and from bleeding from the nose or gums. Causes: bronchiectasis, malignancy, tuberculosis, pneumonia, PE, pulmonary oedema, anticoagulation, vasculitis. **Massive haemoptysis is an emergency** — the risk is asphyxiation rather than exsanguination, so position the patient with the bleeding side **down** to protect the good lung, and involve respiratory, interventional radiology and thoracic surgery urgently.

**S/Smx:** Volume, colour, odour, timing, positional variation, and presence of blood. Associated fever, weight loss, dyspnoea and chest pain.

**Ix:** Sputum culture and sensitivities (*why:* directs therapy, and in bronchiectasis identifies chronic colonisers such as *Pseudomonas* that change both antibiotic choice and prognosis; *what:* organism, sensitivities). Sputum for acid-fast bacilli and mycobacterial culture where indicated (*why:* tuberculosis and non-tuberculous mycobacteria require specific stains and prolonged culture and are missed on routine testing; *what:* AFB, culture). CXR (*why:* first-line for consolidation, abscess, mass; *what:* findings). High-resolution CT chest where bronchiectasis is suspected (*why:* the diagnostic test, and chest radiography is insufficiently sensitive; *what:* bronchial dilatation exceeding the accompanying artery, wall thickening, tree-in-bud). FBC and inflammatory markers (*why:* infection and eosinophilia, the latter suggesting ABPA; *what:* leucocytosis, eosinophilia). Immunoglobulins and, in appropriate patients, sweat test and CFTR genetics (*why:* immunodeficiency and cystic fibrosis are treatable underlying causes of bronchiectasis and are diagnosed in adulthood more often than expected; *what:* low immunoglobulins, positive sweat test). *Aspergillus* IgE and specific IgG where ABPA is considered (*why:* it is a treatable cause of bronchiectasis in asthmatics; *what:* raised total and specific IgE). Bronchoscopy where a localised abnormality, obstruction or unexplained haemoptysis (*why:* visualises and samples endobronchial lesions; *what:* tumour, foreign body, bleeding source).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat infection where present. For massive haemoptysis: airway protection, bleeding side down, oxygen, correct coagulopathy, urgent specialist involvement.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Antibiotics guided by culture. Bronchial artery embolisation for massive haemoptysis. Treat the underlying cause of bronchiectasis.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Airway clearance techniques with physiotherapy input** are the mainstay in bronchiectasis and are consistently under-prescribed. Exacerbation action plan, vaccination, exercise, and long-term macrolide therapy in selected patients. Smoking cessation.

## 0.7 Solitary Pulmonary Nodule

**D:** A single rounded opacity in the lung, up to approximately 3 cm, surrounded by aerated lung, without associated lymphadenopathy, atelectasis or effusion. `UNVERIFIED — the size boundary defining nodule versus mass requires verification.` A larger lesion is a mass and carries a substantially higher probability of malignancy.

**A/P:** Nodules represent granuloma, hamartoma, infection, primary malignancy, metastasis, vascular malformation or inflammatory lesion. Most are incidental findings on imaging performed for another reason, and the frequency has risen substantially with CT use. **The clinical task is not to diagnose the nodule but to stratify the probability of malignancy and choose the least invasive appropriate follow-up.**

> [!tip] Features favouring benign versus malignant
> **Benign:** small size · smooth well-defined margins · **benign calcification patterns — central, laminated, diffuse, or "popcorn" (characteristic of hamartoma)** · fat density (also hamartoma) · **documented stability over an adequate interval on previous imaging** · younger patient, never-smoker.
> **Malignant:** larger size · **spiculated or lobulated margins** · upper lobe location · part-solid or ground-glass with a solid component · growth on serial imaging · older age, smoking history, previous malignancy, asbestos or radon exposure, family history · associated weight loss or haemoptysis.
> **Eccentric or stippled calcification does not indicate benignity** — malignancy can engulf a pre-existing calcified granuloma.

> [!danger] The highest-yield single action is finding old imaging
> **A nodule that is unchanged over an adequate period on prior films is almost certainly benign, and finding those films can end the workup immediately.** Chase previous chest radiographs and CTs from other hospitals, the GP, and interstate before ordering anything new. This is the step most often skipped and the one that saves the most investigation, cost and anxiety.

> [!warning] Follow-up intervals are guideline-driven and must be read, not recalled
> The **Fleischner Society** recommendations stratify follow-up by nodule size, solid versus subsolid versus ground-glass character, single versus multiple, and patient risk category. `UNVERIFIED — every size threshold, follow-up interval and duration of surveillance is omitted from this file. Obtain the current Fleischner Society guidance and your local radiology department's protocol. These figures are revised periodically, are not intuitive, and reconstructing them from memory is exactly the failure mode this corpus is guarding against.` Note also that the Fleischner criteria do not apply to patients under a certain age, those with known malignancy, or immunocompromised patients, all of whom are managed differently.

> [!info] Australian context
> Granulomatous nodules from prior tuberculosis or non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection occur, and the endemic fungal causes common in North America are not a significant local consideration. Consider prior TB exposure, particularly in patients born overseas. Lung cancer screening programs have implications for how incidental nodules are handled — `UNVERIFIED — confirm the current status and eligibility of the Australian National Lung Cancer Screening Program, which is recent and may have changed.`

**S/Smx:** Usually asymptomatic and incidental. Where symptoms exist — cough, haemoptysis, weight loss, chest pain — the probability of malignancy rises and the lesion is no longer being managed as an incidental nodule.

**Ix:** Retrieval and comparison with all available prior imaging (*why:* documented stability is the fastest route to a benign conclusion and requires no new test; *what:* unchanged size and morphology over an adequate interval). Thin-section CT chest (*why:* characterises margins, density, calcification pattern and precise size far better than a chest radiograph, and the morphology drives risk stratification; *what:* spiculation, calcification pattern, solid versus subsolid, fat density). Risk assessment incorporating age, smoking history, nodule size and location, and prior malignancy (*why:* the follow-up pathway is explicitly risk-stratified and applying the wrong category leads to over- or under-investigation; *what:* estimated malignancy probability). PET-CT for nodules above a threshold size (*why:* metabolic activity supports or reduces suspicion and simultaneously stages the mediastinum, though small nodules and some indolent tumours are falsely negative and infection is falsely positive; *what:* FDG avidity — `UNVERIFIED — the size threshold below which PET is unreliable`). CT-guided percutaneous biopsy or bronchoscopic sampling (*why:* provides tissue where the lesion is accessible and the result will change management; *what:* histology — with pneumothorax a recognised complication of the percutaneous route). Surgical resection, usually video-assisted thoracoscopic wedge resection (*why:* both diagnostic and therapeutic where suspicion is high; *what:* histology and definitive treatment). Sputum cytology, of limited value (*why:* poor sensitivity for peripheral lesions and rarely changes management; *what:* malignant cells if central and large).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
None. This is not an emergency, and communicating that to an understandably anxious patient is part of the job — an incidental nodule is far more often benign than not.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Surveillance imaging per guideline, tissue sampling, or resection depending on risk category. **Discussion at a lung cancer multidisciplinary meeting** for anything indeterminate or suspicious.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Smoking cessation, which changes the risk of both this nodule and the next one. Completion of the surveillance interval — **nodules lost to follow-up are a recognised source of delayed cancer diagnosis**, so ensure the follow-up is explicitly booked and communicated to the GP in writing rather than left as a recommendation in a radiology report.
