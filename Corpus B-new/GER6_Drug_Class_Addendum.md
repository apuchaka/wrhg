## 0.1 Safe Prescribing and the High-Risk Medicines

> [!danger] APINCH — the high-risk medicine classification used in Australian hospitals
> **A — ANTIMICROBIALS**
> **P — POTASSIUM and other electrolytes (concentrated potassium is a classic never-event drug)**
> **I — INSULIN**
> **N — NARCOTICS (opioids) and other sedatives**
> **C — CHEMOTHERAPEUTIC agents**
> **H — HEPARIN and anticoagulants**
> **These classes account for a disproportionate share of serious medication harm, and they attract additional checking requirements in most Australian health services.** **Knowing the mnemonic is less important than knowing that when you are prescribing from one of these groups, the error rate and the consequence are both higher — so slow down and check.** `UNVERIFIED — your health service's specific high-risk medicine policy.`

> [!warning] The writing errors that cause tenfold overdoses
> **· NEVER ABBREVIATE "UNITS" AS "U" OR "IU".** **A "U" is read as a zero — "10U" becomes "100". This has killed people, repeatedly, and it is the single most important prescribing abbreviation rule.** Write "units" in full.
> **· NEVER USE A TRAILING ZERO** ("1.0 mg" reads as 10 mg if the decimal point is missed). **ALWAYS USE A LEADING ZERO** ("0.5 mg", never ".5 mg").
> **· Write micrograms in FULL — not "mcg" or "µg".**
> **· Beware LOOK-ALIKE, SOUND-ALIKE names.**
> **· PAEDIATRIC WEIGHT-BASED DOSING — calculate, have it independently checked, and never do it from memory.** Cross-refer [[M1_The_Seriously_Unwell_Child]] 0.3.
> **· Avoid VERBAL ORDERS where possible; where unavoidable, write them down and read them back.**
> **THE SEVEN THINGS ON EVERY PRESCRIPTION: right patient · drug · dose · route · frequency · INDICATION · and DURATION or review date.** **The last two are the ones routinely omitted, and their absence is why medications continue for years after the reason has gone.**

> [!tip] Before prescribing anything, check five things
> **1. ALLERGY STATUS — and WHAT THE REACTION ACTUALLY WAS.** A rash in childhood is not the same as anaphylaxis, and most penicillin allergy labels are wrong. Cross-refer [[K4_Allergy_and_Clinical_Immunology]] 0.3 and [[G1_Acute_Rash_and_Erythema]] 0.4.
> **2. RENAL FUNCTION AND WEIGHT** — the two variables that most commonly require dose adjustment, and the two most often not checked.
> **3. HEPATIC FUNCTION.**
> **4. PREGNANCY, POSSIBLE PREGNANCY OR BREASTFEEDING.** Cross-refer [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]] 0.6.
> **5. THE REST OF THE DRUG CHART — for interactions, for duplication, and for the anticholinergic burden.** See 0.6.
> **AND MEDICATION RECONCILIATION AT EVERY TRANSITION** — admission, transfer, discharge. **Most medication errors originate at a transition of care.** Cross-refer [[EBM1_Evidence_and_Clinical_Process]] 0.5.

## 0.2 NaSSAs and Cardiac Glycosides

> [!tip] NaSSAs — mirtazapine, and the counterintuitive dose effect
> **NORADRENERGIC AND SPECIFIC SEROTONERGIC ANTIDEPRESSANT — the class is essentially MIRTAZAPINE.**
> **MECHANISM: central alpha-2 adrenoceptor ANTAGONISM increases noradrenaline and serotonin release; 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 antagonism accounts for the relative absence of the sexual dysfunction and nausea that limit SSRIs; and potent H1 ANTAGONISM causes the sedation and appetite stimulation.**
> **WHERE IT IS GENUINELY USEFUL: depression with prominent INSOMNIA and WEIGHT LOSS or poor appetite — which makes it a sensible choice in older, frail, palliative or cachectic patients where those effects are wanted rather than tolerated.** **The low rate of sexual dysfunction is also a real advantage, and matters for adherence.** Cross-refer [[N4_Mood_Disorders]] 0.3.
> **THE COUNTERINTUITIVE POINT: MIRTAZAPINE IS MORE SEDATING AT LOW DOSES THAN AT HIGHER ONES.**
> **At low dose the antihistamine effect predominates; as the dose rises, increased noradrenergic transmission offsets it.** **So a patient who is intolerably drowsy may need the dose INCREASED, not decreased — which is the opposite of the usual instinct and is regularly got wrong.**
> **Other effects: WEIGHT GAIN (substantial, and a common reason for stopping) · dry mouth · rare AGRANULOCYTOSIS, so a fever or sore throat warrants a blood count · and hyponatraemia, as with other antidepressants in older patients.**

> [!danger] Cardiac glycosides — digoxin, and hypokalaemia is the trap
> **MECHANISM: inhibition of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase raises intracellular sodium, which reduces calcium efflux via the sodium-calcium exchanger, raising intracellular calcium and increasing contractility. Separately, vagal enhancement slows AV nodal conduction.**
> **USES: rate control in ATRIAL FIBRILLATION — particularly in sedentary patients and those with heart failure, though it controls resting rate better than exertional rate · and symptomatic benefit in HFrEF, where it reduces hospitalisation but NOT mortality.** Cross-refer [[B3_Arrhythmia__Bradycardia_and_Cardiac_Devices]] 0.4 and [[CV-X_Chronic_Heart_Failure]] 0.3.
> **HYPOKALAEMIA POTENTIATES DIGOXIN TOXICITY, AND THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT PRACTICAL POINT.**
> **Digoxin and potassium COMPETE for the same binding site on the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — so when potassium is low, digoxin binds more avidly and toxicity occurs at a "therapeutic" level.**
> **This matters enormously because the patients on digoxin are frequently also on LOOP OR THIAZIDE DIURETICS, which cause hypokalaemia. Checking the potassium is part of monitoring the digoxin.** **Hypomagnesaemia and hypercalcaemia also potentiate it.**
> **TOXICITY: anorexia, nausea and vomiting (often the earliest and easily attributed to something else) · confusion, particularly in the elderly · VISUAL disturbance — blurring, and classically XANTHOPSIA (yellow-green vision) and haloes · and ARRHYTHMIAS.** **Digoxin can cause almost any arrhythmia; BIDIRECTIONAL VENTRICULAR TACHYCARDIA is the classic but rare one, and the combination of an atrial tachycardia with AV block is characteristic.**
> **RISK FACTORS: RENAL IMPAIRMENT (digoxin is renally cleared, so an AKI precipitates toxicity in a stable patient) · age · low body weight · and electrolyte disturbance.**
> **INTERACTIONS that raise the level: AMIODARONE · VERAPAMIL · macrolides · quinine · and others.** **Starting amiodarone in a digitalised patient usually requires a dose reduction.**
> **TREATMENT of significant toxicity is DIGOXIN-SPECIFIC ANTIBODY FRAGMENTS (Fab).** **And note: in digoxin toxicity with hyperkalaemia, CALCIUM is traditionally avoided** — the concern being that adding calcium to an already calcium-loaded myocyte worsens the situation, though the evidence is debated. `UNVERIFIED — levels, sampling timing (which must be several hours post-dose or the result is meaningless), and toxicity management.`

## 0.3 Weak Opioids and Muscle Relaxants

> [!danger] Codeine is a prodrug, and the metabolism is unpredictable
> **CODEINE HAS NO MEANINGFUL ANALGESIC ACTIVITY OF ITS OWN.** **It is a prodrug requiring conversion to MORPHINE by CYP2D6 — and CYP2D6 activity varies enormously between individuals for genetic reasons.**
> **· POOR METABOLISERS convert very little and get NO ANALGESIA — they are not "drug-seeking" when they report it does not work.**
> **· ULTRA-RAPID METABOLISERS convert far more than expected and receive an effectively large dose of morphine.**
> **THE CONSEQUENCES ARE NOT THEORETICAL:**
> **· CHILDREN HAVE DIED from codeine given after tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy — ultra-rapid metabolisers with obstructive sleep apnoea receiving an unexpectedly large opioid dose.** **Codeine is contraindicated in children.**
> **· BREASTFED INFANTS have died when the mother was an ultra-rapid metaboliser and morphine accumulated in breast milk.** **Avoid codeine in breastfeeding.**
> **IN AUSTRALIA, CODEINE-CONTAINING PRODUCTS WERE UPSCHEDULED TO PRESCRIPTION-ONLY** — over-the-counter codeine combinations are no longer available, a change made because of harm from long-term low-dose use, including analgesic nephropathy, dependence and gastrointestinal bleeding from the paracetamol or ibuprofen component. `UNVERIFIED — the current scheduling.`
> **TRAMADOL — a dual mechanism agent: a weak opioid AND a serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor.**
> **The consequences of the second mechanism: it LOWERS THE SEIZURE THRESHOLD (relevant in epilepsy, head injury and overdose) · it causes SEROTONIN TOXICITY in combination with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, triptans and linezolid (cross-refer [[A6_Thermal_and_Environmental_Injury]] 0.3) · and it is also CYP2D6-dependent with the same variability as codeine.**
> **THE BROADER POINT: "WEAK OPIOID" IS A MISLEADING CATEGORY.** **A small dose of a predictable strong opioid is often safer and more effective than a weak opioid with variable metabolism and additional pharmacology — and the intermediate step of the analgesic ladder is increasingly questioned.** Cross-refer [[F0-4_Resuscitation_Algorithms_and_Emergency_Procedures]] 0.9 and [[J5_Oncological_Emergencies_and_Palliative_Care]] 0.5.

> [!warning] Muscle relaxants — two very different groups
> **1. CENTRALLY ACTING ANTISPASTICITY AGENTS:**
> **· BACLOFEN — a GABA-B agonist used for spasticity in multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury and cerebral palsy, orally or by intrathecal pump.**
> **THE CRITICAL POINT: ABRUPT BACLOFEN WITHDRAWAL — especially from an INTRATHECAL PUMP (failure, disconnection, battery, empty reservoir) — CAUSES A LIFE-THREATENING SYNDROME with high fever, severe rebound rigidity and spasticity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, rhabdomyolysis and death.** **It closely mimics neuroleptic malignant syndrome and sepsis, and it is missed unless someone asks whether the patient has a pump.** **It is a medical emergency requiring urgent restoration of baclofen.**
> **· TIZANIDINE (alpha-2 agonist; hypotension, sedation, hepatotoxicity) · DIAZEPAM · and DANTROLENE.**
> **· DANTROLENE acts directly on skeletal muscle by inhibiting calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum — which is why it is the antidote for MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA and is used in neuroleptic malignant syndrome.** Cross-refer [[A6_Thermal_and_Environmental_Injury]] 0.3.
> **· "Muscle relaxants" prescribed for acute low back pain have limited evidence, cause sedation and dizziness, and should be avoided in older people.** Cross-refer [[L4_Back_and_Neck_Pain]] 0.4.
> **2. NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKERS (anaesthetic):**
> **· DEPOLARISING — SUXAMETHONIUM. Rapid onset and offset. THE THREE DANGERS: HYPERKALAEMIA (severe in burns beyond about 24–48 hours, denervation injury, spinal cord injury and prolonged immobility, through receptor upregulation) · MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA trigger · and SUXAMETHONIUM APNOEA in pseudocholinesterase deficiency, which is inherited and must be communicated to the family.**
> **· NON-DEPOLARISING — rocuronium, vecuronium — reversed by neostigmine or, for the aminosteroids, sugammadex.**
> **· RESIDUAL NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKADE is a cause of post-operative respiratory compromise and aspiration.** Cross-refer [[AN1_Perioperative_Care]] 0.2.

## 0.4 Mast Cell Stabilisers, Pleuromutilins and Antimycobacterials

> [!tip] Mast cell stabilisers — prophylaxis only, and that is why they "fail"
> **SODIUM CROMOGLICATE, nedocromil and ketotifen — they stabilise the mast cell membrane and prevent DEGRANULATION.**
> **USES: allergic conjunctivitis and allergic rhinitis (topical) · historically asthma prophylaxis, now largely superseded by inhaled corticosteroids · and ORAL cromoglicate for the gastrointestinal symptoms of MASTOCYTOSIS, where it acts locally on gut mast cells rather than systemically.** Cross-refer [[G6_Flushing__Sweating_and_Cyanosis]] 0.2 and [[K4_Allergy_and_Clinical_Immunology]] 0.2.
> **THE POINT THAT DETERMINES WHETHER THEY WORK: THEY ARE PURELY PROPHYLACTIC.**
> **They prevent degranulation; they do NOTHING for symptoms already present, because the mediators have already been released.**
> **THEY MUST BE USED REGULARLY, IN ADVANCE, AND THEY TAKE DAYS TO WEEKS TO REACH FULL EFFECT.**
> **A patient who uses them as required when their eyes are already itching will conclude they are useless — and that is the commonest reason for perceived failure.** **Explaining this at the point of prescribing is the whole intervention.**

> [!info] Pleuromutilins — a niche class worth recognising
> **A distinct antibacterial class binding the 50S ribosomal subunit at a site different from that of macrolides and other protein synthesis inhibitors — which gives it LOW CROSS-RESISTANCE with existing classes.**
> **· RETAPAMULIN — topical, for impetigo.**
> **· LEFAMULIN — systemic, for community-acquired pneumonia.**
> **They are reserved agents with a narrow role, and the reason to know the class exists is that novel antibacterial classes are rare and their stewardship matters.** `UNVERIFIED — current Australian availability and indications.`

> [!danger] Antimycobacterials — four drugs, four characteristic toxicities
> **The standard initial regimen for tuberculosis is a four-drug combination, and each has a signature adverse effect:**
> **· ISONIAZID → PERIPHERAL NEUROPATHY (through pyridoxine antagonism — hence PYRIDOXINE is co-prescribed, particularly in those at risk: diabetes, alcohol use, malnutrition, HIV, pregnancy and renal failure) · and HEPATOTOXICITY.**
> **· RIFAMPICIN → a POTENT CYTOCHROME P450 INDUCER, and HEPATOTOXICITY, and ORANGE-RED DISCOLOURATION of urine, sweat and tears.**
> **THE INDUCTION IS CLINICALLY MAJOR: it causes ORAL CONTRACEPTIVE FAILURE (women must use an alternative or additional method), reduces warfarin, corticosteroid, antiretroviral, azole, methadone and many other drug levels — and stopping rifampicin later causes those levels to rise again.**
> **AND WARN ABOUT THE ORANGE SECRETIONS — it PERMANENTLY STAINS SOFT CONTACT LENSES, and an unwarned patient who finds their tears and urine orange is understandably alarmed.**
> **· PYRAZINAMIDE → HEPATOTOXICITY and HYPERURICAEMIA (which may precipitate gout).**
> **· ETHAMBUTOL → OPTIC NEURITIS, with loss of visual acuity and — characteristically — RED-GREEN COLOUR DISCRIMINATION.**
> **BASELINE AND PERIODIC VISUAL ACUITY AND COLOUR VISION TESTING IS REQUIRED, and the patient must be told to report any visual change immediately and stop the drug.** **The toxicity is dose- and duration-related and is usually reversible IF caught early — and permanent if not.**
> **THE SHARED ISSUES: HEPATOTOXICITY from three of the four (baseline and monitored liver function, and a clear instruction about symptoms) · ADHERENCE, which determines both cure and the emergence of resistance, hence directly observed therapy in some settings · DRUG-RESISTANT tuberculosis requiring specialist regimens · and NOTIFICATION and contact tracing.** Cross-refer [[K3_Exposure__Tuberculosis__HIV_and_Immunodeficiency]] 0.3.
> **LATENT TB TREATMENT uses shorter regimens and is what allows immunosuppression or biologic therapy to proceed safely.** Cross-refer [[K3_Exposure__Tuberculosis__HIV_and_Immunodeficiency]] 0.5.
> **Related: DAPSONE and clofazimine for leprosy — and dapsone causes HAEMOLYSIS (severe in G6PD deficiency) and METHAEMOGLOBINAEMIA.** Cross-refer [[J2_Haemoglobinopathy_and_Haemolysis]] 0.2 and [[G6_Flushing__Sweating_and_Cyanosis]] 0.6.

## 0.5 Antiseptics, Skin Protectants and Vesicants

> [!danger] Chlorhexidine — a hidden and increasingly recognised cause of anaphylaxis
> **A BISBIGUANIDE antiseptic with broad-spectrum activity and — importantly — PERSISTENT RESIDUAL ACTIVITY on the skin, which is why it is preferred for surgical skin preparation, hand hygiene, vascular access site care and oral rinses.**
> **THREE SAFETY POINTS THAT MATTER:**
> **1. CHLORHEXIDINE ANAPHYLAXIS IS REAL, INCREASINGLY RECOGNISED, AND FREQUENTLY MISSED.**
> **It is a documented cause of unexplained PERIOPERATIVE ANAPHYLAXIS — and the exposure is often invisible, because chlorhexidine is impregnated into central venous catheters, urinary catheter lubricant gel, dressings and wound products, as well as being used for skin preparation.**
> **So a patient who collapses after line insertion or catheterisation may be reacting to the chlorhexidine rather than to any drug given.** **It must be on the differential, tested for after unexplained anaphylaxis, and clearly labelled thereafter.** Cross-refer [[K4_Allergy_and_Clinical_Immunology]] 0.1.
> **2. ALCOHOLIC CHLORHEXIDINE MUST BE ALLOWED TO DRY COMPLETELY BEFORE DIATHERMY OR DRAPING.** **Pooled alcoholic solution ignites, and surgical fires have caused severe burns.**
> **3. IT IS OTOTOXIC AND NEUROTOXIC.** **It must NOT enter the middle ear through a perforated tympanic membrane, must not contact the meninges or neural tissue, and higher concentrations damage the eye — so the preparation and concentration must match the site.**

> [!tip] Skin protectants — an unglamorous, high-value intervention
> **Barrier preparations — zinc oxide, dimeticone, petrolatum-based ointments and no-sting barrier films — protect skin from moisture, urine, faeces and exudate.**
> **THE USES ARE COMMON AND THE CONDITION IS COMMON: INCONTINENCE-ASSOCIATED DERMATITIS · peristomal skin protection · periwound maceration · nappy dermatitis · and moisture-associated skin damage generally.**
> **WHY IT MATTERS MEDICALLY: MOISTURE-ASSOCIATED SKIN DAMAGE IS A DIRECT PRECURSOR TO PRESSURE INJURY** — macerated, excoriated skin breaks down far faster under pressure and shear.
> **So barrier care is pressure injury prevention, not cosmetic nursing work — and it is one of the interventions doctors most consistently ignore while writing about the pressure injury it would have prevented.** Cross-refer [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.2 and [[G2_Blisters_and_Skin_Failure]] 0.6.
> **The practical rule: a patient who is incontinent and immobile needs a barrier product prescribed and applied, and it belongs in the plan.**

> [!danger] Vesicants and extravasation — and the ones an intern actually encounters
> **A VESICANT is any agent that causes tissue necrosis if it extravasates from the vein.**
> **EVERYONE THINKS OF CHEMOTHERAPY — anthracyclines and vinca alkaloids are the classic vesicants. BUT THE ONES A GENERAL WARD DOCTOR ENCOUNTERS ARE NON-CYTOTOXIC:**
> **· CONCENTRATED POTASSIUM · CALCIUM salts · HYPERTONIC SALINE and concentrated dextrose · SODIUM BICARBONATE · VASOPRESSORS, particularly NORADRENALINE · radiological CONTRAST · PHENYTOIN · amiodarone · and parenteral nutrition.**
> **PREVENTION IS THE MAIN CONTROL: known vesicants and vasopressors should be given through CENTRAL access wherever possible, the cannula site should be visible and checked, and infusions should not run through a cannula that is painful, swollen or poorly flushing.**
> **MANAGING AN EXTRAVASATION:**
> **1. STOP THE INFUSION IMMEDIATELY.**
> **2. DO NOT FLUSH THE LINE — flushing pushes more drug into the tissue.**
> **3. ASPIRATE what you can back through the cannula, then remove it (or leave it in initially if an antidote is to be instilled — follow local policy).**
> **4. MARK the affected area, PHOTOGRAPH it, and ELEVATE the limb.**
> **5. APPLY warm or cold compress ACCORDING TO THE AGENT — they differ, and the wrong one worsens injury.**
> **6. SPECIFIC ANTIDOTES where they exist: HYALURONIDASE for many agents to disperse the extravasate · and PHENTOLAMINE (an alpha-blocker, infiltrated locally) for VASOPRESSOR extravasation, which reverses the intense local vasoconstriction and can prevent full-thickness necrosis.** **Topical nitrate is an alternative.**
> **7. ESCALATE — pharmacy, the treating team, and PLASTIC SURGERY early for significant extravasation.**
> `UNVERIFIED — agent-specific compress and antidote protocols; use your local extravasation policy, and know where it is before you need it.`

## 0.6 Interactions, Monitoring and Deprescribing

> [!warning] The interaction mechanisms, reduced to what you need
> **PHARMACOKINETIC — one drug alters the concentration of another:**
> **· CYP450 INDUCERS lower the levels of other drugs: RIFAMPICIN · CARBAMAZEPINE · PHENYTOIN · phenobarbitone · ST JOHN'S WORT.**
> **THE CLASSIC CONSEQUENCE IS CONTRACEPTIVE FAILURE**, and it is the one to remember. Cross-refer [[O6_Sexual_and_Reproductive_Health]] 0.3 and [[D6_Seizures_and_Movement_Disorders]] 0.3.
> **· CYP450 INHIBITORS raise the levels of other drugs: MACROLIDES (clarithromycin, erythromycin) · AZOLE antifungals · protease inhibitors · amiodarone · GRAPEFRUIT JUICE.**
> **THE CLASSIC CONSEQUENCES: statin toxicity and RHABDOMYOLYSIS when a macrolide or azole is added, and warfarin over-anticoagulation.**
> **· P-GLYCOPROTEIN effects (relevant to digoxin and the DOACs).**
> **· RENAL AND HEPATIC IMPAIRMENT altering clearance.**
> **PHARMACODYNAMIC — additive effects at the same endpoint, and these are the ones that catch people because no level changes:**
> **· QT PROLONGATION — antipsychotics, methadone, macrolides, quinolones, ondansetron, amiodarone, some antidepressants — compounded by hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia.** Cross-refer [[B3_Arrhythmia__Bradycardia_and_Cardiac_Devices]] 0.3.
> **· SEROTONERGIC — SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol, triptans, linezolid, MAOIs.** Cross-refer [[A6_Thermal_and_Environmental_Injury]] 0.3.
> **· BLEEDING — anticoagulant plus antiplatelet plus NSAID plus SSRI, each individually modest and collectively substantial.**
> **· SEDATION — opioids plus benzodiazepines plus antihistamines plus alcohol.**
> **· NEPHROTOXIC — the "TRIPLE WHAMMY" of ACE inhibitor or ARB plus diuretic plus NSAID.** Cross-refer [[H3_Acute_Kidney_Injury_and_CKD]] 0.2.
> **· ANTICHOLINERGIC — see below.**

> [!danger] The anticholinergic burden is cumulative and invisible
> **No single drug on the chart looks like "an anticholinergic" — but the effect is ADDITIVE across many common medicines: oxybutynin and other bladder antimuscarinics · tricyclic antidepressants · sedating antihistamines · some antipsychotics · benztropine · antispasmodics · and some antiemetics.**
> **THE CONSEQUENCES, particularly in older people: COGNITIVE IMPAIRMENT AND DELIRIUM · FALLS · urinary RETENTION · CONSTIPATION and ileus · dry mouth with its dental and nutritional consequences · blurred vision and precipitation of ANGLE-CLOSURE GLAUCOMA · and IMPAIRED SWEATING with heat intolerance and heatstroke risk.**
> **AND THE ASSOCIATION WITH LONGER-TERM COGNITIVE DECLINE.**
> **THE PRACTICAL ACTION: total the anticholinergic load across the whole chart rather than assessing drugs one at a time, and treat reduction of that load as an intervention in its own right — particularly before summer, and in anyone with delirium, falls, retention or cognitive change.** Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.4, [[G6_Flushing__Sweating_and_Cyanosis]] 0.5 and [[E1_Red_and_Painful_Eye]] 0.3.

> [!tip] The drugs that require monitoring — and what you are actually monitoring for
> | Drug | Monitor | For |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Warfarin** | INR | Efficacy and bleeding |
> | **Digoxin** | Level, **POTASSIUM**, renal function | Toxicity, potentiated by hypokalaemia |
> | **Lithium** | Level, renal, thyroid, calcium | Narrow index; toxicity with dehydration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, thiazides |
> | **Aminoglycosides** | Levels, renal function, **hearing** | Nephrotoxicity and irreversible ototoxicity |
> | **Vancomycin** | Levels, renal function | Nephrotoxicity |
> | **Clozapine** | **Neutrophils**, plus troponin/CRP on initiation | Agranulocytosis; myocarditis |
> | **Methotrexate** | FBC, LFT, renal | Marrow suppression, hepatotoxicity — **and it is WEEKLY** |
> | **Amiodarone** | TFT, LFT, chest imaging, eyes | Thyroid, hepatic, pulmonary toxicity |
> | **Antiepileptics** | Levels (selected), FBC, LFT, sodium | Toxicity, hyponatraemia (carbamazepine) |
> | **Antipsychotics** | Weight, glucose, lipids, BP, ECG | Metabolic syndrome and QT |
> | **Ethambutol** | **Visual acuity and colour vision** | Optic neuritis |
> | **Thyroxine** | TSH | Over- and under-replacement |
> `UNVERIFIED — all target ranges and monitoring intervals.`

> [!warning] The deprescribing view
> **Every prescription should have a review date, and the question at every medication review is not only "what should I add?" but "WHAT CAN COME OFF?"**
> **The highest-yield deprescribing targets: benzodiazepines and Z-drugs · anticholinergics · proton pump inhibitors continued without indication · antipsychotics in dementia · long-term opioids without benefit · and preventive medications whose time-to-benefit exceeds the patient's life expectancy.**
> **The principles: one drug at a time · taper where withdrawal effects are expected (benzodiazepines, opioids, beta-blockers, corticosteroids, antidepressants, PPIs) · agree the plan with the patient · and monitor.** Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.4 and [[GER3_Preventive_and_Occupational_Health]] 0.1.
> **AND REPORT SUSPECTED ADVERSE REACTIONS TO THE TGA.** **Post-marketing surveillance depends on clinicians reporting, reporting rates are low, and several important drug harms have been identified this way.** `UNVERIFIED — the current reporting mechanism.`
