## 0.1 Preoperative Assessment

> [!tip] The purpose is four things, and knowing them shapes the assessment
> **1. Identify and OPTIMISE modifiable risk** before the operation.
> **2. Plan the anaesthetic** — airway, technique, monitoring.
> **3. Inform CONSENT** with a realistic discussion of individual risk.
> **4. Plan the POSTOPERATIVE pathway** — level of care, analgesia, disposition.
> **The assessment that changes nothing was not worth doing** — which is the principle governing investigation below.

> [!info] Risk stratification tools
> **· ASA physical status classification (I–V, with E for emergency)** — a global descriptor of systemic disease, widely used and simple. `UNVERIFIED — the class definitions.`
> **· FUNCTIONAL CAPACITY in metabolic equivalents (METs).** **The practical question: "can you climb two flights of stairs without stopping?"** A patient who can generally has adequate reserve; one who cannot, or whose activity is limited by symptoms rather than by joints, warrants closer assessment. `UNVERIFIED — the MET threshold.`
> **· Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI)** — combines ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes on insulin, renal impairment and high-risk surgery. `UNVERIFIED — components and interpretation.`
> **· FRAILTY — a better predictor of postoperative outcome than chronological age**, and increasingly used to inform whether to operate at all. Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.2.

> [!danger] Do not stop dual antiplatelet therapy after a recent coronary stent
> **Premature cessation of dual antiplatelet therapy after coronary stenting causes STENT THROMBOSIS, which carries very high mortality.**
> **There is a mandatory minimum duration of DAPT after stenting, which differs by stent type and indication, and elective surgery should be DEFERRED until it is complete.**
> **If surgery cannot wait, the decision requires explicit discussion with cardiology and the surgical and anaesthetic teams** — it is never a decision for a junior doctor or a pre-admission clinic to make unilaterally.
> **Similarly, elective surgery is deferred for a defined period after myocardial infarction.** `UNVERIFIED — the DAPT durations by stent type, and the interval after infarction.`

> [!warning] Preoperative anaemia should be found and treated weeks in advance
> **Anaemia before elective surgery is common, is an INDEPENDENT risk factor for morbidity, mortality and transfusion, and is frequently ignored until the day.**
> **Patient Blood Management principles: identify anaemia EARLY (weeks before), determine the cause, and treat it — most often with INTRAVENOUS IRON for iron deficiency, which works within weeks.**
> **Australia has a National Blood Authority Patient Blood Management framework**, and this is one of the clearest examples of a preoperative intervention that measurably improves outcomes.
> **The practical failure: the haemoglobin is checked on admission the day before surgery, when it is too late to do anything but transfuse.** Cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] and [[J1_Cytopenias_and_Marrow_Failure]]. `UNVERIFIED — the PBM thresholds and iron regimens.`

> [!tip] The other optimisable factors
> **· SMOKING — cessation reduces respiratory and wound complications, and the benefit increases with the duration of abstinence. Even short-term cessation before surgery helps, and the preoperative period is a highly effective teachable moment** — patients are more likely to succeed at quitting around surgery than at almost any other time. `UNVERIFIED — the recommended cessation interval.`
> **· ALCOHOL — reduction reduces complications, and withdrawal must be anticipated in dependent patients.**
> **· DIABETES — glycaemic optimisation, and identification of unrecognised diabetes.**
> **· OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNOEA — screen with STOP-BANG; it predicts postoperative respiratory complications and changes monitoring.** Cross-refer [[RESP-X_Occupational_and_Chronic_Lung_Disease]] 0.5.
> **· Nutrition, exercise ("prehabilitation"), and management of heart failure, COPD and asthma.**

> [!danger] Do not order routine preoperative investigations in well patients
> **Routine blood tests, ECGs and chest radiographs in asymptomatic patients undergoing low-risk surgery do not improve outcomes.** They generate incidental abnormalities that cause delay, further testing, and anxiety without benefit.
> **Investigations should be targeted by the PATIENT (comorbidity, medications, age) and the PROCEDURE (magnitude, expected blood loss).**
> **What is usually justified: FBC where anaemia or bleeding is plausible or blood loss expected · UEC in renal disease, diuretics, ACE inhibitors or major surgery · glucose or HbA1c in diabetes · ECG in cardiac disease or above an age threshold for major surgery · group and hold or crossmatch where transfusion is possible · pregnancy test where relevant.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the specific indications and age thresholds.`

**Ix:** As above — **targeted rather than routine.** **Group and hold or crossmatch** (*why:* preparation for transfusion, and antibody screening takes time; *what:* group and antibodies). **Echocardiography only where it will change management** — new or worsening heart failure, an undiagnosed murmur with symptoms, or suspected severe valvular disease (*why:* **severe aortic stenosis substantially changes anaesthetic conduct and risk**, whereas an echo that confirms known stable disease changes nothing; *what:* valve disease, ventricular function). Respiratory function tests in significant lung disease undergoing thoracic or major surgery (*why:* predicts postoperative respiratory failure; *what:* FEV1, DLCO). **Cervical spine imaging in rheumatoid arthritis** (*why:* atlantoaxial subluxation risks cord injury on intubation; *what:* instability — cross-refer [[L2_Polyarthralgia_and_Inflammatory_Arthritis]] 0.2).

## 0.2 Airway Assessment and Anaesthetic Technique

> [!warning] Predictors of a difficult airway
> **· Previous difficult intubation — the single most useful predictor, and the reason to ASK and to CHECK old anaesthetic records.**
> **· Mallampati class, limited MOUTH OPENING, short THYROMENTAL distance, reduced NECK EXTENSION, prominent incisors, receding mandible.**
> **· Obesity, particularly with a large neck circumference · obstructive sleep apnoea · pregnancy · beard.**
> **· Pathology: head and neck cancer, previous neck RADIOTHERAPY or surgery, burns contractures, airway tumours, and infection.**
> **· RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS — atlantoaxial instability.** Cross-refer [[L2_Polyarthralgia_and_Inflammatory_Arthritis]] 0.2.
> **· ANKYLOSING SPONDYLITIS — a fixed flexed cervical spine, which makes both intubation and neuraxial access difficult.** Cross-refer [[L2_Polyarthralgia_and_Inflammatory_Arthritis]] 0.3.
> **DOCUMENT a difficult airway prominently in the record AND give the patient written documentation to carry** — a difficult airway discovered a second time, unwarned, is a preventable catastrophe.

> [!tip] Regional anaesthesia and analgesia — the advantages worth knowing
> **Reduced opioid requirement, superior analgesia, less postoperative nausea and vomiting, earlier mobilisation, and — importantly in older patients — LESS DELIRIUM.**
> **The fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block in hip fracture is a specific, high-value example** that reduces opioid use and delirium and is under-used. Cross-refer [[L7_Fractures_and_Eponyms]] 0.4.

> [!danger] New back pain with neurological deficit after neuraxial anaesthesia is an emergency
> **EPIDURAL HAEMATOMA or ABSCESS causes permanent paraplegia if not decompressed quickly.**
> **The warning features: new or worsening back pain · a motor or sensory block that is DENSER, HIGHER or LASTING LONGER than expected · a block that recurs after wearing off · new bladder dysfunction · and, with abscess, fever and raised inflammatory markers.**
> **A patient whose epidural block should have worn off and has not, or who develops new leg weakness, needs URGENT MRI and urgent neurosurgical discussion — not observation overnight.**
> **This is why anticoagulant timing around neuraxial procedures is strictly protocolised**, and why those intervals must be checked rather than estimated. Cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.4 and [[L4_Back_and_Neck_Pain]] 0.2. `UNVERIFIED — all anticoagulant timing intervals for neuraxial procedures.`

> [!warning] Post-dural puncture headache
> **A POSTURAL headache — markedly worse on sitting or standing, relieved by lying flat** — following dural puncture, typically appearing within a day or two. Often frontal or occipital, sometimes with neck stiffness, photophobia, tinnitus or diplopia.
> **The postural nature is the diagnostic feature**, and it distinguishes it from other postoperative headaches.
> **Managed conservatively at first, with an EPIDURAL BLOOD PATCH for persistent or severe symptoms, which is often dramatically effective.** Cross-refer [[D1_Headache_and_Meningism]] 0.4.

> [!danger] Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity — know that lipid emulsion exists and where it is
> **Occurs after inadvertent intravascular injection or excessive dose, particularly with large-volume blocks.**
> **The progression: perioral tingling, TINNITUS, a METALLIC TASTE, visual disturbance, agitation and confusion → SEIZURES → cardiovascular collapse and arrhythmia refractory to standard resuscitation.**
> **Management: STOP the injection · call for help · A–B–C with 100% oxygen · control seizures · and give INTRAVENOUS LIPID EMULSION ("Intralipid"), which is the specific antidote.**
> **Prolonged resuscitation may be required, and recovery is possible after extended arrest — do not stop early.**
> **Every area where regional blocks are performed should have a LAST kit and a copy of the protocol. Know where yours is.** `UNVERIFIED — the lipid emulsion regimen; follow the ANZCA/ASRA protocol on the wall.`

> [!tip] The anaesthetic drug problems worth recognising
> **· MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA — triggered by volatile agents and suxamethonium. A rising END-TIDAL CO₂ is the earliest sign, before temperature rises. Dantrolene is the antidote, and the family requires screening.** Cross-refer [[A6_Thermal_and_Environmental_Injury]] 0.3.
> **· SUXAMETHONIUM — causes a transient rise in potassium, which becomes DANGEROUS HYPERKALAEMIA in burns beyond about 24–48 hours, denervation injury, spinal cord injury, and prolonged immobility, through upregulation of acetylcholine receptors.** **Also: suxamethonium apnoea in pseudocholinesterase deficiency — prolonged paralysis requiring continued ventilation, which is inherited and should be documented and communicated to the family.**
> **· Residual neuromuscular blockade — a cause of postoperative respiratory compromise and aspiration**, and a reason for careful monitoring in recovery.
> **· AWARENESS under anaesthesia — rare, deeply distressing, and requires acknowledgement, explanation and psychological follow-up rather than dismissal.**

## 0.3 Perioperative Medication Management

> [!tip] Drugs to CONTINUE — including on the morning of surgery
> **· BETA-BLOCKERS — continue. Abrupt withdrawal causes rebound tachycardia, hypertension and ischaemia.**
> **· Statins — continue.**
> **· Inhalers — continue, and bring them.**
> **· Antiepileptics — continue; breakthrough seizures are a real risk with omitted doses.**
> **· PARKINSON DISEASE MEDICATIONS — continue, ON TIME.** **These are time-critical: a missed or delayed dose leaves the patient rigid, unable to swallow or communicate, and abrupt withdrawal can precipitate an akinetic crisis or a neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome.** **Prescribe them at the patient's own home times, and plan an alternative route if they will be nil by mouth.** Cross-refer [[D6_Seizures_and_Movement_Disorders]] 0.5.
> **· Thyroid replacement, most psychotropics, and antiretrovirals.**
> **· CORTICOSTEROIDS — continue, WITH stress dosing for surgery.** A patient on long-term steroids cannot mount a cortisol response and requires perioperative supplementation. Cross-refer [[I4_Pituitary__Adrenal_and_Sex_Hormone]] 0.3.

> [!danger] Drugs to WITHHOLD — and the two newest ones matter most
> **· SGLT2 INHIBITORS — WITHHOLD before surgery because of EUGLYCAEMIC DIABETIC KETOACIDOSIS.** **The glucose looks acceptable while the patient is profoundly ketotic and acidotic, which is exactly why it is missed perioperatively.** **Check ketones, not just glucose, in any unwell patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor.** Cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.3 and [[I2_Diabetes_and_Glucose_Disorders]] 0.7. `UNVERIFIED — the withholding interval.`
> **· GLP-1 RECEPTOR AGONISTS — delay gastric emptying, and there is emerging concern about residual gastric contents and ASPIRATION at induction despite adequate fasting.** Guidance on withholding has been issued and is evolving. `UNVERIFIED — this is recent and changing; check current ANZCA guidance.`
> **· ACE INHIBITORS and ARBs — commonly withheld on the morning of surgery** because of refractory intraoperative hypotension. `UNVERIFIED — local policy varies.`
> **· ANTICOAGULANTS AND ANTIPLATELETS — timing depends on the AGENT, the RENAL FUNCTION (for DOACs), the BLEEDING RISK of the procedure, and the THROMBOTIC RISK of stopping.** **This is a protocol decision, not a memory exercise.** Cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.4.
> **· Metformin, per local policy.**
> **· Combined hormonal contraception and HRT** — considered for major surgery because of VTE risk, balanced against contraceptive need.
> **· Herbal and complementary preparations** — several affect bleeding and anaesthetic drug metabolism, and patients do not consider them medications. **Ask specifically.**

> [!warning] Diabetes management around surgery
> **· NEVER STOP BASAL INSULIN IN TYPE 1 DIABETES**, including while fasting. **A person with type 1 diabetes makes no insulin and will develop DKA without basal cover, regardless of oral intake.** Cross-refer [[I2_Diabetes_and_Glucose_Disorders]] 0.3.
> **· Put diabetic patients FIRST ON THE LIST** where possible, to minimise fasting.
> **· A variable rate intravenous insulin infusion is used for prolonged fasting, major surgery, poor control, or type 1 diabetes where the fast is more than brief.**
> **· Monitor glucose regularly**, and have a plan for hypoglycaemia.

## 0.4 Fasting, Fluids and Enhanced Recovery

> [!danger] Patients are routinely fasted far longer than necessary, and it causes harm
> **The standard fasting rules are approximately "2–4–6": clear fluids up to 2 hours, breast milk up to 4 hours, and solids and formula up to 6 hours before anaesthesia.** `UNVERIFIED — the exact intervals and the categories, from ANZCA guidance.`
> **In practice, patients are made nil by mouth from midnight for a list that may not reach them until the afternoon, then fasted again when the list runs over — producing fasts of many hours.**
> **The consequences: thirst, hunger, headache, irritability, hypoglycaemia, dehydration, hypotension on induction, and — in older patients — delirium.**
> **ACTIVELY ENCOURAGE CLEAR FLUIDS UNTIL TWO HOURS BEFORE**, and re-clarify fasting times when a list is delayed. **This is a concrete thing a junior doctor can do that improves the patient's experience and their physiology.**

> [!tip] Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS)
> A bundle of evidence-based measures that collectively **reduce length of stay and complications**:
> **· Preoperative education and expectation setting · CARBOHYDRATE LOADING drinks rather than prolonged fasting · avoidance of routine bowel preparation where evidence does not support it · minimally invasive surgery where appropriate · REGIONAL and MULTIMODAL OPIOID-SPARING ANALGESIA · avoidance of routine drains and nasogastric tubes · goal-directed fluid therapy · EARLY ORAL INTAKE — feeding on the day of surgery rather than waiting for flatus · EARLY MOBILISATION · and early removal of urinary catheters.**
> **The recurring theme is that most traditional postoperative restrictions — prolonged fasting, bed rest, drains, delayed feeding — were never evidence-based and are actively harmful.**

> [!warning] Perioperative fluids — the two errors
> **· OVER-resuscitation causes tissue oedema, impaired anastomotic healing, ileus, pulmonary oedema and delayed recovery.**
> **· UNDER-resuscitation causes hypoperfusion, acute kidney injury and delayed recovery.**
> **Large volumes of 0.9% sodium chloride cause HYPERCHLORAEMIC METABOLIC ACIDOSIS and are associated with worse renal outcomes — BALANCED CRYSTALLOIDS are preferred for most resuscitation and maintenance.** Cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.6.
> **Postoperative fluid prescribing is a daily junior doctor task and it is done badly:**
> **· CHECK THE ELECTROLYTES AND THE WEIGHT before writing the next bag, rather than continuing the same prescription by default.**
> **· Account for maintenance, existing deficits and ONGOING LOSSES (drains, stomas, nasogastric aspirate, insensible losses with fever).**
> **· HYPOTONIC FLUIDS in the postoperative period cause HYPONATRAEMIA**, because surgery, pain, nausea and opioids all stimulate ADH release — **postoperative SIADH is common, and giving hypotonic fluid into it has killed people, particularly young women.** Cross-refer [[I5_Weight__Lipids_and_Fluid_Balance]] 0.5.
> **· Stop intravenous fluids as soon as the patient is drinking.**

## 0.5 Postoperative Complications

> [!tip] By timing — which narrows the differential immediately
> **IMMEDIATE (recovery/PACU): airway obstruction, LARYNGOSPASM, hypoxia, residual neuromuscular blockade, hypotension, primary haemorrhage, pain, nausea and vomiting, shivering, emergence delirium.**
> **EARLY (first 48 hours): atelectasis, hypovolaemia, reactionary haemorrhage, MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION, urinary retention, ileus, delirium.**
> **DAYS 3–7: infection (wound, chest, urinary, line), ANASTOMOTIC LEAK, collection, deep vein thrombosis.**
> **LATER: pulmonary embolism, abscess, *C. difficile*, pressure injury, deconditioning, incisional hernia.**
> Cross-refer [[K1_Fever_Workup]] 0.6 for the fever timeline.

> [!danger] The deteriorating post-operative patient
> **TACHYCARDIA is the earliest and most consistent sign, and it is repeatedly attributed to pain, anxiety or dehydration.**
> **A patient who is not progressing as expected — persistent tachycardia, unresolving ileus, unexplained oxygen requirement, rising inflammatory markers, or simply "not right" from around day 3–5 — has an ANASTOMOTIC LEAK or a COLLECTION until proven otherwise.**
> **NEW POSTOPERATIVE ATRIAL FIBRILLATION is a recognised early flag for an anastomotic leak** and should trigger a search for a surgical cause rather than just rate control.
> Cross-refer [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.5 and [[C7_Pancreatobiliary_Disease]] 0.5.

> [!danger] Perioperative myocardial infarction is frequently SILENT
> **It often occurs without chest pain** — masked by analgesia, sedation and the postoperative state — and presents as hypotension, arrhythmia, breathlessness, confusion, or nothing at all beyond a troponin rise.
> **It carries high mortality**, and it is concentrated in the first days after surgery.
> **Have a low threshold for an ECG and troponin in a high-risk patient who deteriorates**, and interpret the troponin in context — cross-refer [[B1_Chest_Pain_Framework_and_Cardiac_Biomarkers]] 0.2.

> [!warning] Postoperative hypoxia — work through the causes
> **· Atelectasis — the commonest, and the reason for early mobilisation, deep breathing and incentive spirometry.**
> **· OPIOID-INDUCED HYPOVENTILATION — with a SEDATION SCORE that rises BEFORE the respiratory rate falls.** See 0.6.
> **· Residual neuromuscular blockade.**
> **· Aspiration and pneumonia.**
> **· Pulmonary oedema — from fluid overload or cardiac dysfunction.**
> **· PULMONARY EMBOLISM.**
> **· Pneumothorax — after central line insertion, thoracic or upper abdominal surgery, or positive pressure ventilation.**

> [!tip] The common, treatable postoperative problems
> **· POSTOPERATIVE NAUSEA AND VOMITING — risk predicted by the Apfel factors: FEMALE sex, NON-SMOKER, previous PONV or motion sickness, and postoperative OPIOIDS.** **Prophylaxis is given based on risk, and multimodal antiemetics targeting different receptors work better than repeated doses of one.** Cross-refer [[C2_Nausea_and_Vomiting]] 0.5.
> **· POSTOPERATIVE URINARY RETENTION — BLADDER SCAN rather than guess.** Risk with opioids, anticholinergics, spinal or epidural anaesthesia, male sex, pelvic and anorectal surgery. **An agitated, distressed postoperative patient may be in retention** — cross-refer [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.5.
> **· POSTOPERATIVE DELIRIUM — anticipate and prevent** rather than treat: orientation, sensory aids, sleep, early mobilisation, adequate analgesia, removal of tethers, avoidance of benzodiazepines and anticholinergics. Cross-refer [[D2_Altered_Consciousness_and_Cognition]] 0.2.
> **· ILEUS — early feeding and mobilisation help; distinguish from mechanical obstruction.** Cross-refer [[C5_Bowel_Habit__Obstruction_and_Distension]] 0.5.
> **· VTE — risk-assess every surgical patient and prescribe mechanical and pharmacological prophylaxis; EXTENDED duration prophylaxis is indicated after major cancer and major orthopaedic surgery.** `UNVERIFIED — agents, doses and durations.`

## 0.6 Postoperative Analgesia

> [!tip] Multimodal is the principle
> **Combine agents acting at different sites so that each can be used at a lower dose: REGULAR PARACETAMOL + an NSAID where safe + REGIONAL or local anaesthetic technique + opioid as required.**
> **Regular background analgesia with as-required top-up beats as-required alone** — waiting for pain to become severe before treating it produces worse control at higher total doses.
> **Adjuncts: ketamine at low dose, clonidine, and — with caution — gabapentinoids, whose benefit in acute postoperative pain is more limited than was assumed and which cause sedation, particularly in the elderly and in combination with opioids.**

> [!danger] Patient-controlled analgesia — the sedation score is the early warning, and only the patient presses the button
> **The safety of PCA depends on the fact that a sedated patient cannot press the button.**
> **"PCA BY PROXY" — a family member or a well-meaning nurse pressing it for a sleeping patient — REMOVES that safety mechanism and has caused deaths.** **Explain this explicitly to the patient AND to the family.**
> **SEDATION SCORE IS THE EARLIEST SIGN OF OPIOID TOXICITY — it rises BEFORE the respiratory rate falls and before the saturation drops.**
> **So a rising sedation score is the trigger to act, and waiting for a low respiratory rate or desaturation means waiting too long — particularly in a patient on supplemental oxygen, where the saturation stays normal while the CO₂ climbs.** Cross-refer [[A3_Respiratory_Failure__Bronchospasm_and_Hypoxia]].

> [!warning] Patients on long-term opioids or opioid agonist therapy need MORE, not less
> **A patient with opioid tolerance, or on methadone or buprenorphine, requires their BASELINE opioid CONTINUED, with additional analgesia on top for the acute surgical pain.**
> **Withholding or reducing their usual dose produces withdrawal AND uncontrolled pain simultaneously**, and it is a common and damaging error made from misplaced caution or moral judgement.
> **Involve the acute pain service early**, use regional techniques and non-opioid adjuncts aggressively, and document the plan clearly. Cross-refer [[F0-4_Resuscitation_Algorithms_and_Emergency_Procedures]] 0.9.

> [!danger] Discharge opioid prescribing drives persistent opioid use
> **A substantial proportion of long-term opioid use begins with a postoperative prescription**, and the risk relates to the quantity supplied and the presence of repeats.
> **Practical rules:**
> **· Prescribe a SMALL quantity, matched to the expected duration of significant pain — typically days, not weeks.**
> **· Do NOT provide repeats by default.**
> **· Give a written plan for stepping DOWN and stopping, and say explicitly that the opioid is expected to be stopped.**
> **· Advise on safe storage and disposal of unused tablets** — leftover opioids in the home are a source of diversion and of paediatric poisoning.
> **· Ensure the GP knows what was prescribed and what the plan is.**
> **In South Australia, real-time prescription monitoring (ScriptCheckSA) applies**, and checking it is part of safe prescribing. `UNVERIFIED — current obligations.`

> [!tip] Analgesia in specific groups
> **· RENAL IMPAIRMENT — morphine metabolites accumulate and cause prolonged sedation and respiratory depression. Avoid NSAIDs.**
> **· HEPATIC IMPAIRMENT — reduced metabolism; paracetamol is usually still used but at reduced dose.**
> **· ELDERLY — start lower, titrate carefully, avoid anticholinergics and benzodiazepines, and prioritise regional techniques.**
> **· OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNOEA — heightened sensitivity to opioid-induced respiratory depression; use regional and non-opioid techniques, monitor closely, and continue CPAP postoperatively.**
> **· PREGNANCY AND BREASTFEEDING — most analgesics are compatible; avoid codeine in breastfeeding because of variable metabolism, and consult a specialist service rather than withholding analgesia.**

## 0.7 Specific Perioperative Situations

> [!warning] The steroid-dependent patient
> **Long-term corticosteroid therapy suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, so the patient cannot mount the cortisol response that surgery demands.**
> **Perioperative stress dosing is required, scaled to the magnitude of surgery, and their usual dose must be continued.**
> **Omitting it causes intraoperative or postoperative hypotension that does not respond to fluids and vasopressors — and adrenal crisis.** Cross-refer [[I4_Pituitary__Adrenal_and_Sex_Hormone]] 0.3 and [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.6. `UNVERIFIED — the stress dosing regimens.`

> [!danger] The frail older patient — "can we" is a different question from "should we"
> **Frailty predicts postoperative mortality, complications, delirium, functional decline and institutionalisation better than age or the individual comorbidities.**
> **The conversation that matters is not only whether the patient can survive the operation, but what their function and quality of life will be afterwards, and whether that aligns with what they want.**
> **This requires: a frailty assessment · a clear discussion of realistic outcomes including the possibility of not returning home · involvement of the patient and family · and consideration of non-operative management as a legitimate option rather than a failure.**
> **Document goals of care and the ceiling of treatment BEFORE the operation, not after the deterioration.** Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.2, [[GER2_Geriatric_Syndromes_and_End_of_Life_Care]] 0.6 and [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.3.

> [!tip] Other situations worth anticipating
> **· NON-OBSTETRIC SURGERY IN PREGNANCY — defer elective surgery; use LEFT LATERAL TILT or manual uterine displacement after about 20 weeks to avoid aortocaval compression; involve obstetrics; arrange fetal monitoring appropriate to gestation; and do not withhold necessary imaging or surgery.** Cross-refer [[L8_Facial__Head_and_Torso_Trauma]] 0.6 and [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]].
> **· JEHOVAH'S WITNESS PATIENTS — plan in ADVANCE.** Establish precisely which products and techniques are acceptable (individuals differ), document it, and use **preoperative iron and erythropoietin, tranexamic acid, cell salvage where acceptable, meticulous haemostasis and a lower transfusion threshold.** **A competent adult's refusal of blood is binding even if death results.** Cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.2 and [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]].
> **· EMERGENCY SURGERY IN SEPSIS — resuscitate, but do NOT delay SOURCE CONTROL for the sake of optimisation.** In a septic patient with an intra-abdominal source, the operation is part of the resuscitation. Cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.7.
> **· PAEDIATRIC — weight-based everything, parental presence at induction, attention to fasting (children tolerate prolonged fasting poorly), and emergence delirium.**
> **· OBESITY — airway, positioning, vascular access, drug dosing (some by lean body weight, some by total), higher VTE risk, and OSA.**

**Ix:** **Postoperative: targeted rather than routine.** **FBC and UEC where there has been significant blood loss, fluid shifts or ongoing losses** (*why:* anaemia and electrolyte disturbance, particularly sodium; *what:* haemoglobin, sodium, potassium, renal function). **ECG and troponin in a deteriorating high-risk patient** (*why:* silent perioperative infarction; *what:* ischaemic change, troponin rise). **CT of the operative field in the patient who is not progressing** (*why:* anastomotic leak and collection are the diagnoses to exclude, and clinical signs are unreliable postoperatively; *what:* collection, free gas, leak). **Bladder scan for the agitated or oliguric patient** (*why:* retention is common, easily missed and easily fixed; *what:* residual volume). **Ketones in any unwell patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor, regardless of glucose** (*why:* euglycaemic DKA; *what:* beta-hydroxybutyrate). Blood cultures and septic screen for fever (*why:* per the postoperative fever timeline; *what:* source — cross-refer [[K1_Fever_Workup]] 0.6).
