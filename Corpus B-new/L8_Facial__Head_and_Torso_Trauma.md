## 0.1 The Primary Survey and Trauma Principles

> [!tip] <C>ABCDE — catastrophic haemorrhage comes first where it applies
> **In penetrating trauma, blast injury and traumatic amputation, exsanguinating external haemorrhage is addressed BEFORE the airway** — direct pressure, a tourniquet, or a haemostatic dressing. A perfect airway in an exsanguinated patient achieves nothing.
> **In blunt trauma without catastrophic external bleeding, the conventional ABCDE sequence applies.**

> [!danger] B — the six immediately life-threatening chest injuries
> Found and treated **in the primary survey**, clinically, before imaging:
> **· Airway obstruction · TENSION PNEUMOTHORAX · OPEN pneumothorax · MASSIVE HAEMOTHORAX · FLAIL CHEST with pulmonary contusion · CARDIAC TAMPONADE.**
> **Tension pneumothorax is a CLINICAL diagnosis — decompress it; do not wait for a chest radiograph.** Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.2.

> [!danger] C — "blood on the floor and four more"
> **The five places a trauma patient bleeds enough to die:**
> **· EXTERNAL (on the floor) · CHEST · ABDOMEN · PELVIS and RETROPERITONEUM · LONG BONES (particularly femoral shaft).**
> **Each is addressed by a specific adjunct: chest radiograph, eFAST, pelvic radiograph and binder, and examination of the limbs.**
> **A patient who remains shocked with all five excluded should prompt consideration of a non-haemorrhagic cause — tamponade, tension pneumothorax, neurogenic shock, or a cardiac event that caused the crash.**
> **Tranexamic acid is given early in significant trauma** — the benefit is time-dependent and is lost, or reversed, if given late. Cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.1. `UNVERIFIED — dose and the time window.`

> [!warning] The lethal triad
> **HYPOTHERMIA → ACIDOSIS → COAGULOPATHY**, each worsening the others in a self-reinforcing spiral that is the proximate cause of death in many trauma patients.
> **Practically: keep the patient warm from the outset — remove wet clothing, use warmed fluids and blood, forced-air warming, and a warm resuscitation room.** **Exposure for examination must be brief and followed by re-covering.**
> **This is why "damage control" surgery exists — arrest the bleeding and contamination, then correct physiology in ICU, and return for definitive repair later.**

> [!tip] The secondary and tertiary surveys
> **Secondary survey — a head-to-toe examination once the primary survey is complete and the patient is not deteriorating**, including a log roll with inspection and palpation of the back, and a **rectal and perineal examination where indicated.** **AMPLE history: Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last meal, Events.**
> **TERTIARY SURVEY — a repeat full examination at around 24 hours, when the patient is awake and communicative.**
> **Missed injuries are common in major trauma** — small bone fractures, tendon injuries, ligamentous injuries and peripheral nerve deficits are routinely overlooked during the initial resuscitation because the patient is intubated, distracted by a major injury, or intoxicated.
> **The tertiary survey is what finds them, and it is frequently omitted.**

**Ix:** **Adjuncts to the primary survey: chest radiograph, pelvic radiograph, eFAST, VBG or ABG with lactate, bedside glucose, ECG** (*why:* each addresses a specific life threat in real time; *what:* pneumothorax, haemothorax, pelvic disruption, free fluid, pericardial fluid, hypoperfusion). **FBC, group and hold or crossmatch, coagulation profile, UEC, LFT, lipase, troponin as indicated, and a pregnancy test in any woman of reproductive age** (*why:* baseline, transfusion preparation, and pregnancy changes the whole management; *what:* baseline and pregnancy status). **Whole-body CT ("pan-scan") in the stable major trauma patient** (*why:* identifies injuries that clinical examination misses, particularly in the intubated or distracted patient; *what:* the injury burden — **but the unstable patient goes to theatre, not the scanner**). **Repeat lactate and base deficit** (*why:* trends reflect the adequacy of resuscitation better than blood pressure does; *what:* clearance or persistence).

## 0.2 Facial Trauma

> [!danger] The airway is the first concern in facial trauma
> **Facial fractures threaten the airway through: bleeding into the pharynx · progressive soft tissue swelling · displaced fracture segments · and — with BILATERAL MANDIBULAR fractures — loss of the anterior tongue support, allowing the tongue to fall back.**
> **The threat is progressive**, so a patient who is currently maintaining their airway may not be in an hour. **Early senior airway involvement, and a low threshold for definitive airway control.** Cross-refer [[A2_Airway_Compromise__Stridor_and_Tracheostomy_Emergencies]] 0.1.

> [!tip] Le Fort fractures
> **· Le Fort I — a transverse fracture separating the maxillary alveolus from the rest of the maxilla.** The hard palate moves independently.
> **· Le Fort II — pyramidal, extending through the nasal bridge and infraorbital rims.** The maxilla and nose move together.
> **· Le Fort III — craniofacial DISJUNCTION**, separating the entire facial skeleton from the skull base. The whole face moves.
> **Tested by holding the forehead and gently rocking the upper alveolus** — the level at which movement occurs indicates the pattern. **All are high-energy injuries; look for associated head, cervical spine and ocular injury, and for CSF leak.**

> [!danger] Orbital blowout — and the paediatric "white-eyed" variant
> **A blow to the globe raises intraorbital pressure and fractures the thin ORBITAL FLOOR**, herniating orbital contents into the maxillary sinus.
> **Features: DIPLOPIA, particularly on UPGAZE · restricted upward eye movement · ENOPHTHALMOS · INFRAORBITAL NERVE NUMBNESS over the cheek, lateral nose and upper lip and gum · surgical emphysema, and epistaxis.**
> **Advise against nose blowing** — it forces air into the orbit and can cause dramatic swelling and, rarely, orbital compartment syndrome.
> **THE PAEDIATRIC "WHITE-EYED BLOWOUT" IS A SURGICAL EMERGENCY.** Children's bones are more elastic, so the floor springs back as a **trapdoor, entrapping muscle**. **The eye looks white and quiet with minimal bruising or swelling — so the injury looks trivial — while the inferior rectus is strangulated.**
> **The clue is the OCULOCARDIAC REFLEX: bradycardia, nausea and vomiting, sometimes syncope, on attempted upgaze.**
> **A child with a periorbital injury who is vomiting and bradycardic with restricted upgaze needs urgent CT and urgent ophthalmic or maxillofacial surgery — muscle ischaemia and permanent diplopia follow delay.** **The vomiting is easily attributed to head injury, which is how this is missed.**

> [!danger] Examine inside the nose — septal haematoma
> **A boggy, bluish swelling of the nasal septum after nasal trauma.**
> **It is missed because nobody looks inside the nose with a light and a speculum.**
> **Untreated, pressure causes septal cartilage necrosis within days, producing septal perforation and a permanent SADDLE-NOSE deformity.**
> **It requires urgent incision and drainage, not observation.** **Examine the septum in every nasal injury**, including in children.

> [!tip] The other facial injuries
> **· Zygomatic (tripod) fracture** — flattening of the cheek prominence, a palpable step at the infraorbital rim and zygomatic arch, **infraorbital numbness**, subconjunctival haemorrhage without a posterior limit, and **TRISMUS** if the arch impinges on the coronoid process.
> **· Mandibular fracture — the mandible is a ring, so it usually breaks in TWO places; find the second one.** Features: **malocclusion ("does your bite feel normal?" is the single best question), trismus, a step in the dental arch, and a SUBLINGUAL HAEMATOMA, which is near-pathognomonic.** Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.10.
> **· Base of skull fracture** — periorbital and mastoid bruising, haemotympanum, CSF rhinorrhoea or otorrhoea. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.7.
> **· Facial lacerations** — consider **facial nerve** injury (test all branches before infiltrating local anaesthetic) and **parotid duct** injury in cheek lacerations along a line from tragus to upper lip.

> [!danger] Ocular and dental emergencies within facial trauma
> **· GLOBE RUPTURE — do NOT press on the eye, do NOT instil drops or ointment, do NOT attempt to measure pressure. Apply a rigid SHIELD (not a pad), keep the patient upright and nil by mouth, give antiemetics, and refer urgently.** Cross-refer [[E1_Red_and_Painful_Eye]].
> **· RETROBULBAR HAEMATOMA — proptosis, a tense eye, severe pain, reduced acuity and a RELATIVE AFFERENT PUPILLARY DEFECT. This is an orbital compartment syndrome and vision is lost within hours. It requires urgent LATERAL CANTHOTOMY AND CANTHOLYSIS**, which is a bedside procedure that may need to be performed before ophthalmology arrives.
> **· HYPHAEMA — blood in the anterior chamber; upright posture and urgent ophthalmology.**
> **· AVULSED PERMANENT TOOTH — TIME-CRITICAL. Handle by the crown only, do not scrub the root, rinse gently, and REIMPLANT IMMEDIATELY into the socket if possible.** **If not, transport in MILK, saliva, or a proprietary tooth preservation solution — NOT in water, which destroys the periodontal ligament cells.** **Deciduous teeth are NOT reimplanted.** **Urgent dental review.** This is examinable and the storage medium is the point.

**Ix:** **CT facial bones with fine cuts and reconstructions** (*why:* plain radiographs are inadequate for facial fractures; *what:* fracture pattern, orbital contents, sinus opacification). **CT brain and cervical spine** (*why:* high-energy facial trauma is associated with head and cervical injury; *what:* intracranial and spinal injury). **Visual acuity and pupil examination — documented, before swelling closes the eye** (*why:* it becomes impossible to assess later, and a relative afferent pupillary defect is the key sign of optic nerve compromise; *what:* acuity, RAPD). Examination of the nasal septum (*why:* septal haematoma; *what:* boggy septal swelling). Assessment of occlusion and facial nerve function (*why:* mandibular and facial nerve injury; *what:* malocclusion, nerve deficit).

## 0.3 Chest Trauma

> [!danger] Flail chest — the problem is the lung underneath, not the paradox
> **Three or more contiguous ribs fractured in two or more places, producing a segment that moves paradoxically.**
> **The paradoxical movement looks dramatic but is not the main threat — the underlying PULMONARY CONTUSION is**, and it evolves over the first 24–48 hours, so the initial chest radiograph may underestimate it.
> **Management: excellent ANALGESIA (regional techniques — intercostal, serratus anterior or paravertebral blocks, or epidural — rather than escalating opioids), respiratory physiotherapy, judicious fluid (over-resuscitation worsens contusion), oxygen, and non-invasive or invasive ventilation where required.** Surgical rib fixation is used in selected cases.

> [!warning] Rib fractures in the elderly are far more dangerous than they look
> **Mortality and pneumonia risk rise steeply with the number of rib fractures and with age.**
> **An older patient with three or more rib fractures needs ADMISSION, aggressive multimodal analgesia including regional blocks, respiratory physiotherapy and incentive spirometry — not discharge with oral analgesia.**
> **The mechanism of death is simple and preventable: pain → splinting → poor cough → atelectasis → pneumonia → respiratory failure.**
> **Analgesia is the treatment.**

> [!tip] Rib fracture location tells you what else to look for
> **· FIRST or SECOND rib fracture → very high energy. Look for aortic, brachial plexus, subclavian vessel and major airway injury.**
> **· Ribs 9–12 → INTRA-ABDOMINAL injury. LEFT-sided → SPLEEN. RIGHT-sided → LIVER.**
> **· Sternal fracture → blunt cardiac injury; get an ECG and consider troponin.**
> **· Scapular fracture → high energy.**

> [!danger] The potentially life-threatening injuries found after the primary survey
> **· PULMONARY CONTUSION — evolves over 24–48 hours; the initial film underestimates it.**
> **· BLUNT AORTIC INJURY — a rapid deceleration mechanism (high-speed collision, fall from height). Chest radiograph clues: a WIDENED MEDIASTINUM, loss of the aortic knuckle, left apical cap, depressed left main bronchus, deviated nasogastric tube. CT ANGIOGRAPHY is the investigation, and the injury is lethal if missed.**
> **· TRACHEOBRONCHIAL INJURY — persistent large air leak, massive surgical emphysema, a pneumothorax that will not re-expand despite a functioning drain.**
> **· BLUNT CARDIAC INJURY — arrhythmia, ECG changes, raised troponin; monitor.**
> **· DIAPHRAGMATIC RUPTURE — more often LEFT-sided (the liver protects the right). Bowel or the nasogastric tube seen in the chest is diagnostic. It may present LATE, months or years afterwards, with obstruction or strangulation.**
> **· OESOPHAGEAL INJURY — rare in blunt trauma, with mediastinal air and effusion.**

> [!tip] Chest drain insertion
> **Inserted in the "safe triangle" — bordered by the lateral border of pectoralis major, the anterior border of latissimus dorsi, a line at the level of the nipple, and the apex below the axilla.**
> **Blunt dissection over the TOP of the rib below**, avoiding the neurovascular bundle running beneath each rib.
> **Complications: injury to lung, heart, liver, spleen and diaphragm; malposition; and infection.**
> **A massive haemothorax with a large immediate drain output, or high ongoing output, is an indication for thoracotomy** — `UNVERIFIED — the volume thresholds.`

**Ix:** **Chest radiograph** (*why:* the primary survey adjunct — pneumothorax, haemothorax, mediastinal width, diaphragm; *what:* the findings above). **eFAST** (*why:* detects pericardial fluid and pneumothorax rapidly at the bedside; *what:* tamponade, pneumothorax). **CT chest with contrast / CT angiography** (*why:* the definitive assessment, and the only way to exclude aortic injury; *what:* aortic injury, contusion, occult pneumothorax, rib fractures). ECG and troponin (*why:* blunt cardiac injury, particularly with sternal fracture; *what:* arrhythmia, ischaemic change). ABG (*why:* oxygenation and ventilation; *what:* hypoxia, hypercapnia).

## 0.4 Abdominal Trauma

> [!tip] eFAST — know what it can and cannot do
> **It detects FREE FLUID. It does NOT reliably detect solid organ injury, hollow viscus injury or retroperitoneal injury.**
> **A NEGATIVE FAST DOES NOT EXCLUDE SIGNIFICANT ABDOMINAL INJURY** — this is the most important limitation and the source of most errors in its use.
> **Its value is in the UNSTABLE patient: a positive FAST in a hypotensive patient means the abdomen is the source and the patient goes to theatre, not to CT.**
> **In the STABLE patient, CT with intravenous contrast is the standard** and is far more informative.

> [!warning] The organs and their patterns
> **· SPLEEN — the commonest solid organ injured in blunt trauma.** Associated with **LEFT lower rib fractures** and with **KEHR SIGN — left shoulder tip pain from diaphragmatic irritation by blood**, classically worse when supine. **DELAYED splenic rupture occurs days to weeks later** — so a patient managed non-operatively needs clear advice about what to return for.
> **· LIVER — right upper quadrant, right lower rib fractures.**
> **· HOLLOW VISCUS and MESENTERY — the ones that are missed.** They may cause few early signs and present hours to days later with peritonitis. **FREE FLUID ON CT WITHOUT AN IDENTIFIED SOLID ORGAN INJURY SHOULD RAISE HOLLOW VISCUS OR MESENTERIC INJURY** and warrants observation, serial examination and repeat imaging rather than discharge.
> **· RETROPERITONEAL — duodenum, pancreas and kidneys.** **Pancreatic and duodenal injury from a handlebar or seatbelt, particularly in children, is subtle and frequently delayed in diagnosis.** Serial lipase and repeat imaging.

> [!danger] The seat belt sign
> **A band of bruising across the abdomen or chest from the restraint indicates significant transmitted energy.**
> **It is associated with: HOLLOW VISCUS injury (small bowel and mesentery) · and CHANCE FRACTURES of the lumbar spine — a flexion-distraction injury through the vertebral body and posterior elements.**
> **So a seat belt sign mandates careful abdominal assessment AND imaging of the lumbar spine**, and the spinal injury is missed if only the abdomen is scanned.

> [!tip] Non-operative management, and when it is not an option
> **Haemodynamically stable patients with solid organ injury are now routinely managed NON-OPERATIVELY**, with observation, serial examination and haemoglobin, and angioembolisation where there is a contrast blush.
> **Indications for laparotomy: haemodynamic instability with a positive FAST · peritonitis · evisceration · a retained impaling object · free intraperitoneal air · diaphragmatic rupture · and gunshot wounds traversing the peritoneum.**
> **Penetrating trauma: gunshot wounds crossing the peritoneum generally require laparotomy; stab wounds may be selectively managed** with local wound exploration, serial examination or CT depending on the trajectory and the institution.

**Ix:** **eFAST** (*why:* rapid identification of free fluid in the unstable patient; *what:* free fluid — with the limitations above). **CT abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast** (*why:* the standard in the stable patient, grading solid organ injury and identifying retroperitoneal and vascular injury; *what:* organ injury grade, contrast blush, free air, free fluid). Serial FBC and haemoglobin (*why:* ongoing bleeding in non-operative management; *what:* falling haemoglobin). **Serial abdominal examination by the same clinician where possible** (*why:* a changing examination is the most sensitive indicator of missed hollow viscus injury, and continuity makes the change detectable; *what:* developing peritonism). Lipase (*why:* pancreatic injury, particularly with a handlebar mechanism; *what:* rising lipase). **Urinalysis** (*why:* haematuria indicates renal tract injury and prompts dedicated imaging; *what:* blood). Lumbar spine imaging where a seat belt sign is present (*why:* Chance fracture; *what:* flexion-distraction injury).

## 0.5 Pelvic Trauma

> [!danger] Do NOT "spring" the pelvis
> **Repeated compression or distraction of the pelvis to test for instability is unreliable, painful, and — critically — can DISLODGE FORMED CLOT and precipitate further haemorrhage.**
> **The examination is not recommended.** If pelvic injury is suspected from the mechanism, **apply a binder and image.**

> [!danger] Apply the binder at the level of the GREATER TROCHANTERS
> **The commonest error is placing it too high, over the iliac crests, where it does nothing to reduce pelvic volume.**
> **The binder works by reducing an "open book" injury and reducing the pelvic volume, tamponading the venous plexus and cancellous bone bleeding.**
> **Position it over the greater trochanters, apply it before or during transfer, and do not remove it for imaging or examination without a plan.**

> [!tip] The mechanisms
> **· LATERAL COMPRESSION — the commonest.** Internal rotation of the hemipelvis; associated with head and abdominal injury.
> **· ANTEROPOSTERIOR COMPRESSION ("open book")** — external rotation, increasing pelvic volume, with **the highest haemorrhage risk** and the pattern the binder most helps.
> **· VERTICAL SHEAR** — fall from height; highly unstable.
> **The bleeding in pelvic fracture is predominantly VENOUS and from cancellous bone, with a minority arterial** — which is why mechanical stabilisation (binder, external fixator, packing) is as important as angioembolisation.
> Management depends on the institution: **binder and resuscitation → angioembolisation, preperitoneal packing, or external fixation**, in an order determined by local resources and the pattern of injury.

> [!danger] The associated injuries that determine management
> **· URETHRAL INJURY — blood at the urethral meatus, perineal or scrotal bruising, and a high-riding prostate.** **DO NOT ATTEMPT URETHRAL CATHETERISATION** — it can convert a partial tear into a complete disruption. **A retrograde urethrogram is performed first, and a suprapubic catheter may be needed.** **This is examinable and it is a genuine harm.**
> **· BLADDER RUPTURE** — intraperitoneal (usually from a full bladder and blunt force, requiring repair) or extraperitoneal (usually managed with catheter drainage).
> **· RECTAL OR VAGINAL INJURY — this makes the pelvic fracture an OPEN fracture**, with a much higher infection and mortality risk. **A rectal and vaginal examination is part of the assessment**, and any breach mandates urgent surgical involvement and antibiotics.
> **· Lumbosacral plexus and nerve root injury.**

**Ix:** **Pelvic radiograph as a primary survey adjunct** (*why:* identifies the disruption in the unstable patient without moving them to CT; *what:* symphyseal widening, sacroiliac disruption, fractures). **CT with contrast** (*why:* defines the fracture pattern and identifies arterial contrast extravasation directing embolisation; *what:* fracture, blush). **Retrograde urethrogram before catheterisation where urethral injury is suspected** (*why:* prevents converting a partial to a complete injury; *what:* extravasation). CT cystogram (*why:* bladder rupture and whether it is intra- or extraperitoneal; *what:* contrast extravasation pattern). **Rectal and vaginal examination** (*why:* identifies an open pelvic fracture, which changes prognosis and management entirely; *what:* mucosal breach, bleeding).

## 0.6 Trauma in Special Populations

> [!danger] The elderly — normal vital signs are falsely reassuring
> **· BETA-BLOCKERS AND OTHER RATE-LIMITING DRUGS BLUNT THE TACHYCARDIC RESPONSE**, so a "normal" heart rate does not mean a normal circulating volume.
> **· A patient with baseline hypertension may be profoundly shocked at a blood pressure that reads as normal** — a systolic of 110 in someone who normally runs at 170 is significant hypotension.
> **· A GROUND-LEVEL FALL IS A MAJOR TRAUMA MECHANISM IN AN OLDER PERSON**, and under-triage of these patients is well documented.
> **· ANTICOAGULATION means a low threshold for CT head even after apparently trivial injury**, and a low threshold for repeat imaging. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.8 and [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.4.
> **· Rib fractures, as above, carry disproportionate mortality.**
> **· Frailty predicts outcome better than injury severity score alone**, and should inform goals-of-care discussions early rather than after a prolonged ICU course. Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.2.
> **Lower every threshold: for trauma call activation, for imaging, for admission, and for senior involvement.**

> [!warning] Children
> **· Greater physiological reserve, then ABRUPT decompensation. HYPOTENSION IS A LATE AND PRE-TERMINAL SIGN** — tachycardia, poor perfusion, and altered conscious state come first and must be acted on.
> **· A larger head-to-body ratio** — more head injury, and a different flexion point in the cervical spine.
> **· A larger surface-area-to-mass ratio** — rapid HYPOTHERMIA. Keep them warm actively.
> **· Less protected abdominal organs** — a compliant rib cage and thin abdominal wall mean **significant liver, spleen and pancreatic injury can occur WITHOUT rib fractures or external marks.**
> **· Weight-based drug and fluid dosing**, with a length-based tape or a validated estimate.
> **· NON-ACCIDENTAL INJURY must be considered in every paediatric trauma presentation** — cross-refer [[L7_Fractures_and_Eponyms]] 0.5, [[GER4_Safeguarding_and_Forensic]] and [[M6_Paediatric_Respiratory_and_Neurology]].
> **· Family presence during resuscitation is generally supported and should be facilitated with a dedicated staff member.**

> [!danger] Pregnancy — resuscitate the mother
> **The best treatment for the fetus is effective maternal resuscitation. Do not divert attention from the mother to fetal monitoring.**
> **· AORTOCAVAL COMPRESSION after about 20 weeks: the gravid uterus compresses the inferior vena cava when supine, reducing venous return by a large fraction.** **Apply MANUAL LEFT UTERINE DISPLACEMENT, or tilt the patient (or the spinal board) to the left.** **This alone can restore a blood pressure.**
> **· Physiological changes MASK blood loss** — plasma volume is substantially increased, so **a pregnant woman can lose a large proportion of her blood volume before becoming hypotensive**, and by then the placental circulation has already been sacrificed. **Fetal distress may be the first sign of maternal hypovolaemia.**
> **· PLACENTAL ABRUPTION can occur after relatively minor trauma and may be CONCEALED, with little or no vaginal bleeding.** **Cardiotocographic monitoring for a defined period is required after significant trauma in a viable pregnancy.**
> **· Give ANTI-D to Rh-negative women after abdominal trauma**, and consider a Kleihauer test. `UNVERIFIED — the gestational thresholds and dosing.`
> **· PERIMORTEM CAESAREAN — in maternal cardiac arrest beyond around 20 weeks, delivery should be commenced early in the arrest**, both to improve maternal resuscitation by relieving aortocaval compression and for fetal survival. Cross-refer [[F0-4_Resuscitation_Algorithms_and_Emergency_Procedures]] and [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]].
> **· Do not withhold necessary imaging because of pregnancy** — the risk of a missed injury exceeds the radiation risk, though shielding and dose reduction should be used.

> [!tip] Two other groups
> **· Obesity** — imaging may be limited by table weight and bore size, vascular access is harder, landmarks are obscured, and drug dosing requires thought. **Plan for these before they become urgent.**
> **· Anticoagulated and antiplatelet patients** — establish the agent and the time of the last dose early, and **reverse promptly where there is significant bleeding or intracranial injury.** Cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.4.

**Ix:** As per the preceding sections, with **a pregnancy test in every woman of reproductive age** (*why:* it changes positioning, imaging decisions, monitoring and disposition; *what:* positive). **Cardiotocography after significant trauma in a viable pregnancy** (*why:* detects abruption and fetal distress, and abruption may be concealed; *what:* uterine irritability, fetal heart rate abnormality). **Kleihauer test in Rh-negative pregnant women** (*why:* quantifies fetomaternal haemorrhage and guides anti-D dosing; *what:* fetal cells in maternal circulation). **A collateral history and baseline observations for older patients** (*why:* establishes what their normal blood pressure and function actually are, which is what makes the current numbers interpretable; *what:* baseline).
