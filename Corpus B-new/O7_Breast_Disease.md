## 0.1 Triple Assessment

> [!danger] Triple assessment means all three components, and they must be CONCORDANT
> **1. CLINICAL assessment — history and examination.**
> **2. IMAGING — ultrasound and/or mammography, chosen by age.**
> **3. PATHOLOGY — core biopsy.**
> **The critical principle is CONCORDANCE. If any one component is suspicious and the others are not, the case is NOT closed.**
> **A benign biopsy result in a clinically or radiologically suspicious lump means the biopsy may have missed the lesion — it does not mean the lump is benign.** **Escalate: repeat sampling, excision biopsy, or multidisciplinary discussion.**
> **Discordance is the mechanism by which cancers are missed after a "normal" biopsy**, and recognising it is the single most important safety concept in breast assessment.

> [!tip] Imaging is chosen by age, because breast density changes
> **· Younger women (broadly under 35–40) — ULTRASOUND first.** Dense glandular tissue makes mammography insensitive, and the yield of malignancy is low.
> **· Older women — MAMMOGRAPHY plus ULTRASOUND.**
> **· MRI — for high-risk screening (BRCA carriers, chest irradiation), assessing disease extent particularly in LOBULAR carcinoma, and for implant assessment.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the age thresholds.`
> **CORE BIOPSY is preferred over fine needle aspiration**, because it provides tissue architecture (distinguishing invasive from in situ disease), grade, and **receptor status (ER, PR, HER2)** — all of which determine treatment. **FNA retains a role for cyst aspiration and for lymph node sampling.**

> [!warning] Take the family history properly — it changes the pathway
> **The features that suggest a hereditary predisposition:**
> **· Multiple affected relatives on the SAME side of the family.**
> **· YOUNG age at diagnosis (particularly under 50).**
> **· BILATERAL breast cancer.**
> **· MALE breast cancer — a strong indicator, particularly of BRCA2.**
> **· OVARIAN cancer in the family, and breast plus ovarian in the same person or family.**
> **· Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry.**
> **· Known familial mutation.**
> **These warrant referral to a familial cancer service**, because the implications extend to surveillance, risk-reducing surgery, treatment choice (PARP inhibitors) and cascade testing of relatives.

> [!tip] Risk factors — dominated by age and oestrogen exposure
> **· AGE — the dominant risk factor by a wide margin.**
> **· Family history and germline mutations (BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2 and others).**
> **· Lifetime oestrogen exposure — early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, later first pregnancy, and menopausal hormone therapy.**
> **· Previous breast biopsy showing ATYPIA or lobular carcinoma in situ.**
> **· Previous CHEST RADIOTHERAPY, particularly for lymphoma in adolescence — these women need early, intensive surveillance and it is frequently overlooked.**
> **· Dense breast tissue · ALCOHOL · obesity after menopause · physical inactivity.**
> **Protective: parity, BREASTFEEDING, and physical activity.**

## 0.2 Breast Lump

> [!danger] Every discrete new breast lump requires triple assessment
> **Age, a benign-feeling lump, and an absent family history are not sufficient grounds for reassurance without assessment.**
> **The one-line rule: if you can feel a discrete lump, it gets assessed** — the pathway differs by age, but the assessment happens.

> [!tip] The common causes and how they feel
> **· FIBROADENOMA — younger women. Smooth, firm, well-defined, highly MOBILE ("breast mouse").** Usually observed once triple assessment confirms it; excised if large, growing or symptomatic.
> **· CYST — often fluctuates with the cycle, may be tender, smooth and sometimes tense.** **Aspiration is both diagnostic and therapeutic where symptomatic.** **Two findings after aspiration mandate further assessment: BLOODSTAINED aspirate, and a RESIDUAL MASS after complete aspiration.**
> **· CARCINOMA — hard, irregular, poorly defined, fixed to skin or chest wall, with skin dimpling or tethering, nipple retraction, or palpable axillary nodes.** **But early cancers can feel unremarkable**, which is why the imaging and biopsy are not optional.
> **· FAT NECROSIS — after trauma, surgery or radiotherapy. It can mimic carcinoma clinically AND radiologically**, so it usually still requires biopsy.
> **· PHYLLODES TUMOUR — rapidly growing, often large, resembling a fibroadenoma but enlarging quickly. It can be borderline or malignant and requires wide excision.**
> **· Lipoma, galactocele, abscess, and duct ectasia.**

> [!danger] Male breast lump — gynaecomastia versus carcinoma
> **GYNAECOMASTIA is CONCENTRIC, rubbery, symmetrically arranged around and beneath the nipple, often bilateral and often tender.**
> **CARCINOMA is ECCENTRIC (off to one side of the nipple), hard, fixed, painless, and may have skin or nipple change and axillary nodes.**
> **Male breast cancer exists, presents late because neither the patient nor the clinician expects it, and is associated with BRCA2** — which has implications for the man's relatives.
> **An eccentric hard male breast lump gets triple assessment, not reassurance.**

> [!tip] Gynaecomastia — look for a cause
> **Physiological: neonatal, pubertal (common, usually resolves), and in older men.**
> **DRUGS — the commonest identifiable cause and the most easily reversed: SPIRONOLACTONE · digoxin · cimetidine · antipsychotics (via prolactin) · antiretrovirals · anti-androgens and androgen deprivation therapy · finasteride · anabolic steroids · CANNABIS · and alcohol.**
> **Systemic: chronic liver disease · hypogonadism (primary or secondary) · hyperthyroidism · chronic kidney disease · refeeding after starvation.**
> **Tumours: hCG-secreting germ cell tumours (TESTICULAR — examine the testes) and adrenal or Leydig cell tumours.**
> **Rapid onset, pain, a large size, or a young man should prompt a search for a hormone-secreting tumour** — check hCG, and examine the testes. Cross-refer [[I4]] 0.6 and [[H4]].

**Ix:** **Triple assessment** as per 0.1 (*why:* the standard, and concordance is what makes it safe; *what:* clinical, imaging and pathological findings). **Aspiration of a symptomatic cyst** (*why:* diagnostic and therapeutic, and the findings determine next steps; *what:* fluid character, residual mass). **In male gynaecomastia: LFT, UEC, TFTs, testosterone, LH, FSH, oestradiol, prolactin, and βhCG, with testicular examination and ultrasound if indicated** (*why:* identifies the hormonal or systemic cause and excludes a secreting tumour; *what:* the underlying cause). **Medication review** (*why:* drugs are the commonest reversible cause of gynaecomastia; *what:* culprit drug).

## 0.3 Breast Pain

> [!tip] Cyclical versus non-cyclical
> **CYCLICAL — bilateral, diffuse, heaviest in the upper outer quadrants, worst in the days before menstruation and relieved by its onset.** Common, hormonally driven, and benign.
> **NON-CYCLICAL — often unilateral, localised, constant, and unrelated to the cycle.** Causes include **chest wall pain (costochondritis, Tietze syndrome), large or unsupported breasts, previous surgery or trauma, medication (hormone therapy, hormonal contraception, some antidepressants), mastitis, and referred pain — including CARDIAC and pleuritic pain, which should be considered before the breast is blamed.**

> [!danger] Breast pain alone is rarely a symptom of cancer
> **This is a genuinely reassuring and useful fact, and it should be given** — most women presenting with breast pain are frightened of cancer even when they do not say so.
> **But: examine both breasts and the axillae, and arrange imaging if the pain is FOCAL and PERSISTENT, if there is any associated lump or skin change, or if the woman's age warrants it.**
> **Reassurance after a proper assessment is treatment. Reassurance instead of an assessment is not.**

> [!tip] Chest wall pain is frequently mislabelled as breast pain
> **Localised, reproducible tenderness over the costochondral junctions or chest wall musculature — and it is confirmed by rolling the patient onto her side so the breast tissue falls away from the chest wall: if the tenderness follows the chest wall rather than the breast, it is musculoskeletal.**
> **This simple manoeuvre distinguishes them and permits confident, specific reassurance.**

> [!warning] Management is mostly non-pharmacological
> **· A well-fitted, supportive bra — including a sports bra for exercise and sometimes a soft bra at night.** **Poor bra fit is common and is a genuinely under-recognised cause**, and referral for professional fitting helps a substantial proportion.
> **· Simple analgesia and topical NSAIDs.**
> **· Review hormonal contraception and hormone therapy**, which may be contributing.
> **· A pain and cycle diary** to establish whether it is cyclical.
> **· Evidence for evening primrose oil is weak**, though it is widely used and harmless.
> **· Specific pharmacotherapy (danazol, tamoxifen) is reserved for severe refractory pain** and carries significant adverse effects.
> `UNVERIFIED — the evidence and dosing for pharmacological options.`

**Ix:** **Examination of both breasts, axillae and the chest wall, including the side-lying manoeuvre** (*why:* distinguishes breast from chest wall pain, and identifies an associated mass; *what:* focal tenderness, mass). Imaging **where the pain is focal, persistent, or associated with other findings, and by age-appropriate criteria** (*why:* excludes underlying pathology and allows definitive reassurance; *what:* normal or abnormal imaging). Consider cardiac and pleuritic causes (*why:* referred pain presents as breast pain, particularly on the left; *what:* ECG, other findings as indicated).

## 0.4 Nipple Discharge and Nipple Changes

> [!danger] The features that make discharge concerning
> **· SPONTANEOUS (staining clothing or bedding) rather than only expressed.**
> **· UNILATERAL.**
> **· SINGLE DUCT.**
> **· BLOODSTAINED or serosanguinous.**
> **· Associated with a MASS.**
> **· Older age.**
> **Reassuring by contrast: bilateral, multiple ducts, expressed only on squeezing, milky, green, yellow or brown, in a younger woman.**
> **The concerning pattern warrants triple assessment.**

> [!tip] The causes
> **· DUCT ECTASIA — older women, green, brown or creamy discharge from MULTIPLE ducts, often bilateral, sometimes with nipple retraction. Benign.**
> **· INTRADUCTAL PAPILLOMA — the commonest cause of SINGLE-DUCT BLOODSTAINED discharge. Benign, but requires excision to exclude malignancy and because it may harbour atypia.**
> **· CARCINOMA and DCIS — can present with bloodstained single-duct discharge.**
> **· GALACTORRHOEA — bilateral milky discharge NOT related to lactation. This is an ENDOCRINE problem, not a breast problem: check a PREGNANCY TEST, PROLACTIN, TFTs, and the MEDICATION LIST (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, SSRIs, opioids).** Cross-refer [[I4]] 0.2.
> **· PERIDUCTAL MASTITIS — strongly associated with SMOKING**, with subareolar inflammation, discharge, and sometimes a mammary duct fistula.

> [!danger] Paget disease of the nipple — biopsy persistent nipple eczema
> **An eczematous, scaly, weeping or ulcerated change of the NIPPLE, usually spreading onto the areola, and it is associated with an underlying breast malignancy in the majority of cases.**
> **The discriminator from ordinary eczema: ECZEMA typically affects the AREOLA and SPARES the nipple, is usually BILATERAL, and RESPONDS to topical steroid. PAGET DISEASE starts at the NIPPLE, is UNILATERAL, and does NOT respond.**
> **Any persistent unilateral nipple change that has not resolved with a short course of appropriate topical treatment must be BIOPSIED.**
> **It is routinely treated as dermatitis for months, which is the mechanism of delayed diagnosis.**

> [!warning] The other nipple and skin changes
> **· NEW nipple INVERSION or retraction, particularly unilateral — concerning. Longstanding, symmetrical inversion is usually benign.**
> **· SKIN DIMPLING or TETHERING — caused by tumour involving Cooper ligaments. Look for it with the arms raised and with the hands pressed on the hips, which accentuates it.**
> **· PEAU D'ORANGE — dermal lymphatic obstruction producing skin oedema with accentuated pores.**
> **· INFLAMMATORY BREAST CANCER — a rapidly developing red, swollen, warm, tender breast with peau d'orange, WITHOUT the fever and systemic illness of infection, and NOT responding to antibiotics.** **Any "mastitis" that does not respond promptly to antibiotics, particularly in a woman who is not breastfeeding, must be assessed for inflammatory breast cancer.** See 0.6.

**Ix:** **Triple assessment for concerning discharge** (*why:* excludes malignancy; *what:* imaging and histology). **Testing discharge for blood** (*why:* frank or occult blood raises concern; *what:* blood). **Prolactin, pregnancy test, TFTs and medication review for bilateral milky discharge** (*why:* galactorrhoea is endocrine and needs a different workup entirely; *what:* hyperprolactinaemia, pregnancy, hypothyroidism, drug cause). **BIOPSY of persistent nipple skin change** (*why:* Paget disease; *what:* histology). Ductography or MRI in selected cases (*why:* localises an intraductal lesion; *what:* filling defect).

## 0.5 Breast Cancer

> [!tip] The types and what they mean practically
> **· DCIS (ductal carcinoma in situ) — pre-invasive, confined within the duct.** Frequently detected by screening as microcalcification. Treated to prevent progression to invasive disease.
> **· INVASIVE DUCTAL CARCINOMA (no special type) — the commonest.**
> **· INVASIVE LOBULAR CARCINOMA — grows in single-file strands rather than forming a discrete mass, so it is HARDER TO FEEL and HARDER TO SEE on mammography, is more often detected late, has a greater tendency to be bilateral and multifocal, and is the type for which MRI is most often used to assess extent.** **A vague area of thickening rather than a discrete lump is a characteristic presentation.**
> **· Receptor status — ER, PR and HER2 — is determined on the biopsy and drives treatment. "Triple negative" disease lacks all three, is more common in younger women and in BRCA1 carriers, and has fewer targeted options.**

> [!info] Treatment principles worth knowing at intern level
> **· SURGERY — breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy has EQUIVALENT SURVIVAL to mastectomy for suitable tumours**, which is an important thing to be able to say to a frightened patient.
> **· SENTINEL LYMPH NODE BIOPSY has replaced routine axillary clearance** in clinically node-negative disease, substantially reducing lymphoedema.
> **· NEOADJUVANT (pre-operative) systemic therapy is increasingly used** — to downstage tumours, permit conservation, and assess response in vivo.
> **· ENDOCRINE THERAPY for hormone receptor-positive disease — TAMOXIFEN and AROMATASE INHIBITORS**, taken for years, and see the adverse effects below.
> **· ANTI-HER2 therapy** for HER2-positive disease.
> **· Radiotherapy and chemotherapy** per protocol and multidisciplinary decision.

> [!danger] The complications an intern actually encounters
> **· NEUTROPENIC SEPSIS during chemotherapy — a medical emergency requiring antibiotics within a short defined window.** Cross-refer [[K1]] 0.5.
> **· ANTHRACYCLINE AND TRASTUZUMAB CARDIOTOXICITY — left ventricular dysfunction, monitored with serial echocardiography.** **A breast cancer patient with new breathlessness needs the heart considered, not just the lungs.** Cross-refer [[CV-X]] 0.1.
> **· TAMOXIFEN — increased VTE risk, and increased ENDOMETRIAL HYPERPLASIA AND CARCINOMA risk. Any abnormal vaginal bleeding in a woman on tamoxifen requires endometrial assessment.** Cross-refer [[O4]] 0.3. It also causes hot flushes and is associated with cataract.
> **· AROMATASE INHIBITORS — accelerated BONE LOSS requiring bone density monitoring and often bisphosphonate therapy, plus prominent ARTHRALGIA which is a common reason for non-adherence** and should be asked about and managed rather than left to cause silent discontinuation. Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.6.
> **· LYMPHOEDEMA after axillary surgery or radiotherapy** — cross-refer [[B6]] 0.3. **Conventional practice avoids venepuncture, cannulation and blood pressure measurement in the affected arm, although the evidence for this is now less certain than the strength of the convention suggests.** **In an emergency, do not withhold necessary vascular access from a patient because of this.**
> **· FERTILITY — discuss and refer for fertility preservation BEFORE starting gonadotoxic treatment.** This is time-critical and is a well-documented gap, particularly for younger women who are focused on survival at the point of diagnosis.
> **· Menopausal symptoms induced by treatment — and vaginal oestrogen can often be used, in discussion with the oncologist**, rather than leaving women with severe genitourinary symptoms untreated. Cross-refer [[O4]] 0.6.

> [!warning] BreastScreen Australia — and the screening/diagnostic distinction
> **BreastScreen Australia provides free screening mammography to asymptomatic women within a defined target age range, at a defined interval.** `UNVERIFIED — the target age range, the invited range, and the interval.`
> **THE CRITICAL POINT: SCREENING IS FOR ASYMPTOMATIC WOMEN.**
> **A woman with a lump, nipple discharge, skin change or any breast symptom must be referred for DIAGNOSTIC assessment — not sent to BreastScreen.**
> **Sending a symptomatic woman for screening delays her diagnosis**, and it happens.
> **Also: a normal recent screening mammogram does NOT exclude cancer in a woman with a new symptom** — interval cancers occur, and lobular cancers in particular are mammographically occult.
> **High-risk women (BRCA carriers, previous chest radiotherapy, strong family history) require a different, more intensive surveillance program including MRI**, arranged through a familial cancer or breast service rather than through population screening.

> [!tip] Metastatic disease — where it goes
> **BONE is the commonest site** — presenting with pain, pathological fracture, **HYPERCALCAEMIA** (cross-refer [[I3]] 0.2) and **METASTATIC SPINAL CORD COMPRESSION** (cross-refer [[L4]] 0.3).
> **Also liver, lung and pleura, and BRAIN.**
> **New back pain in a woman with a history of breast cancer is metastatic disease until proven otherwise**, and it precedes neurological signs by weeks — which is the window.

**Ix:** **Triple assessment** (*why:* the diagnostic standard; *what:* concordant findings). Receptor and HER2 status on the biopsy (*why:* determines systemic therapy; *what:* ER, PR, HER2). Staging investigations per protocol (*why:* determines treatment intent; *what:* nodal and distant disease). **Baseline echocardiography before anthracyclines or trastuzumab** (*why:* cardiotoxicity monitoring; *what:* ejection fraction). **Bone density before and during aromatase inhibitor therapy** (*why:* accelerated bone loss; *what:* reduced density). **Genetic assessment where family history, young age, triple negative or male breast cancer** (*why:* changes treatment and has family implications; *what:* pathogenic variant). **Fertility referral before gonadotoxic therapy** (*why:* time-critical and frequently missed; *what:* preservation options).

## 0.6 Breast Infection and Lactation

> [!danger] Continue breastfeeding through mastitis — it is part of the treatment
> **Lactational mastitis is usually caused by inadequate milk removal leading to inflammation, with or without infection (*Staphylococcus aureus* most commonly).**
> **EFFECTIVE MILK REMOVAL IS THE PRIMARY TREATMENT.** **Continuing to breastfeed or express from the affected breast is safe for the baby and essential for resolution — stopping makes it worse and risks abscess formation.**
> **Women are frequently and wrongly advised to stop feeding**, and this advice causes harm.
> **Management: continue feeding, correct attachment and positioning, analgesia and anti-inflammatories, rest and fluids, and ANTIBIOTICS where there are systemic features, a nipple fissure, or no improvement within about 12–24 hours of improved milk removal.** `UNVERIFIED — antibiotic choice and duration, and the current guidance on management, which has been revised.`

> [!warning] Breast abscess — aspirate rather than incise
> **Ultrasound-guided needle aspiration, repeated if necessary, is preferred over incision and drainage for most breast abscesses** — better cosmesis, less disruption, and **breastfeeding can usually continue.**
> Incision and drainage is reserved for large or non-resolving collections.

> [!danger] Inflammatory breast cancer masquerades as mastitis
> **A red, swollen, warm breast with peau d'orange — WITHOUT fever or systemic illness, and NOT responding to antibiotics.**
> **Any presumed mastitis that has not clearly improved on appropriate antibiotics, and any inflammatory breast presentation in a woman who is NOT breastfeeding, requires urgent imaging and biopsy.**
> **The delay caused by successive antibiotic courses is the classic mechanism of late diagnosis in this aggressive cancer.**

> [!tip] Non-lactational and periductal mastitis
> **Periductal mastitis is STRONGLY associated with SMOKING** — recurrent subareolar inflammation, abscess and sometimes a mammary duct fistula, often with anaerobic organisms.
> **Smoking cessation is a core part of management**, and recurrence is common without it.

> [!warning] Lactation problems — most nipple pain is an attachment problem
> **· NIPPLE PAIN AND TRAUMA — the commonest cause by far is poor ATTACHMENT and POSITIONING**, and the treatment is skilled observation of a feed by a lactation consultant or midwife, not creams. **Observing a feed is the assessment.**
> **· ENGORGEMENT — in the early days; frequent effective feeding, cold packs between feeds, and expressing enough for comfort.**
> **· BLOCKED DUCT — a tender lump; continued feeding, positioning changes and gentle measures.**
> **· LOW SUPPLY versus PERCEIVED low supply — the latter is far commoner. The objective markers are wet nappies and weight gain**, not the feel of the breasts or the volume expressed.
> **· NIPPLE CANDIDIASIS — burning, radiating nipple pain persisting after feeds, often after antibiotics; treat mother and baby simultaneously.**
> **· RAYNAUD PHENOMENON OF THE NIPPLE — blanching then colour change with severe pain after a feed, triggered by cold. Frequently misdiagnosed as thrush.**
> **· Tongue-tie — a contested area; assess for functional feeding impact rather than appearance alone.**

> [!tip] Two practical supports and one common error
> **· AUSTRALIAN BREASTFEEDING ASSOCIATION, lactation consultants (IBCLC), midwives and child health nurses** — refer early rather than after weeks of struggle.
> **· MEDICATION IN BREASTFEEDING — most medicines are compatible with breastfeeding, and the reflex advice to "stop breastfeeding" or to withhold necessary treatment is usually wrong and causes real harm.** **Consult a specialist medicines-in-pregnancy-and-lactation information service rather than deciding from a product information leaflet, which is written defensively.** `UNVERIFIED — the relevant Australian services.`
> **· Screen for postnatal depression at these contacts** — breastfeeding difficulty and postnatal mental health are closely linked in both directions. Cross-refer [[O3]].

**Ix:** **Observation of a breastfeed** (*why:* the diagnostic test for most lactation problems, and it cannot be done by history alone; *what:* attachment, positioning, transfer). **Ultrasound where an abscess is suspected** (*why:* confirms a drainable collection and guides aspiration; *what:* collection). Milk or swab culture where infection is not responding (*why:* directs antibiotics, including resistant organisms; *what:* organism). **Urgent imaging and BIOPSY for any inflammatory breast presentation not responding to antibiotics** (*why:* inflammatory breast cancer; *what:* malignancy). Infant weight and output assessment (*why:* the objective measure of milk transfer; *what:* weight gain, wet nappies).
