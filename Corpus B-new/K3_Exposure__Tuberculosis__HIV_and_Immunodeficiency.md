## 0.1 Occupational and Bloodborne Virus Exposure

**D:** Potential exposure to blood or body fluids capable of transmitting **hepatitis B, hepatitis C or HIV** — most commonly a percutaneous needlestick or sharps injury, or a mucous membrane or non-intact skin splash.

> [!danger] Immediate first aid — and what NOT to do
> **· Percutaneous: wash the wound with soap and running water.**
> **· DO NOT squeeze, scrub, or suck the wound**, and **do not apply bleach, alcohol or antiseptic into the wound** — these damage tissue and there is no evidence they reduce transmission.
> **· Mucous membrane or eye: irrigate copiously with water or saline**, with contact lenses removed and then also irrigated.
> **· Then report immediately** — to the supervisor and to occupational health or the emergency department, because **the PEP window is measured in hours.**

> [!tip] Risk assessment — three variables
> **1. The exposure type.** **Percutaneous > mucous membrane > non-intact skin. Intact skin is NOT an exposure.**
> Higher risk percutaneous features: **a hollow-bore needle, visible blood on the device, a deep injury, and a needle that had been in the source's artery or vein.**
> **2. The source.** Known status, viral load, and treatment. **Testing the source requires their consent** — it cannot be done covertly.
> **3. The recipient.** **Hepatitis B vaccination status and documented immunity** is the single most important recipient variable, because hepatitis B is both the most transmissible and the most preventable of the three.

> [!warning] The transmission risk hierarchy
> **Hepatitis B ≫ hepatitis C > HIV.**
> This surprises people, who worry most about HIV. **Hepatitis B is by far the most transmissible via percutaneous exposure — and it is the one for which the recipient can already be fully protected by vaccination.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the per-exposure transmission risk figures for each virus.`

> [!tip] What each virus requires
> **· Hepatitis B** — management depends on **the recipient's vaccination and anti-HBs status** and the source's status. Options are **hepatitis B immunoglobulin, vaccination, or both, or neither if the recipient is documented immune.** Highly effective when given promptly.
> **· Hepatitis C** — **there is NO post-exposure prophylaxis.** Management is **surveillance with HCV RNA PCR and serology**, with the important point that **hepatitis C is now curable with direct-acting antivirals**, so early detection leads to treatment and cure rather than chronic infection. This is a major change from historical practice.
> **· HIV** — **post-exposure prophylaxis, started as soon as possible and ideally within hours.** There is a defined outer window beyond which it is not recommended, and **the shorter the delay the better it works.** A course of combination antiretroviral therapy is taken for a defined period, with baseline and follow-up testing.
> `UNVERIFIED — the HBIG and vaccine indications by anti-HBs level, the HIV PEP window, regimen and duration, and the follow-up serology schedule.`

> [!warning] The counselling and follow-up that goes with it
> **Until seroconversion is excluded**: safe sex, **no blood, tissue or organ donation**, no sharing of razors or toothbrushes, and — for a breastfeeding woman — a discussion about feeding.
> **Follow-up serology at defined intervals**, and PEP adherence support, since the side effects cause many people to stop early.
> **These events are genuinely distressing** for healthcare workers and students, and the anxiety is frequently disproportionate to the actual risk. **Give the numbers, because they are reassuring**, and offer support.
> **Report through the incident system** — this is both an individual and a workplace safety matter, and pattern data drives prevention.

**Ix:** **Source testing with consent — HIV, hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis C antibody and PCR** (*why:* a negative source result usually ends the process and avoids unnecessary PEP; *what:* status and, where positive, viral load). **Recipient baseline serology — HIV, hepatitis B surface antibody and antigen, hepatitis C** (*why:* establishes pre-exposure status, which is essential medicolegally and for interpreting later results; *what:* baseline status). **Recipient hepatitis B vaccination history and anti-HBs level** (*why:* determines whether immunoglobulin is needed at all; *what:* documented immunity). Follow-up serology at the specified intervals (*why:* detects seroconversion; *what:* conversion). Pregnancy test where PEP is being considered (*why:* affects regimen choice; *what:* positive). UEC and LFT (*why:* baseline before antiretroviral PEP; *what:* renal and hepatic function).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
First aid, report, risk assess, **and start HIV PEP without waiting for source results if the risk assessment warrants it — it can be stopped if the source tests negative.** Hepatitis B immunoglobulin and vaccine as indicated.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Complete PEP, follow-up serology, and treat any seroconversion — **with the important point that acute hepatitis C is curable.**

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Ensure the healthcare worker is hepatitis B vaccinated and their immunity documented** — this is the single most effective preventive measure and there is no excuse for a clinician not knowing their own anti-HBs status. Safe sharps practice, **never recap needles**, and appropriate PPE.

## 0.2 Non-Occupational Exposure, PEP and PrEP

> [!tip] Non-occupational post-exposure prophylaxis (NPEP)
> The same principles and the same time window apply to **sexual exposure, sharing of injecting equipment, and community needlestick injuries.**
> **Risk is stratified by the type of exposure and the likelihood the source is HIV positive with a detectable viral load** — receptive anal intercourse carries the highest per-act risk, and a source on effective treatment with an undetectable viral load carries essentially none.
> **Community needlestick injuries from discarded needles carry very low HIV risk** — the virus does not survive well outside the body — but hepatitis B remains a consideration and the anxiety is often high.
> `UNVERIFIED — NPEP eligibility criteria, risk categories and regimens from ASHM guidelines.`

> [!info] Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) — Australian practice
> **PrEP is antiretroviral medication taken by HIV-negative people at ongoing risk, and it is highly effective at preventing HIV acquisition. It is PBS-listed in Australia and can be prescribed in general practice.**
> Practical requirements: **confirm HIV-negative status before starting and at intervals · check hepatitis B status (because the drugs are active against hepatitis B and stopping them can cause a flare) · monitor renal function · and screen regularly for other sexually transmitted infections**, since PrEP does not prevent them and STI rates in PrEP users are high.
> **Knowing that PrEP exists and how to refer for it is a practical intern-level competency**, and it comes up in sexual health, emergency and general practice contexts. Cross-refer [[O6]]. `UNVERIFIED — eligibility, regimens, on-demand versus daily dosing, and monitoring intervals.`

> [!danger] Sexual assault — a specific and multi-component pathway
> **This is not just an infection risk assessment.** The management involves, in parallel:
> **· Immediate safety and psychological support**, with the person's control over the process respected at every step.
> **· Forensic examination — but ONLY with informed consent, ideally by a specialist service**, and it must not be a prerequisite for medical care. There is a time window for evidence collection, but **a person who declines forensic examination still receives all medical care.**
> **· Emergency contraception and pregnancy testing.**
> **· STI testing and presumptive treatment**, with a plan for repeat testing outside the window period.
> **· HIV PEP assessment and hepatitis B prophylaxis.**
> **· Documentation that is careful, contemporaneous and factual.**
> **· Consideration of mandatory reporting obligations where the person is a child**, and awareness of the reporting position for adults, which differs.
> **Referral to a specialist sexual assault service where available is far better than ad hoc management in an emergency department.** Cross-refer [[GER4]] and [[O6]]. `UNVERIFIED — the SA forensic and reporting pathway, and the relevant services.`

**Ix:** As per 0.1, plus **STI screening — chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAAT from all relevant sites (throat, anus, genital as indicated by exposure), syphilis serology, and hepatitis serology** (*why:* site-specific testing matters because **a genital swab alone misses pharyngeal and rectal infection**; *what:* positive NAAT or serology). Pregnancy test (*why:* emergency contraception and management decisions; *what:* positive).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Risk assess, offer PEP within the window, provide emergency contraception and STI prophylaxis where indicated, and address safety and support first.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Complete prophylaxis, arrange follow-up testing, and refer to sexual health or a specialist service.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Discuss PrEP for those at ongoing risk** — an exposure event is a natural point to raise it. Psychological support and partner notification where relevant.

## 0.3 Tuberculosis — Latent Infection, Contacts and Active Disease

> [!tip] Latent versus active — the distinction that governs everything
> **Latent TB infection: the person is infected, has NO symptoms, is NOT infectious, and has a normal chest radiograph.** They have viable organisms contained by the immune system.
> **Active TB disease: symptomatic, and — if pulmonary — potentially infectious.**
> **Lifetime reactivation risk from latent infection is modest in an immunocompetent person, but rises dramatically with immunosuppression** — HIV above all, and also **TNF inhibitors, transplantation, dialysis, silicosis, diabetes, malnutrition, and recent infection.** Young children are also at higher risk of progression and of severe forms.

> [!danger] Screen for latent TB before starting immunosuppression
> **This is the most common way an intern encounters TB, and it is mandatory before TNF inhibitors in particular**, because reactivation on a TNF inhibitor can be rapid, disseminated and fatal.
> **Screening is by tuberculin skin test (Mantoux) or an interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA), plus a chest radiograph and a risk history.**
> **IGRA is not confounded by prior BCG vaccination**, which matters enormously in Australia's overseas-born population where BCG is common — a positive Mantoux in a BCG-vaccinated person is uninterpretable, and this is precisely why IGRA is preferred in that group.
> `UNVERIFIED — which test in which population, cut-offs, and the latent TB treatment regimens.`

> [!warning] Active TB — the presentation, and why it is missed
> **Pulmonary: cough persisting beyond two to three weeks, haemoptysis, fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss and anorexia.** The cough is often attributed to smoking, asthma or recurrent bronchitis for months.
> **Extrapulmonary — and this is where it is genuinely missed**: **lymph node (cervical, "scrofula" — matted, non-tender, may form a sinus) · pleural effusion · miliary (disseminated, with a diffuse micronodular chest radiograph and a very unwell patient) · CNS (tuberculous meningitis — subacute, with cranial nerve palsies and very high CSF protein with low glucose) · spinal (Pott disease, with vertebral destruction and possible cord compression) · genitourinary (sterile pyuria) · abdominal · pericardial.**
> **Extrapulmonary disease is proportionally more common in HIV and in children**, and the chest radiograph may be atypical or normal in advanced HIV.

> [!danger] Isolate first, then investigate
> **Suspected pulmonary TB requires airborne precautions immediately — a negative-pressure room where available, and a fitted P2/N95 respirator for staff — not a surgical mask.**
> **TB is notifiable**, and **contact tracing is conducted by the public health unit**, not by the treating team, but the notification must be made.
> Placing a suspected case in an open ward while awaiting results exposes staff and patients and is a recurring failure.

> [!tip] Diagnosis
> **· Sputum — three specimens, including an early morning sample — for acid-fast bacilli smear and mycobacterial culture.** **Culture is the gold standard but takes weeks.**
> **· Nucleic acid amplification (Xpert MTB/RIF and successors)** — a rapid PCR that detects *M. tuberculosis* **and simultaneously detects rifampicin resistance within hours**, which has transformed early management and is now the key diagnostic advance to know about.
> **· Chest radiograph** — classically **upper lobe infiltrates with cavitation** in reactivation disease, but **atypical, lower-zone or normal in HIV and immunosuppression.**
> **· Tissue** for histology (caseating granulomas) and culture in extrapulmonary disease.

> [!danger] The four first-line drugs and their toxicities — high-yield
> **· Isoniazid — hepatitis, and PERIPHERAL NEUROPATHY, which is why PYRIDOXINE (vitamin B6) is co-prescribed.**
> **· Rifampicin — hepatitis, ORANGE discolouration of urine, tears and sweat (warn the patient, and warn contact lens wearers), and it is a POTENT ENZYME INDUCER.** **The enzyme induction causes ORAL CONTRACEPTIVE FAILURE, and interacts with warfarin, antiretrovirals, corticosteroids, anticonvulsants and many others.** **The contraceptive interaction is a classic exam point and a real cause of unplanned pregnancy.**
> **· Pyrazinamide — hepatitis and hyperuricaemia (may precipitate gout).**
> **· Ethambutol — OPTIC NEURITIS with loss of visual acuity and RED-GREEN COLOUR DISCRIMINATION.** **Baseline and monitoring of visual acuity and colour vision is required, and the patient must be told to report any visual change immediately.**
> **Three of the four are hepatotoxic** — baseline and monitored LFTs, and a clear plan for what to do if they rise.
> `UNVERIFIED — regimens, phases, durations, doses and monitoring schedules from eTG.`

> [!warning] The Australian epidemiology
> **Australia has low TB incidence, and the majority of cases occur in people born overseas**, reflecting reactivation of latent infection acquired elsewhere.
> **Higher rates occur in some Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, particularly in northern Australia**, and there is a **cross-border consideration in the Torres Strait given multidrug-resistant TB in the region.**
> **Adherence is the determinant of cure and of resistance**, which is why supervised or supported therapy arrangements exist. **Multidrug-resistant TB requires specialist management with prolonged, more toxic regimens.**

**Ix:** **Three sputum specimens for AFB smear, culture and nucleic acid amplification** (*why:* smear indicates infectiousness, culture confirms and provides sensitivities, and NAAT gives a rapid answer including rifampicin resistance; *what:* AFB, *M. tuberculosis* DNA, resistance markers). **Chest radiograph** (*why:* screening and disease assessment, though it may be atypical or normal in immunosuppression; *what:* upper zone infiltrate, cavitation, miliary pattern, effusion). **IGRA or tuberculin skin test** (*why:* detects infection but **does NOT distinguish latent from active disease** — a positive test in a symptomatic patient does not confirm active TB, and a negative test does not exclude it, particularly in the immunosuppressed and in miliary disease; *what:* evidence of infection). **HIV testing in every person diagnosed with TB** (*why:* coinfection is common, changes management, and both are treatable; *what:* positive serology). Tissue biopsy with histology and mycobacterial culture in extrapulmonary disease (*why:* caseating granulomas plus culture; *what:* histology, organism). Baseline **LFT, UEC, FBC, uric acid, and visual acuity with colour vision** (*why:* the toxicity profile of the four first-line drugs; *what:* baseline for monitoring). CT chest and site-directed imaging (*why:* extent, cavitation, and extrapulmonary sites; *what:* the disease distribution). Lumbar puncture where CNS involvement is suspected (*why:* tuberculous meningitis has a characteristic CSF profile and is rapidly fatal untreated; *what:* lymphocytosis, very high protein, low glucose, AFB and PCR — cross-refer [[D1]] 0.5).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
**Airborne isolation. Notify. Do not delay isolation for confirmation.** Collect specimens before treatment.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Multidrug therapy with pyridoxine, adherence support, and specialist infectious diseases or respiratory management. Contact tracing by public health.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Completion of therapy is the priority — **partial treatment generates resistance.** Monitoring for toxicity, and **treatment of latent infection in identified contacts.**

## 0.4 HIV and Opportunistic Infection

> [!danger] Offer HIV testing far more widely than feels necessary
> **HIV is a treatable chronic condition with a normal life expectancy on treatment — and it remains diagnosed late in a substantial proportion of Australian cases**, at which point opportunistic infection has already occurred.
> **Late diagnosis happens because testing is reserved for people who "look at risk".** Test on clinical indication — unexplained fever, weight loss, lymphadenopathy, recurrent infection, unusual infection, thrombocytopenia, seborrhoeic dermatitis, shingles in a younger person, tuberculosis, any sexually transmitted infection, and any FUO.
> **Consent is required but the process should be normalised**, not treated as an exceptional event.

> [!tip] Acute seroconversion illness — commonly missed, and highly infectious
> **Fever, rash, pharyngitis, generalised lymphadenopathy, mouth or genital ulcers, myalgia, headache, and sometimes aseptic meningitis**, typically two to four weeks after exposure. It resembles glandular fever or a viral illness and is dismissed as such.
> **Viral load is extremely high during seroconversion, so transmission risk is at its peak** — recognising it matters for the patient and for public health.
> **The antibody test may be negative during this window.** **A fourth-generation antigen/antibody assay detects it earlier, and HIV RNA (viral load) detects it earliest** — so if acute HIV is suspected, request the right test rather than accepting a negative antibody result. `UNVERIFIED — window periods for each assay.`

> [!info] Opportunistic infections stratify by CD4 count
> **The CD4 count predicts which infections are possible, which is the practical value of knowing it.**
> **· Higher CD4** — tuberculosis, bacterial pneumonia, herpes zoster, oral candidiasis, seborrhoeic dermatitis — all can occur at any count.
> **· Below roughly 200** — ***Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia**, oesophageal candidiasis.
> **· Below roughly 100** — **cerebral toxoplasmosis, cryptococcal meningitis.**
> **· Below roughly 50** — **CMV retinitis, disseminated *Mycobacterium avium* complex.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the CD4 thresholds and the prophylaxis regimens and their start and stop points.`

> [!tip] The four to recognise
> **· *Pneumocystis* pneumonia (PJP)** — **subacute dry cough and progressive exertional dyspnoea over weeks, with EXERTIONAL DESATURATION and a chest radiograph that is often normal or shows only bilateral perihilar interstitial change despite marked hypoxia.** Raised LDH. **The discordance between how hypoxic they are and how unremarkable the film looks is the clue.** **Corticosteroids are added to treatment when hypoxia is significant, and they improve survival** — this is a specific and examinable point.
> **· Cerebral toxoplasmosis** — headache, focal deficit, seizures, with **multiple ring-enhancing lesions** on imaging. The main differential is primary CNS lymphoma.
> **· Cryptococcal meningitis** — subacute headache and fever with **markedly raised CSF opening pressure**, and **repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures to control pressure are a key part of management** — pressure control affects survival as much as the antifungal does.
> **· CMV retinitis** — **floaters, visual field loss or blurring, with a characteristic "pizza pie" retinal appearance.** **It is sight-threatening and requires urgent ophthalmology**, and it is missed because visual symptoms are not asked about.

> [!warning] Two points that shape modern management
> **· Antiretroviral therapy is started immediately at diagnosis, regardless of CD4 count** — the older practice of deferring until a threshold was reached is obsolete.
> **· "Undetectable equals untransmittable" (U=U)** — a person on effective treatment with a sustained undetectable viral load **cannot sexually transmit HIV.** This is well established, it is transformative for patients' relationships and self-perception, and **it should be communicated explicitly**, because many people diagnosed today still carry the assumptions of the 1990s.
> **Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)** — as immunity recovers on treatment, a subclinical infection can become intensely inflamed and appear to worsen. **It is not treatment failure**, and the management differs.

**Ix:** **Fourth-generation HIV antigen/antibody assay, with HIV RNA where acute infection is suspected** (*why:* the antibody component may be negative in the window; *what:* positive result). **CD4 count and viral load** (*why:* stratifies opportunistic infection risk, determines prophylaxis, and provides the treatment baseline; *what:* CD4, viral load). Baseline screening — **hepatitis B and C, syphilis, latent TB, toxoplasma serology, STI screen, cervical screening, and vaccination status** (*why:* coinfection is common and several require treatment or prophylaxis before or alongside ART; *what:* coinfections). **Chest radiograph with arterial or venous gas and exercise oximetry where PJP is suspected** (*why:* exertional desaturation with an unimpressive film is the clue, and resting saturation may be normal; *what:* desaturation on exertion, interstitial change, raised LDH). Induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage for PJP (*why:* diagnostic confirmation; *what:* organism on PCR or stain). CT brain with contrast for focal neurology (*why:* toxoplasmosis versus lymphoma; *what:* ring-enhancing lesions). **Lumbar puncture with OPENING PRESSURE, India ink, cryptococcal antigen and culture** (*why:* opening pressure is both diagnostic and therapeutic in cryptococcal disease and is frequently not measured; *what:* raised pressure, cryptococcal antigen). **Dilated fundoscopy by ophthalmology at low CD4 counts** (*why:* CMV retinitis is sight-threatening and asymptomatic early; *what:* retinitis).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat the opportunistic infection. **Steroids with PJP treatment where hypoxic.** Therapeutic lumbar punctures for raised pressure in cryptococcal meningitis. Urgent ophthalmology for visual symptoms.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
**Start antiretroviral therapy** with specialist involvement, with attention to the timing relative to treating an opportunistic infection because of IRIS risk. Prophylaxis by CD4 count.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Adherence support, monitoring, vaccination, cardiovascular and metabolic risk management (**people with HIV now die of the same things as everyone else**), cancer screening, **partner notification and contact tracing**, and mental health support. **Notification is required.**

## 0.5 Immunodeficiency and Screening Before Immunosuppression

> [!tip] When to suspect a primary immunodeficiency
> The classic pattern is infections that are **too frequent, too severe, too persistent, too unusual in organism or site, or too poorly responsive to appropriate treatment** — plus **failure to thrive in children** and **a family history.**
> **Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is the commonest clinically significant primary immunodeficiency presenting in adults**, and it is **diagnosed after an average delay of many years.** It presents with **recurrent sinopulmonary infections leading to bronchiectasis**, and — counterintuitively — with **autoimmune disease (particularly immune cytopenias), granulomatous disease, gastrointestinal disease and an increased malignancy risk.**
> **An adult with recurrent pneumonia or sinusitis and bronchiectasis should have immunoglobulins checked** — it is a simple test and the diagnosis is transformative, because immunoglobulin replacement prevents the progressive lung damage.

> [!tip] The categories, and the infections each predisposes to
> **· Antibody (B cell) deficiency** — encapsulated bacteria, recurrent sinopulmonary infection, enteroviruses, giardia.
> **· T cell / combined** — opportunistic infections, fungi, viruses, mycobacteria.
> **· Phagocyte defects** — staphylococcal and fungal infections, abscesses, poor wound healing.
> **· Complement deficiency** — **terminal complement deficiency causes recurrent NEISSERIAL infection**, so **recurrent or unusual meningococcal disease should prompt complement testing**, and these patients need vaccination and prophylaxis. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.9.
> **· Asplenia and hyposplenism** — encapsulated organisms and overwhelming post-splenectomy infection. Cross-refer [[C1]] 0.5.

> [!danger] Screening before immunosuppression — the practical intern-level checklist
> Before starting a biologic, prolonged corticosteroids, chemotherapy or transplant immunosuppression:
> **· Latent tuberculosis** — mandatory before TNF inhibitors. See 0.3.
> **· Hepatitis B — surface antigen AND core antibody.** ***Rituximab and other B-cell depleting agents cause hepatitis B reactivation, which can be fulminant and fatal, INCLUDING in people who are surface antigen negative but core antibody positive (resolved infection).*** **Checking only the surface antigen misses them.** Prophylactic antiviral therapy is given.
> **· Hepatitis C and HIV.**
> **· Varicella zoster immunity** — because primary varicella in an immunosuppressed adult is severe, and **live vaccines must be given BEFORE immunosuppression, not after.**
> **· *Strongyloides* serology in anyone from an endemic area** — **including northern Australia and many migrant populations. Corticosteroids can convert chronic asymptomatic strongyloidiasis into HYPERINFECTION SYNDROME with disseminated larvae and Gram-negative sepsis, which has very high mortality.** **This is a genuinely Australian consideration given migration patterns and endemic areas in the north, and it is missed.**
> **· Vaccination status brought up to date, with live vaccines given beforehand.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the full screening panel, the prophylaxis regimens, and the intervals required between live vaccination and starting immunosuppression.`

> [!warning] Live vaccines are contraindicated in significant immunosuppression
> MMR, varicella, zoster (live formulation), yellow fever, BCG, oral typhoid and rotavirus.
> **Plan vaccination before immunosuppression begins**, and remember household contacts can generally be vaccinated safely, which protects the patient indirectly. Cross-refer [[GER3]].

**Ix:** **Immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM) and, where indicated, IgG subclasses and specific antibody responses to vaccination** (*why:* the screening test for antibody deficiency, and the response to a test immunisation is what demonstrates functional deficiency; *what:* low immunoglobulins, poor vaccine response). FBC with differential and lymphocyte subsets (*why:* lymphopenia and specific subset deficiencies; *what:* cell counts). **Complement — CH50 and AH50** where recurrent neisserial infection (*why:* screens the classical and alternative pathways; *what:* absent haemolytic activity). **HIV testing** (*why:* the commonest secondary immunodeficiency and it must be excluded before labelling anything primary; *what:* positive). **Pre-immunosuppression screening panel as above** (*why:* prevents reactivation of TB, hepatitis B and strongyloides, each of which can be fatal; *what:* latent infection). Specialist immunology referral (*why:* primary immunodeficiency diagnosis and management, including immunoglobulin replacement, is specialist; *what:* definitive characterisation).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat infection. **Do not delay antibiotics in an asplenic or neutropenic febrile patient** — cross-refer [[K1]] 0.5.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Immunoglobulin replacement in antibody deficiency, prophylaxis, and treatment of the underlying cause of secondary immunodeficiency.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis where indicated, a medical alert and patient education about presenting early with fever**, and surveillance for the autoimmune and malignant complications of primary immunodeficiency.

## 0.6 Vector-Borne and Zoonotic Disease in Australia

> [!tip] Mosquito-borne
> **· Ross River virus and Barmah Forest virus** — the commonest arboviral infections in Australia. **Fever, rash and prominent polyarthralgia**, which can persist for months and is frequently misdiagnosed as a new inflammatory arthritis. Diagnosis is serological.
> **· Murray Valley encephalitis** — inland riverine areas, particularly after flooding; severe encephalitis with high morbidity.
> **· Japanese encephalitis virus** — **emerged in mainland southeastern Australia in 2022**, with a vaccine available for at-risk groups. Consider in encephalitis with relevant exposure. `UNVERIFIED — the current distribution, at-risk groups and vaccination recommendations, which are actively evolving.`
> **· Dengue** — occurs in **outbreaks in far north Queensland** from imported cases plus local *Aedes*, as well as in returning travellers. Cross-refer [[K1]] 0.4.
> **· Malaria** — imported; Australia is malaria-free but the vector exists in the far north.

> [!warning] Ticks — and three distinctly Australian problems
> **1. Tick paralysis** — the **Australian paralysis tick (*Ixodes holocyclus*)** on the eastern seaboard injects a neurotoxin causing **ascending flaccid paralysis, particularly in small children and pets.** **The treatment is finding and removing the tick**, and the diagnosis is missed if nobody searches the scalp and behind the ears.
> **2. Tick-induced anaphylaxis and mammalian meat allergy** — **tick bites can sensitise to alpha-gal, producing DELAYED anaphylaxis three to six hours after eating red meat.** This is genuinely Australian in its recognition, increasingly reported, and the delayed timing means the connection to food is frequently not made. **Ask about tick exposure in unexplained anaphylaxis.**
> **3. Tick removal technique** — **do not squeeze the tick body**, which injects more saliva, toxin and allergen. Current Australian advice for *Ixodes holocyclus* is to **kill the tick in situ** with an appropriate agent and allow it to drop off, rather than grasping and pulling — **which differs from the advice given in the northern hemisphere for Lyme-vector ticks.** `UNVERIFIED — the current recommended removal technique and agents from ASCIA and state health guidance.`
> **· Rickettsial infections do occur in Australia** — **Queensland tick typhus and Flinders Island spotted fever** — with fever, rash and an **eschar.** Cross-refer [[K1]] 0.4.
> **· Lyme disease** — **there is no established evidence of locally acquired Lyme borreliosis in Australia**, and this is a contested and emotive area. Imported Lyme disease in returned travellers is real and treatable. `UNVERIFIED — this remains a debated area and the official position should be checked; handle the conversation with patients carefully and without dismissiveness.`

> [!tip] Zoonoses worth knowing, with their exposures
> **· Q fever (*Coxiella burnetii*)** — **abattoir workers, farmers, veterinarians, shearers, and those exposed to livestock birth products or contaminated dust.** Fever, hepatitis, pneumonia; can cause chronic infection with **endocarditis**. **A vaccine exists and is recommended for at-risk occupations, with pre-vaccination screening required** — this is a preventive point worth knowing and a genuinely Australian occupational health issue.
> **· Leptospirosis** — freshwater exposure, flooding, rodents, farm and abattoir work.
> **· Brucellosis** — feral pig hunters in Queensland, and unpasteurised dairy overseas.
> **· Hendra virus** — **horses and those in contact with them, notably veterinarians**, in Queensland and northern New South Wales. Rare, severe, with an equine vaccine that reduces risk.
> **· Psittacosis** — birds, including exposure to wild bird droppings; presents as atypical pneumonia.
> **· Australian bat lyssavirus** — any bat contact. Cross-refer [[K2]] 0.5.
> **· Melioidosis** — northern Australia, wet season, soil and water exposure. Cross-refer [[K1]] 0.4.
> **· Hydatid disease** — dogs and sheep, in rural Australia.

> [!danger] The exposure history is the diagnosis
> **None of these will be diagnosed unless asked about.** In an unexplained febrile or systemic illness, ask specifically about: **occupation, animals (domestic, livestock, wild, bats, birds), soil and water exposure, recreational activities, insect and tick bites, travel including within Australia, and unpasteurised food.**
> **"Have you been anywhere?" is not a travel history. "What do you do, where do you live, and what have you been in contact with?" is.**

Blood cultures (*why:* bacteraemic zoonoses including melioidosis and brucellosis, with **brucella requiring prolonged incubation and a warning to the laboratory because it is a laboratory hazard**; *what:* organism). FBC with film (*why:* thrombocytopenia and atypical lymphocytes; *what:* the pattern). LFT (*why:* hepatitis in Q fever, leptospirosis and arboviral infection; *what:* transaminitis). **Full body examination for an eschar or an attached tick, including the scalp, behind the ears, axillae and groin** (*why:* both make the diagnosis and, in tick paralysis, removal is the treatment; *what:* eschar, attached tick). Echocardiography where chronic Q fever is suspected (*why:* Q fever endocarditis; *what:* vegetation). **Notification to public health** (*why:* many are notifiable and drive outbreak investigation; *what:* the notifiable list).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
**Empirical doxycycline where rickettsial infection or Q fever is suspected**, since serology is retrospective. **Find and remove the tick** in suspected tick paralysis. Isolate and consult where a high-consequence pathogen is possible.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Organism-specific therapy with infectious diseases involvement, and prolonged therapy for chronic Q fever, brucellosis and melioidosis.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Occupational vaccination — Q fever vaccine for at-risk occupations, and Japanese encephalitis vaccine where recommended.** Bite and exposure avoidance advice. Notification and, where relevant, workplace assessment. Cross-refer [[GER3]].
