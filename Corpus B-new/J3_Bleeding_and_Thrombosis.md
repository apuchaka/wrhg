## 0.1 Interpreting the Coagulation Screen

> [!tip] What each test measures, and what it is sensitive to
> **· PT / INR — the EXTRINSIC and COMMON pathways (VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen).** **Most sensitive to WARFARIN, liver disease and vitamin K deficiency** — because factor VII has the shortest half-life and falls first.
> **· APTT — the INTRINSIC and COMMON pathways (XII, XI, IX, VIII, plus the common factors).** **Most sensitive to HEPARIN, haemophilia, von Willebrand disease and the LUPUS ANTICOAGULANT.**

> [!info] The patterns
> | Pattern | Think |
> |---|---|
> | **Isolated prolonged PT/INR** | Warfarin · EARLY liver disease · early vitamin K deficiency · factor VII deficiency |
> | **Isolated prolonged APTT** | Heparin · **HAEMOPHILIA A or B** · von Willebrand disease · factor XI or XII deficiency · **LUPUS ANTICOAGULANT** |
> | **BOTH prolonged** | Liver disease · **DIC** · vitamin K deficiency · massive transfusion and dilution · warfarin excess · common pathway deficiency |
> | **BOTH NORMAL but the patient bleeds** | **PLATELET disorder (number or function)** · **VON WILLEBRAND DISEASE** · **FACTOR XIII deficiency** · vascular and connective tissue disorders · antiplatelet drugs · mild factor deficiency |

> [!danger] A normal coagulation screen does not exclude a bleeding disorder
> **The PT and APTT test only the plasma clotting factors. They say NOTHING about platelet FUNCTION, about von Willebrand factor (which may give a normal APTT if factor VIII is preserved), or about factor XIII (which is not measured by either test and causes DELAYED bleeding and umbilical stump bleeding).**
> **THE BLEEDING HISTORY IS MORE INFORMATIVE THAN THE SCREEN.**
> **Ask about: epistaxis requiring intervention · bleeding after DENTAL EXTRACTION · bleeding after previous surgery or childbirth · POSTPARTUM HAEMORRHAGE · HEAVY MENSTRUAL BLEEDING since menarche · easy bruising without trauma · joint or muscle bleeds · and a FAMILY history.**
> **Structured bleeding assessment tools exist and outperform the coagulation screen for predicting a genuine disorder.**

> [!warning] Two interpretive points that come up constantly
> **1. THE MIXING STUDY.** Mix the patient's plasma with normal plasma and repeat the test.
> **· CORRECTS → a FACTOR DEFICIENCY (the normal plasma supplied what was missing).**
> **· DOES NOT CORRECT → an INHIBITOR — a lupus anticoagulant, or an acquired factor inhibitor.**
> **2. THE LUPUS ANTICOAGULANT PARADOX.** **It PROLONGS the APTT in vitro but causes THROMBOSIS in vivo, not bleeding.** **It is named for what it does in the test tube, not to the patient.** **An unexpectedly prolonged APTT in a patient with thrombosis, recurrent miscarriage, or an autoimmune disease should prompt antiphospholipid testing** — see 0.6.

> [!danger] The coagulation screen does not monitor DOACs
> **Direct oral anticoagulants may produce a NORMAL PT and APTT at therapeutic levels, and the degree of prolongation correlates poorly with drug effect.**
> **A NORMAL COAGULATION SCREEN DOES NOT MEAN A PATIENT IS NOT ANTICOAGULATED.**
> **This matters acutely — in bleeding, in trauma, and before urgent surgery or a lumbar puncture — where the relevant question is which drug, what dose, when was the last one, and what is the renal function.**
> **Specific assays exist (anti-Xa activity calibrated for the agent, dilute thrombin time for dabigatran) but are not universally or rapidly available.** **The history is the practical test.** Cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.4 and [[AN1_Perioperative_Care]] 0.3.

## 0.2 Inherited Bleeding Disorders

> [!warning] Von Willebrand disease — the commonest, and the pattern is mucocutaneous
> **Von Willebrand factor does two things: it mediates PLATELET ADHESION to damaged endothelium, and it is the CARRIER PROTEIN FOR FACTOR VIII** — which is why severe deficiency also lowers factor VIII and prolongs the APTT.
> **THE BLEEDING PATTERN IS MUCOCUTANEOUS: epistaxis · easy bruising · gum bleeding · HEAVY MENSTRUAL BLEEDING SINCE MENARCHE · bleeding after dental extraction and surgery · and POSTPARTUM HAEMORRHAGE.**
> **This contrasts with haemophilia, where bleeding is DEEP — into joints and muscles.** **Mucocutaneous versus deep is the discriminating pattern.**
> **Types: 1 (partial quantitative deficiency — the commonest and mildest) · 2 (qualitative defects, with subtypes) · 3 (near-complete deficiency, severe).**

> [!danger] A single normal von Willebrand level does not exclude the diagnosis
> **vWF levels are highly variable and RISE with: stress, exercise, acute illness and INFLAMMATION, PREGNANCY, and OESTROGEN (including the combined pill).**
> **And they vary with BLOOD GROUP — people with blood group O have physiologically LOWER vWF levels**, which affects interpretation of the reference range.
> **So testing a stressed, inflamed, pregnant or oestrogen-exposed patient can produce a falsely normal result.** **REPEAT TESTING on a separate occasion is often required, and a strong bleeding history should not be dismissed on one normal set.**
> **Treatment: DESMOPRESSIN — which releases stored vWF from endothelium and works in TYPE 1, does NOT work in TYPE 3 (there is nothing to release), and can WORSEN TYPE 2B (by causing platelet clumping and thrombocytopenia).** **A trial of desmopressin establishes whether an individual responds.**
> **Also: von Willebrand factor concentrate · and TRANEXAMIC ACID, which is cheap, effective and under-used for mucosal bleeding and heavy menstrual bleeding.** Cross-refer [[O4_Abnormal_and_Menstrual_Bleeding]] 0.2.

> [!tip] Haemophilia A and B
> **X-linked deficiency of FACTOR VIII (haemophilia A) or FACTOR IX (haemophilia B), with severity graded by residual factor level.**
> **The bleeding pattern is DEEP: HAEMARTHROSIS (recurrent joint bleeding leading to chronic arthropathy) and MUSCLE bleeds — including iliopsoas bleeds, which present as hip flexion, groin pain and a femoral nerve palsy and can cause substantial concealed blood loss.**
> **Practical rules for the ward:**
> **· TREAT FIRST, INVESTIGATE SECOND. "When in doubt, treat" — factor replacement is given at the first suspicion of a bleed, not after imaging confirms it.**
> **· HEAD INJURY IN A HAEMOPHILIC PATIENT: GIVE FACTOR BEFORE THE CT SCAN.** Intracranial haemorrhage is the leading cause of death, and waiting for imaging wastes the window.
> **· NO INTRAMUSCULAR INJECTIONS.** **This includes vaccinations, which should be given subcutaneously or with factor cover.**
> **· AVOID ASPIRIN AND NSAIDs.**
> **· Involve the patient's HAEMOPHILIA CENTRE early — they know the patient's regimen, inhibitor status and product.** **These patients know their disease better than you do.**
> **INHIBITORS — alloantibodies against the replaced factor — are the major treatment complication, rendering standard replacement ineffective and requiring bypassing agents.**
> **Female carriers can be symptomatic**, with low factor levels causing bleeding, and are frequently overlooked.

> [!info] The rarer inherited causes
> **· FACTOR XIII DEFICIENCY — a NORMAL PT AND APTT, with DELAYED bleeding after initial haemostasis, poor wound healing, and classically UMBILICAL STUMP BLEEDING in a neonate.** **It is missed because the screen is normal.**
> **· Other rare factor deficiencies, and inherited platelet function disorders (Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard-Soulier syndrome).**
> **· CONNECTIVE TISSUE AND VASCULAR: Ehlers-Danlos syndrome · and HEREDITARY HAEMORRHAGIC TELANGIECTASIA (Osler-Weber-Rendu) — with recurrent epistaxis, mucocutaneous telangiectasia, iron deficiency, and ARTERIOVENOUS MALFORMATIONS in lung, liver and brain, which have their own consequences including paradoxical embolism and cerebral abscess.**

## 0.3 Acquired Bleeding Disorders

> [!danger] The INR does not predict bleeding risk in liver disease
> **Liver disease reduces BOTH procoagulant factors AND the natural anticoagulants (protein C, protein S, antithrombin) — producing a "REBALANCED" but fragile haemostatic state.**
> **THEREFORE: A RAISED INR IN CIRRHOSIS IS NOT A RELIABLE MARKER OF BLEEDING RISK, and these patients ARE STILL AT RISK OF THROMBOSIS — including portal vein thrombosis and venous thromboembolism.**
> **Two practical consequences that are commonly got wrong:**
> **1. Do NOT transfuse plasma to "correct" the INR before a procedure in a cirrhotic patient purely on the basis of the number — it is largely ineffective, causes volume overload and raises portal pressure.**
> **2. Do NOT withhold VTE prophylaxis in a cirrhotic patient simply because the INR is raised.** Cross-refer [[C3_Jaundice_and_Liver_Disease]] 0.5.

> [!warning] The acquired causes
> **· VITAMIN K DEFICIENCY — malabsorption, biliary obstruction (no bile, no fat-soluble vitamin absorption), prolonged antibiotics, malnutrition, and the newborn.** Cross-refer [[M3_Neonatal_Problems]] 0.3. **Corrected with vitamin K, which takes hours — so acute reversal needs factor concentrate.**
> **· DIC — cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.3.**
> **· RENAL FAILURE — uraemia causes PLATELET DYSFUNCTION with a normal platelet count and normal clotting screen. DESMOPRESSIN is the specific short-term treatment**, alongside dialysis and correcting anaemia.
> **· DRUGS — antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and the contributors people forget: SSRIs (which impair platelet serotonin uptake, particularly combined with NSAIDs or anticoagulants) and NSAIDs.** Cross-refer [[N4_Mood_Disorders]] 0.3.
> **· MASSIVE TRANSFUSION and dilutional coagulopathy.**

> [!danger] Two acquired disorders worth recognising by pattern
> **1. ACQUIRED HAEMOPHILIA — an autoantibody against factor VIII.**
> **The pattern: NEW, SEVERE, often SPONTANEOUS bleeding — typically extensive bruising, muscle and soft tissue bleeds rather than haemarthroses — in an OLDER person with NO prior bleeding history, with an ISOLATED PROLONGED APTT THAT DOES NOT CORRECT ON MIXING.**
> **Associated with malignancy, autoimmune disease, pregnancy and the postpartum period, and drugs — but often idiopathic.**
> **It carries significant mortality and requires urgent haematology involvement: bypassing agents for the bleeding, and immunosuppression to eradicate the inhibitor.**
> **It is missed because a new bleeding disorder in an elderly patient is not expected.**
> **2. ACQUIRED VON WILLEBRAND SYNDROME — and HEYDE SYNDROME specifically.**
> **HEYDE SYNDROME: severe AORTIC STENOSIS + gastrointestinal bleeding from ANGIODYSPLASIA + ACQUIRED von Willebrand disease.** **The mechanism: high shear stress across the stenotic valve unfolds and degrades the large vWF multimers, impairing platelet adhesion at exactly the sites (angiodysplastic lesions) most likely to bleed.**
> **The point that makes it examinable and clinically important: IT RESOLVES WITH AORTIC VALVE REPLACEMENT.** **Recurrent obscure GI bleeding in a patient with aortic stenosis should prompt this thought rather than endless endoscopy.** Cross-refer [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.2 and [[C4_Gastrointestinal_Bleeding]] 0.3.
> **Acquired vWD also occurs with myeloproliferative neoplasms, monoclonal gammopathy, and ventricular assist devices.**

## 0.4 Venous Thromboembolism — Diagnosis

> [!danger] Clinical probability FIRST, then the test — the order is not optional
> **The D-dimer is only useful in patients with LOW or INTERMEDIATE clinical probability, where a NEGATIVE result RULES OUT venous thromboembolism.**
> **IN HIGH PROBABILITY, THE D-DIMER IS USELESS — a negative result does not exclude the diagnosis and a positive result adds nothing. GO STRAIGHT TO IMAGING.**
> **Sending a D-dimer first, and then being reassured by a negative result in a patient with a swollen leg and a recent long-haul flight, is a recognised diagnostic error.**
> **D-dimer is raised by: infection · MALIGNANCY · PREGNANCY · recent surgery or trauma · inflammation of any kind · liver disease · and INCREASING AGE — which is why AGE-ADJUSTED D-dimer thresholds improve specificity in older patients without losing sensitivity.** `UNVERIFIED — the Wells scores, PERC rule, and age-adjusted D-dimer formula.`

> [!warning] Investigating DVT and PE
> **DVT: COMPRESSION ULTRASOUND.** **A negative scan in a patient with high clinical suspicion warrants a REPEAT SCAN after a few days**, because a distal thrombus may propagate and become detectable.
> **PE: CT PULMONARY ANGIOGRAPHY is the standard.**
> **V/Q or V/Q SPECT where CTPA is contraindicated — contrast allergy, significant renal impairment, or PREGNANCY (where the radiation dose to breast tissue from CTPA is a consideration and the choice should be discussed with the woman).** Cross-refer [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]].
> **The supporting tests, and what they actually contribute:**
> **· ECG — SINUS TACHYCARDIA is the commonest finding. The classic S1Q3T3 is UNCOMMON and its absence means nothing. Right heart strain (right axis deviation, RBBB, T inversion in V1–V4) suggests a larger clot burden.**
> **· CHEST RADIOGRAPH — usually normal or non-specific; its role is excluding alternatives.**
> **· ABG — non-specific, and a NORMAL A-a gradient does NOT exclude PE.**
> **· TROPONIN and BNP — prognostic rather than diagnostic; elevation indicates right ventricular strain and worse outcome.**
> **· ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY — for right ventricular strain in the unstable patient, where it may support immediate thrombolysis without CT.** Cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.4.

> [!danger] Do not wait for imaging to anticoagulate
> **In a patient with HIGH clinical probability of VTE and no contraindication to anticoagulation, START TREATMENT while awaiting imaging.**
> **Delays occur routinely — the scan is overnight, the radiologist is busy, the patient is nil by mouth — and untreated pulmonary embolism kills during those hours.**
> **The corollary: massive PE with haemodynamic instability is a resuscitation problem requiring immediate senior involvement and consideration of THROMBOLYSIS, not a diagnostic problem.** Cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.4.

## 0.5 Management and Anticoagulant Choice

> [!tip] DOACs are first-line for most — and the exceptions are what get tested
> **Direct oral anticoagulants are the default for most venous thromboembolism and for non-valvular atrial fibrillation: no monitoring, fewer interactions, at least equivalent efficacy, and less intracranial bleeding.**
> **THE SITUATIONS WHERE A DOAC IS NOT APPROPRIATE:**
> **· MECHANICAL HEART VALVES — DOACs are CONTRAINDICATED; warfarin is required. This was demonstrated in a trial stopped early for harm.**
> **· MODERATE TO SEVERE MITRAL STENOSIS.**
> **· ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME, particularly triple-antibody-positive or arterial thrombosis — warfarin is preferred, as DOACs performed worse.**
> **· SEVERE RENAL IMPAIRMENT — dose reduction or avoidance depending on the agent and the creatinine clearance.**
> **· PREGNANCY AND BREASTFEEDING — LMWH is used; DOACs and warfarin are not.**
> **· Extremes of body weight, significant drug interactions (enzyme inducers), and adherence concerns where monitored therapy may be safer.**
> `UNVERIFIED — agents, doses, renal thresholds and PBS criteria.`

> [!warning] Warfarin practicalities
> **· It takes DAYS to work, and it is transiently PROCOAGULANT at initiation because protein C (short half-life) falls before the procoagulant factors — hence the need for heparin cover at the start, and the risk of warfarin-induced skin necrosis.**
> **· INTERACTIONS are numerous — antibiotics (particularly metronidazole, macrolides and co-trimoxazole), amiodarone, antifungals, and enzyme inducers.** **Check the interaction whenever prescribing anything to a warfarinised patient.**
> **· DIET: the message is CONSISTENCY, not avoidance.** **Patients are frequently told to avoid green vegetables entirely, which is wrong and unhelpful — a steady intake is what matters.**
> **· Reversal — cross-refer [[A9_Transfusion__Coagulopathy_and_Anticoagulant_Emergencies]] 0.4.**

> [!tip] Duration of anticoagulation — the framework
> **The question is whether the thrombosis was PROVOKED, and by what:**
> **· Provoked by a MAJOR TRANSIENT risk factor (surgery, trauma, immobilisation, pregnancy) → a defined SHORTER course, because the risk factor has resolved and recurrence risk is low.**
> **· UNPROVOKED → a LONGER or EXTENDED course, because recurrence risk after stopping is substantial — weighed against bleeding risk and patient preference, and reviewed periodically rather than decided once.**
> **· CANCER-ASSOCIATED → anticoagulation while the cancer is active or being treated.**
> **· RECURRENT unprovoked VTE → indefinite.**
> **· Isolated DISTAL (calf) DVT → may be managed with surveillance ultrasound rather than anticoagulation in selected low-risk patients.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the specific durations.`
> **Reduced-dose extended anticoagulation is an option for extended prevention** in some patients.

> [!warning] Three management points that are commonly got wrong
> **1. AFTER AN UNPROVOKED VTE, DO ONLY AGE-APPROPRIATE CANCER SCREENING.** **Extensive occult malignancy screening — whole-body CT and tumour markers — does NOT improve outcomes and generates incidental findings, anxiety and cost.** **A thorough history and examination, basic bloods, and ensuring the patient is up to date with bowel, breast and cervical screening is what is indicated.**
> **2. GRADUATED COMPRESSION STOCKINGS ARE NO LONGER ROUTINELY RECOMMENDED for preventing post-thrombotic syndrome** after DVT, following trials that failed to show benefit — though they may help symptomatically. `UNVERIFIED — current guidance.`
> **3. IVC FILTERS have NARROW indications** — essentially an ABSOLUTE contraindication to anticoagulation in the presence of acute VTE. **They are over-used, they carry their own thrombotic and mechanical complications, and RETRIEVABLE FILTERS ARE FREQUENTLY NEVER RETRIEVED.** **If one is inserted, the retrieval plan must be documented and followed up.**

> [!danger] Two long-term complications worth anticipating
> **· POST-THROMBOTIC SYNDROME — chronic leg pain, swelling, heaviness, skin changes and ulceration after DVT, affecting a substantial proportion of patients.** Cross-refer [[B6_Oedema__Fatigue__Weakness_and_Undifferentiated_Presentations]] 0.4.
> **· CHRONIC THROMBOEMBOLIC PULMONARY HYPERTENSION — persistent or progressive BREATHLESSNESS months after a pulmonary embolism.** **It is treatable, potentially curable with pulmonary endarterectomy, and REPEATEDLY MISSED because post-PE breathlessness is attributed to deconditioning or anxiety.**
> **A patient who remains breathless three to six months after a PE needs assessment — echocardiography and specialist referral — not reassurance.**

## 0.6 Thrombophilia and Special Situations

> [!danger] Mostly, do not test for thrombophilia
> **Thrombophilia testing is over-requested and RARELY CHANGES MANAGEMENT.**
> **The reasons:**
> **· The decision about anticoagulation duration is driven by whether the event was PROVOKED and by bleeding risk — not by a heritable thrombophilia.**
> **· A positive result causes lasting anxiety, insurance and employment implications, and unnecessary testing of relatives.**
> **· Results are UNRELIABLE at the time they are usually taken — see the timing box below.**
> **Testing may be justified where it will genuinely change management: suspected ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME · thrombosis at an unusual site · a strong family history in specific contexts · and some pregnancy-related decisions — ideally after specialist discussion rather than as a reflex panel.**

> [!warning] If you do test, the timing determines whether the result means anything
> **· NOT DURING THE ACUTE THROMBOSIS — antithrombin, protein C and protein S are consumed and falsely low.**
> **· NOT ON WARFARIN — it lowers protein C and protein S (both vitamin K dependent), producing false positives.**
> **· NOT ON A DOAC — DOACs INTERFERE WITH LUPUS ANTICOAGULANT assays, producing false positives.**
> **· NOT DURING PREGNANCY or on oestrogen — protein S falls physiologically.**
> **The practical result: the time to test is weeks after stopping anticoagulation, well away from the acute event — which is another reason the test rarely changes acute management.**

> [!danger] Antiphospholipid syndrome
> **Requires BOTH a clinical criterion AND persistent antibodies.**
> **· CLINICAL: vascular thrombosis (venous, ARTERIAL — including stroke in a young person — or small vessel) OR pregnancy morbidity (recurrent early miscarriage, fetal death, or severe pre-eclampsia and placental insufficiency).**
> **· LABORATORY: LUPUS ANTICOAGULANT, anticardiolipin antibodies, or anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I antibodies — POSITIVE ON TWO OCCASIONS AT LEAST 12 WEEKS APART.** **A single positive result does not make the diagnosis; transient positives are common with infection.**
> **The clues: an unexplained prolonged APTT (that does not correct on mixing) · arterial thrombosis in a young person · thrombosis with thrombocytopenia · recurrent pregnancy loss · livedo reticularis · and coexisting SLE.**
> **Management: WARFARIN rather than a DOAC in triple-positive disease and arterial events · and in pregnancy, ASPIRIN PLUS LOW MOLECULAR WEIGHT HEPARIN, which substantially improves live birth rates.** Cross-refer [[L2_Polyarthralgia_and_Inflammatory_Arthritis]] 0.4 and [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]].
> **CATASTROPHIC antiphospholipid syndrome — multi-organ thrombosis over days, with high mortality, requiring urgent immunosuppression and anticoagulation.**

> [!warning] Thrombosis at an unusual site changes the workup
> **Splanchnic (portal, mesenteric, hepatic), cerebral venous sinus, or upper limb thrombosis without a catheter — these warrant a different differential:**
> **· MYELOPROLIFERATIVE NEOPLASM — check a JAK2 mutation, and note that the blood count may be NORMAL in splanchnic thrombosis because of hypersplenism masking the counts.**
> **· PAROXYSMAL NOCTURNAL HAEMOGLOBINURIA — cross-refer [[J2_Haemoglobinopathy_and_Haemolysis]] 0.4.**
> **· MALIGNANCY, local compression or invasion.**
> **· BEHÇET DISEASE, inflammatory bowel disease, and intra-abdominal sepsis or pancreatitis for splanchnic events.**
> **· Antiphospholipid syndrome.**
> **· In cerebral venous sinus thrombosis: dehydration, oral contraception, pregnancy and the puerperium, local infection, and — historically important — vaccine-induced immune thrombotic thrombocytopenia.** Cross-refer [[D1_Headache_and_Meningism]] 0.2.

> [!tip] The other situations
> **· CANCER-ASSOCIATED THROMBOSIS — higher recurrence AND higher bleeding risk.** **Anticoagulant choice considers the tumour: DOACs carry more gastrointestinal bleeding, so LMWH may be preferred with luminal GI or genitourinary tumours.** Cross-refer [[J5_Oncological_Emergencies_and_Palliative_Care]].
> **· PREGNANCY AND THE PUERPERIUM — a hypercoagulable state, with VTE a leading cause of maternal death.** **LMWH is the anticoagulant; warfarin is teratogenic and DOACs are not used.** Cross-refer [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]].
> **· SUPERFICIAL VENOUS THROMBOSIS — not always benign. Extensive thrombus, or thrombus close to the SAPHENOFEMORAL JUNCTION, can propagate into the deep system and warrants imaging and anticoagulation.**
> **· TRAVEL-RELATED VTE — the risk relates to duration and to underlying risk factors rather than to flying itself.**
> **· HOSPITAL VTE PROPHYLAXIS — every admitted patient should have a documented VTE risk assessment, with mechanical and pharmacological prophylaxis as indicated, and EXTENDED prophylaxis after major cancer and orthopaedic surgery.** **Hospital-acquired VTE is common, largely preventable, and a recognised quality measure.** Cross-refer [[AN1_Perioperative_Care]] 0.5.

**Ix:** **A structured BLEEDING HISTORY** (*why:* more informative than the coagulation screen, which is normal in platelet disorders, von Willebrand disease and factor XIII deficiency; *what:* the pattern and severity). **FBC, film, PT/INR, APTT, FIBRINOGEN** (*why:* the baseline, and the pattern narrows the differential; *what:* per the table above). **MIXING STUDY on a prolonged APTT** (*why:* distinguishes factor deficiency from an inhibitor, which is the entire question in acquired haemophilia and antiphospholipid syndrome; *what:* correction or not). **Von Willebrand screen — vWF antigen, vWF activity, factor VIII — REPEATED if normal with a strong history** (*why:* levels are labile and vary with stress, oestrogen, inflammation and blood group; *what:* deficiency or dysfunction). **Clinical probability score BEFORE ordering a D-dimer** (*why:* the D-dimer is only interpretable at low or intermediate probability; *what:* the pre-test probability). **Compression ultrasound or CTPA per the algorithm** (*why:* diagnosis; *what:* thrombus). **Renal function and weight before dosing any anticoagulant** (*why:* DOAC and LMWH dosing are renal- and weight-dependent, and this is a common prescribing error; *what:* creatinine clearance, weight). **Antiphospholipid antibodies, repeated at 12 weeks** (*why:* transient positives are common and a single result does not diagnose the syndrome; *what:* persistent positivity). **JAK2 mutation testing in splanchnic or unusual-site thrombosis** (*why:* an occult myeloproliferative neoplasm, which may have normal blood counts in this setting; *what:* the mutation). **Age-appropriate cancer screening after unprovoked VTE — and NOT an extensive occult malignancy search** (*why:* extensive screening does not improve outcomes; *what:* up-to-date routine screening).
