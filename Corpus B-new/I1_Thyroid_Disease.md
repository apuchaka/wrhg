## 0.1 Thyroid Function Tests — Interpretation

**A/P:** Hypothalamic TRH → pituitary **TSH** → thyroid **T4 (the main secreted hormone, largely a prohormone) and T3 (the active hormone)** → negative feedback on the pituitary and hypothalamus. Most T3 is produced peripherally by deiodination of T4.

> [!tip] Why TSH is the most sensitive test
> **The relationship between free T4 and TSH is log-linear: a small change in free T4 produces a large, amplified change in TSH.**
> **So TSH becomes abnormal before free T4 leaves the reference range** — which is why it is the screening test of choice in primary thyroid disease, and why **subclinical disease (abnormal TSH, normal free T4) is a real biochemical entity rather than an artefact.**

> [!info] The patterns
> | TSH | Free T4 | Interpretation |
> |---|---|---|
> | **High** | **Low** | **Primary hypothyroidism** |
> | **High** | Normal | **Subclinical hypothyroidism** |
> | **Low** | **High** (or high T3) | **Primary hyperthyroidism** |
> | **Low** | Normal | **Subclinical hyperthyroidism** |
> | **Low or normal** | **Low** | **CENTRAL (secondary/tertiary) hypothyroidism** — pituitary or hypothalamic |
> | **High** | **High** | TSH-secreting adenoma, thyroid hormone resistance, **assay interference**, or recent non-adherence with a catch-up dose |
> `UNVERIFIED — all reference ranges.`

> [!danger] TSH is unreliable in central hypothyroidism — and the trap that follows
> **In pituitary or hypothalamic disease the TSH may be low, normal or even mildly raised despite a genuinely low free T4**, because the TSH produced is biologically inactive. **Relying on TSH alone will miss it.**
> **A low free T4 with a non-elevated TSH is central hypothyroidism until proven otherwise, and it demands assessment of the OTHER pituitary axes** — particularly **cortisol.** Cross-refer [[I4]].
> **This matters because starting thyroxine in a patient with undiagnosed adrenal insufficiency can precipitate an adrenal crisis** — thyroid hormone increases cortisol clearance and metabolic demand. **Always exclude and, if present, treat adrenal insufficiency BEFORE starting thyroxine.** This is a high-yield safety point.

> [!danger] Do not check thyroid function in an acutely unwell inpatient without a specific indication
> **Non-thyroidal illness syndrome ("sick euthyroid")** occurs in any significant acute illness: **T3 falls first, then T4 falls, and TSH is variable — often low during the illness and REBOUNDING HIGH during recovery.**
> **The results are uninterpretable, they do not reflect thyroid disease, and treating them causes harm.** A raised TSH in a recovering inpatient is far more likely to be recovery from non-thyroidal illness than new hypothyroidism.
> **Test when thyroid disease is genuinely suspected, and repeat abnormal results after recovery** rather than acting on them.

> [!warning] Interference and drug effects that produce spurious results
> **· Biotin supplements — high-dose biotin interferes with many immunoassays and produces a pattern that can mimic Graves disease (low TSH, high free T4).** **Ask about supplements**, which patients do not consider "medications". Biotin is in many hair, skin and nail products.
> **· Heterophile antibodies and macro-TSH** — laboratory artefacts producing discordant results. If the biochemistry does not match the patient, discuss with the laboratory.
> **· Oestrogen (pregnancy, the pill, HRT) raises thyroxine-binding globulin → raised TOTAL T4 with a normal FREE T4.** This is why **free** hormone levels are measured.
> **· Drugs affecting the axis:** **amiodarone** (both hypo- and hyperthyroidism — see 0.6), **lithium** (hypothyroidism and goitre), **corticosteroids and dopamine** (suppress TSH), **immune checkpoint inhibitors** (thyroiditis — cross-refer [[A5]] 0.7), phenytoin and carbamazepine, and **tyrosine kinase inhibitors.**

**Ix:** **TSH as the first-line test in a stable outpatient** (*why:* the most sensitive marker of primary thyroid dysfunction; *what:* the pattern above). **Free T4, and free T3 where hyperthyroidism is suspected** (*why:* free T4 confirms the degree of dysfunction, and **T3 toxicosis exists in which T3 is raised with a normal free T4 — so a suppressed TSH with a normal free T4 warrants a T3**; *what:* free hormone levels). **Thyroid autoantibodies** (*why:* **anti-TPO supports Hashimoto thyroiditis and predicts progression from subclinical to overt hypothyroidism; TSH receptor antibodies (TRAb) confirm Graves disease and predict neonatal thyrotoxicosis in pregnancy**; *what:* positive antibodies). Medication and supplement history including biotin (*why:* interference and drug-induced dysfunction; *what:* culprit).

## 0.2 Hypothyroidism

**R/Causes:** **Autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis — the commonest cause in iodine-replete countries such as Australia**, often with a goitre and positive anti-TPO antibodies, and clustering with other autoimmune conditions. **Iatrogenic** — after thyroidectomy, radioiodine, or external beam radiotherapy to the neck. **Drugs** — amiodarone, lithium, checkpoint inhibitors, tyrosine kinase inhibitors. **Iodine deficiency** — the commonest cause worldwide. **Congenital.** **Central.** **Transient** — postpartum thyroiditis and the hypothyroid phase of subacute thyroiditis.

**S/Smx:** **Fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, hair thinning and loss of the outer eyebrow, hoarse voice, menorrhagia, low mood, cognitive slowing, myalgia and carpal tunnel syndrome.**
Signs: **bradycardia, delayed relaxation phase of the deep tendon reflexes** (a genuinely useful and specific sign), coarse dry skin, non-pitting periorbital and peripheral oedema, goitre, pericardial and pleural effusions, and hypothermia in severe cases.

> [!tip] The biochemical fellow-travellers
> **Hyponatraemia · hyperlipidaemia · raised creatine kinase (which can be markedly elevated and mistaken for a primary myopathy) · normocytic or macrocytic anaemia · raised prolactin.**
> **Unexplained hyperlipidaemia, a raised CK, or hyponatraemia should prompt a TSH** — it is a cheap test that occasionally explains everything.

> [!warning] Levothyroxine — the practical points that determine whether it works
> **· Take it once daily, on an empty stomach, ideally 30–60 minutes before food** — absorption is significantly reduced by food.
> **· Separate it by several hours from calcium, iron, magnesium, proton pump inhibitors, cholestyramine and soy**, all of which impair absorption. **This is the commonest reason a patient's TSH will not normalise despite escalating doses.**
> **· Start at a low dose in the elderly and in ischaemic heart disease**, and increase slowly — a full replacement dose can precipitate angina, arrhythmia or infarction.
> **· Recheck after an adequate interval** — the axis takes weeks to re-equilibrate, and checking too early leads to unnecessary dose changes.
> `UNVERIFIED — starting doses, dose adjustment increments, the recheck interval, and target TSH.`

> [!danger] Do not over-treat — a suppressed TSH is not a better result
> **Over-replacement causes atrial fibrillation and osteoporosis**, and the risk is greatest in **older people and postmenopausal women** — exactly the population most often over-treated.
> **A suppressed TSH on thyroxine is a reason to reduce the dose**, not a sign of good control.

> [!tip] Persistent symptoms with normal thyroid function
> A common and difficult scenario. **Once the TSH is genuinely in range and absorption is optimised, look for another explanation** — **anaemia and iron deficiency, coeliac disease, vitamin D and B12 deficiency, depression, obstructive sleep apnoea, adrenal insufficiency, or another autoimmune condition** (which cluster with Hashimoto disease).
> **Combination liothyronine (T3) and thyroxine therapy is not routinely recommended**, and desiccated thyroid extract is not recommended — but patients frequently ask about both, so it is worth being able to explain the position without dismissiveness. `UNVERIFIED — the current position on combination therapy.`

> [!info] Subclinical hypothyroidism
> Raised TSH with a normal free T4. **Treatment is considered where the TSH is above a defined threshold, where the patient is symptomatic, where anti-TPO antibodies are positive (predicting progression), and — importantly — in pregnancy or when planning pregnancy.**
> **Otherwise, many cases are managed with observation and repeat testing**, since a proportion normalise spontaneously. `UNVERIFIED — the TSH threshold for treatment and the monitoring interval.`

**Ix:** TSH and free T4 (*why:* diagnosis and severity; *what:* the pattern). **Anti-TPO antibodies** (*why:* confirms autoimmune aetiology and predicts progression in subclinical disease; *what:* positive). FBC, lipids, UEC, CK (*why:* the associated abnormalities, several of which resolve with treatment; *what:* anaemia, hyperlipidaemia, hyponatraemia, raised CK). Coeliac serology and other autoimmune screening where clinically indicated (*why:* autoimmune conditions cluster — type 1 diabetes, coeliac disease, pernicious anaemia, Addison disease; *what:* associated autoimmunity). **Morning cortisol where central hypothyroidism is suspected** (*why:* to exclude adrenal insufficiency before starting thyroxine; *what:* low cortisol — cross-refer [[I4]]).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Only in myxoedema coma — see 0.4.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Levothyroxine with the administration advice above, titrated to a target TSH.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Annual TFTs once stable, dose review in pregnancy (see 0.6), and awareness that **requirements change with weight, pregnancy, oestrogen therapy and interacting drugs.**

## 0.3 Hyperthyroidism and Thyrotoxicosis

**R/Causes:**
**· Graves disease** — the commonest, an autoimmune condition with **TSH receptor stimulating antibodies**, producing a **diffuse goitre with a bruit**, and — uniquely — **ophthalmopathy and pretibial myxoedema.**
**· Toxic multinodular goitre and toxic adenoma** — autonomous nodules, more common in older patients.
**· Thyroiditis** — **subacute (de Quervain): painful, tender thyroid, post-viral, with a raised ESR**; **postpartum**; and **silent/painless.** All are **destructive**, releasing preformed hormone.
**· Iodine-induced (Jod-Basedow)** — **amiodarone, iodinated contrast**, iodine supplements, in a susceptible nodular thyroid.
**· Factitious** — exogenous thyroid hormone, sometimes for weight loss.
**· hCG-mediated** — hyperemesis gravidarum, molar pregnancy.
**· Rare** — struma ovarii, TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma, metastatic thyroid cancer.

> [!danger] The most important distinction: thyroiditis versus true hyperthyroidism
> **Thyroiditis is DESTRUCTIVE — preformed hormone leaks from a damaged gland. Nothing is being synthesised.**
> **Therefore ANTITHYROID DRUGS DO NOT WORK IN THYROIDITIS**, because there is no excess synthesis to block. Giving carbimazole is futile and exposes the patient to agranulocytosis risk for no benefit.
> **Thyroiditis is self-limiting**, typically running through a thyrotoxic phase, then a hypothyroid phase, then usually recovery. **Treatment is symptomatic — a beta-blocker, and analgesia or corticosteroids for painful subacute thyroiditis.**
> **How to distinguish:**
> **· Radionuclide uptake scan — the definitive test. Uptake is HIGH in Graves disease and toxic nodules (the gland is actively making hormone), and LOW or ABSENT in thyroiditis and in factitious thyrotoxicosis (the gland is not).**
> **· TSH receptor antibodies (TRAb)** — positive in Graves disease.
> **· Clinical** — pain and tenderness with raised inflammatory markers suggests subacute thyroiditis; eye signs and a diffuse bruit suggest Graves.

**S/Smx:** **Weight loss despite increased appetite, heat intolerance, sweating, palpitations, anxiety and irritability, tremor, frequent loose stools, oligomenorrhoea, proximal muscle weakness, insomnia.**
Signs: **tachycardia or atrial fibrillation, fine tremor, warm moist skin, lid retraction and lid lag** (which occur in any thyrotoxicosis), goitre with or without bruit, **proximal myopathy, brisk reflexes, onycholysis.**

> [!danger] Apathetic thyrotoxicosis in the elderly
> **Older patients frequently present WITHOUT the hyperkinetic features** — instead with **weight loss, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, depression, apathy and proximal weakness.**
> **Check thyroid function in every new atrial fibrillation, in unexplained weight loss in an older person, and in unexplained heart failure.** This is one of the more reliably missed endocrine diagnoses.

> [!warning] Graves ophthalmopathy is a separate disease process
> **Proptosis, lid retraction, chemosis, conjunctival injection, diplopia from extraocular muscle involvement, and — in severe disease — corneal exposure and compressive OPTIC NEUROPATHY with reduced acuity and colour vision.**
> **It follows its own course, independent of the biochemical thyroid status**, and treating the thyrotoxicosis does not necessarily improve the eyes.
> **Three things matter clinically:**
> **1. SMOKING is the strongest modifiable risk factor and markedly worsens it. Cessation is a core part of management, not a general health suggestion.**
> **2. RADIOIODINE CAN WORSEN eye disease**, so it is used with caution and often with corticosteroid cover in patients with active ophthalmopathy.
> **3. Reduced visual acuity, colour desaturation or corneal ulceration is SIGHT-THREATENING and requires urgent ophthalmology.**

> [!danger] Antithyroid drugs — the agranulocytosis counselling is mandatory
> **Carbimazole (and thiamazole) and propylthiouracil can cause AGRANULOCYTOSIS**, which is rare, idiosyncratic, unpredictable, and potentially fatal.
> **Every patient must be told explicitly: "If you develop a sore throat, mouth ulcers, or a fever, STOP the tablets immediately and get a full blood count the same day."**
> **Routine monitoring of the white cell count does not reliably detect it** — the counselling is the safeguard, and **documenting that you gave it is important.**
> Also: **hepatotoxicity — more severe with propylthiouracil, which can cause fulminant hepatic failure**, which is why carbimazole is generally preferred outside the first trimester of pregnancy.
> **Carbimazole is associated with a characteristic embryopathy, so PROPYLTHIOURACIL is preferred in the FIRST TRIMESTER, with a switch back thereafter.** Cross-refer 0.6.
> `UNVERIFIED — doses, monitoring, titration versus block-and-replace regimens, and the pregnancy switching points.`

**Ix:** TSH, free T4 and **free T3** (*why:* T3 toxicosis produces a suppressed TSH with a normal free T4, and is missed if T3 is not measured; *what:* the pattern). **TSH receptor antibodies (TRAb)** (*why:* confirms Graves disease, avoids the need for a scan in many cases, and **predicts fetal and neonatal thyrotoxicosis in pregnancy**; *what:* positive). **Radionuclide uptake scan** (*why:* **the test that distinguishes hyperthyroidism from thyroiditis, which determines whether antithyroid drugs will work at all**; *what:* diffuse high uptake in Graves, focal in a toxic nodule, absent in thyroiditis — **contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding**). ESR and CRP (*why:* markedly raised in subacute thyroiditis; *what:* elevation). **ECG** (*why:* atrial fibrillation is common and may be the presenting feature; *what:* AF, tachycardia). LFT and FBC before starting antithyroid drugs (*why:* baseline before hepatotoxic and marrow-toxic agents; *what:* baseline). Bone density in longstanding or subclinical disease (*why:* thyrotoxicosis causes accelerated bone loss; *what:* reduced density). Pregnancy test (*why:* changes the agent, contraindicates radioiodine, and hCG-mediated thyrotoxicosis is a differential; *what:* positive).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
**Beta-blocker for symptomatic relief** — it treats the adrenergic symptoms rapidly while definitive therapy takes weeks. Thyroid storm per 0.4.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
**Antithyroid drugs** with agranulocytosis counselling, then **definitive therapy — radioiodine or surgery** — depending on cause, patient preference, eye disease, pregnancy plans and goitre size.
**Surgical risks to counsel: recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (voice change) and hypoparathyroidism with hypocalcaemia.**
**Radioiodine: contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding, requires avoidance of pregnancy for a defined period afterwards, may worsen eye disease, and usually results in eventual hypothyroidism requiring lifelong replacement** — which should be presented as an expected outcome rather than a complication.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Lifelong monitoring after any definitive treatment**, because hypothyroidism develops in most and may appear years later. **Smoking cessation, particularly with eye disease.** Bone and cardiac assessment.

## 0.4 Thyroid Emergencies

> [!danger] Thyroid storm — the sequence of drugs matters
> **Decompensated thyrotoxicosis with organ dysfunction: fever, marked tachycardia or tachyarrhythmia, heart failure, agitation, delirium or coma, vomiting, diarrhoea and jaundice.**
> **Precipitants: infection, surgery (especially thyroid surgery in an unprepared patient), trauma, radioiodine, an iodine load including contrast, childbirth, diabetic ketoacidosis, and abrupt cessation of antithyroid medication.**
> **This is a CLINICAL diagnosis — the biochemistry is no different from uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis. Do not wait for thyroid function tests to treat.** Scoring systems such as Burch-Wartofsky support the assessment. `UNVERIFIED — the score.`
>
> **The treatment sequence, and why the order matters:**
> **1. Beta-blocker** — controls the adrenergic manifestations and, at high dose, reduces peripheral T4 to T3 conversion.
> **2. Antithyroid drug** — blocks new hormone synthesis.
> **3. IODINE — and this must be given at least an hour AFTER the antithyroid drug.** Iodine blocks hormone release (the Wolff-Chaikoff effect), **but if given before synthesis is blocked it provides substrate and can WORSEN the storm.** **Getting this order wrong is a genuine and examinable error.**
> **4. Corticosteroid** — reduces peripheral T4 to T3 conversion and treats the relative adrenal insufficiency of severe thyrotoxicosis.
> **5. Supportive care and treatment of the precipitant** — cooling, fluids, and **treating the infection or other trigger, which is frequently the thing that actually resolves it.**
> **Avoid aspirin**, which displaces thyroid hormone from binding proteins and raises the free fraction.
> `UNVERIFIED — all agents and doses.`

> [!danger] Myxoedema coma — give hydrocortisone with the thyroid hormone
> **Decompensated hypothyroidism: hypothermia (which may be profound and missed with a standard thermometer), bradycardia, hypoventilation with hypercapnia, hypotension, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, and reduced consciousness progressing to coma.**
> **Precipitants: infection, cold exposure, sedatives and opioids, stroke, myocardial infarction, and non-adherence with thyroxine — often in an older person living alone in winter.**
> **Treatment: intravenous thyroid hormone, supportive care with passive rewarming, ventilatory support, cautious fluid and glucose management, and treatment of the precipitant.**
> **Give intravenous HYDROCORTISONE before or with the thyroid hormone** — coexisting adrenal insufficiency is common, and thyroid hormone increases cortisol clearance and metabolic demand, so **giving thyroid hormone alone can precipitate an adrenal crisis.** This mirrors the point in 0.1 and is the same physiology.
> **Rewarm passively rather than actively** — active external rewarming causes peripheral vasodilatation and cardiovascular collapse.
> **Mortality remains high.** ICU-level care.
> `UNVERIFIED — thyroid hormone preparation, loading and maintenance doses, and hydrocortisone dosing.`

**Ix:** **Treat on clinical grounds — the investigations confirm afterwards.** TSH, free T4 and free T3 (*why:* confirmatory; *what:* the pattern). **Cortisol, taken before hydrocortisone if it costs no delay** (*why:* coexisting adrenal insufficiency; *what:* inappropriately low cortisol). **Septic screen and a search for the precipitant** (*why:* infection is the commonest trigger for both, and treating it is essential; *what:* source). Glucose, UEC, sodium, VBG (*why:* hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia and hypercapnia in myxoedema coma; *what:* derangement). **Core temperature with a low-reading thermometer** (*why:* standard thermometers do not read low enough to detect the degree of hypothermia; *what:* accurate core temperature). ECG and cardiac monitoring (*why:* arrhythmia in both conditions; *what:* AF, bradyarrhythmia). CK (*why:* markedly raised in myxoedema; *what:* elevation).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
As per the sequences above. **ICU involvement, endocrinology involvement, and treatment of the precipitant.**

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Transition to oral therapy and definitive management of the underlying thyroid disease once stable.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Address the reason it happened — most often non-adherence, an unrecognised precipitant, or inadequate preparation before surgery or radioiodine.** Education and follow-up.

## 0.5 Thyroid Nodule and Goitre

> [!warning] Thyroid nodules are extremely common and the great majority are benign
> Prevalence rises with age, and **incidental nodules are found frequently on imaging performed for other reasons** — carotid ultrasound, neck CT, PET.
> **The clinical task is to identify the small proportion that are malignant or functional, without subjecting everyone to biopsy.**

> [!tip] The assessment pathway, and why TFTs come first
> **1. Thyroid function tests FIRST.**
> **2. If the TSH is SUPPRESSED → radionuclide uptake scan.** A **"hot" (autonomously functioning) nodule is almost never malignant**, so **it does not need fine needle aspiration** — it needs treatment for hyperthyroidism.
> **3. If the TSH is normal or raised → ultrasound with sonographic risk stratification**, and then **fine needle aspiration based on the size and the sonographic features combined**, not on size alone.
> **Doing the TFT first is what prevents an unnecessary biopsy of a hot nodule.**

> [!tip] Sonographic features suggesting malignancy
> **· Microcalcifications · a "taller than wide" shape · irregular or infiltrative margins · marked hypoechogenicity · extrathyroidal extension · associated abnormal cervical lymph nodes.**
> **Purely cystic lesions and spongiform nodules are almost always benign.**
> Risk stratification systems (TIRADS and equivalents) formalise this and determine the FNA threshold. `UNVERIFIED — the systems, categories and size thresholds.`

> [!warning] Clinical features raising concern
> **· Age extremes — under 20 or over 70 · male sex · a history of childhood head or neck irradiation · a family history of thyroid cancer or MEN2 · rapid growth · a hard, fixed nodule · hoarseness from recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement · cervical lymphadenopathy · compressive symptoms.**
> **New hoarseness with a thyroid nodule is malignancy until proven otherwise** and warrants urgent assessment including laryngoscopy. Cross-refer [[D7]] 0.4.

> [!info] The thyroid cancers, and the one FNA cannot diagnose
> **· Papillary — the commonest.** Spreads via lymphatics to cervical nodes. **Excellent prognosis.** Associated with radiation exposure.
> **· Follicular** — spreads haematogenously to bone and lung. **CRITICAL POINT: fine needle aspiration CANNOT distinguish follicular adenoma from follicular carcinoma**, because the distinction rests on capsular and vascular invasion, which requires the whole lesion. **A "follicular neoplasm" on cytology therefore requires diagnostic surgical excision.**
> **· Medullary** — arises from **parafollicular C cells**, secretes **CALCITONIN**, and is associated with **MEN2**, so a diagnosis mandates **RET proto-oncogene testing and family screening**, and **phaeochromocytoma must be excluded before any surgery.** Cross-refer [[I4]] and [[B2]] 0.4.
> **· Anaplastic** — elderly, **rapidly enlarging hard mass with compressive symptoms**, very poor prognosis.
> **· Thyroid lymphoma** — arising on a background of **Hashimoto thyroiditis**, presenting as a rapidly enlarging goitre.
> Cytology is reported using the **Bethesda system**, which stratifies malignancy risk and guides management. `UNVERIFIED — the categories and their associated risks.`

> [!tip] Goitre and compressive symptoms
> **Diffuse** (Graves, Hashimoto, iodine deficiency, physiological in puberty and pregnancy) or **multinodular.**
> **Retrosternal extension** produces compressive symptoms — **dysphagia, dyspnoea, stridor, and superior vena cava obstruction.**
> **Pemberton sign — facial plethora, distress and venous distension on raising both arms above the head** — indicates thoracic inlet obstruction and is a simple, useful bedside test. Cross-refer [[B6]] 0.4.
> **Assess the airway**: stridor, tracheal deviation, and — where compression is suspected — **CT and flow-volume loops** rather than reassurance. Cross-refer [[A2]] 0.3.

**Ix:** **TSH first** (*why:* determines whether the next step is a scan or an ultrasound, and prevents unnecessary biopsy of a hot nodule; *what:* suppressed, normal or raised). **Thyroid ultrasound with risk stratification** (*why:* characterises the nodule and assesses cervical nodes, and the features determine the FNA threshold; *what:* size, sonographic features, lymphadenopathy). **Radionuclide uptake scan if TSH suppressed** (*why:* identifies an autonomously functioning nodule; *what:* hot or cold). **Fine needle aspiration per size and risk criteria** (*why:* the diagnostic test for most nodules, with the follicular caveat; *what:* Bethesda category). **Calcitonin where medullary carcinoma is suspected or there is a family history** (*why:* the tumour marker, and it prompts RET testing and phaeochromocytoma exclusion; *what:* elevated calcitonin). CT neck and thorax **without iodinated contrast where radioiodine treatment is anticipated** (*why:* an iodine load interferes with subsequent radioiodine uptake and treatment for months — **this is an easily made and consequential error**; *what:* retrosternal extension, compression, nodes). Laryngoscopy where there is voice change or before thyroid surgery (*why:* documents pre-existing cord function; *what:* cord palsy).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Airway assessment where there is stridor or significant compression.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Surveillance for benign nodules, surgery for malignancy, indeterminate cytology, compressive symptoms or a toxic nodule, and **radioiodine or antithyroid drugs for functional nodules.**

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Lifelong thyroxine after total thyroidectomy** — and **TSH suppression rather than simple replacement in differentiated thyroid cancer**, to reduce recurrence. Thyroglobulin monitoring as a tumour marker. Family screening in medullary carcinoma.

## 0.6 Thyroid Disease in Pregnancy and Special Situations

> [!danger] Pregnancy changes the physiology, the reference ranges and the doses
> **· hCG structurally resembles TSH and weakly stimulates the TSH receptor, so TSH is PHYSIOLOGICALLY SUPPRESSED in the first trimester.** A low TSH in early pregnancy is often normal, and applying non-pregnant reference ranges over-diagnoses hyperthyroidism.
> **· Trimester-specific reference ranges must be used**, and they are assay-specific. `UNVERIFIED — obtain from your laboratory.`
> **· Oestrogen raises thyroxine-binding globulin**, so **total** T4 rises while free T4 remains appropriate.
> **· Thyroxine requirements INCREASE substantially in pregnancy — commonly by around a third.** **A woman on thyroxine who becomes pregnant needs her dose increased as soon as pregnancy is confirmed, without waiting for a test**, with early and frequent monitoring. **Many services advise the woman in advance to increase her own dose on a positive pregnancy test.** `UNVERIFIED — the recommended dose increase and monitoring schedule.`
> **· Untreated or under-treated maternal hypothyroidism is associated with miscarriage, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, and impaired fetal neurodevelopment** — fetal thyroid hormone is entirely maternal in the first trimester.

> [!warning] Hyperthyroidism in pregnancy
> **· Gestational transient thyrotoxicosis** — hCG-mediated, associated with **hyperemesis gravidarum**, self-limiting, **TRAb negative, no eye signs, no goitre.** Usually needs no antithyroid treatment.
> **· Graves disease** — needs treatment. **Propylthiouracil is preferred in the FIRST trimester because of carbimazole embryopathy, with a switch to carbimazole thereafter because of propylthiouracil hepatotoxicity.**
> **· Use the LOWEST effective dose** — antithyroid drugs cross the placenta and can cause fetal hypothyroidism and goitre.
> **· Radioiodine is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding.**
> **· TRAb crosses the placenta and can cause FETAL AND NEONATAL THYROTOXICOSIS — even in a mother who is euthyroid after previous definitive treatment**, because the antibodies persist. **TRAb should be measured in pregnancy in women with current or past Graves disease**, and a high titre prompts fetal monitoring and neonatal assessment.
> `UNVERIFIED — trimester switching points, dosing, and TRAb monitoring recommendations.`

> [!tip] Postpartum thyroiditis
> Occurs in a meaningful proportion of women in the year after delivery, more commonly in those with **positive anti-TPO antibodies or type 1 diabetes.**
> Classically a **thyrotoxic phase, then a hypothyroid phase, then recovery** — though any phase may occur alone.
> **It is frequently misattributed to postnatal depression, normal postpartum fatigue, or anxiety**, and the hypothyroid phase in particular overlaps heavily with those. **Check thyroid function in a woman with postnatal mood or fatigue symptoms.**
> A proportion develop permanent hypothyroidism, so follow-up is needed. Cross-refer [[O3]].

> [!danger] Amiodarone-induced thyroid dysfunction — two types, opposite treatments
> Amiodarone contains a large iodine load and is highly lipophilic with a very long half-life, so **effects persist for many months after cessation.**
> **· Amiodarone-induced HYPOthyroidism** — commoner in iodine-replete areas such as Australia, straightforward to treat with thyroxine, and **amiodarone can usually be continued.**
> **· Amiodarone-induced THYROTOXICOSIS type 1** — **iodine-induced excess synthesis in a pre-existing nodular or autonomous gland.** Treated with **antithyroid drugs.**
> **· Amiodarone-induced THYROTOXICOSIS type 2** — **a destructive thyroiditis.** Treated with **corticosteroids.**
> **Distinguishing type 1 from type 2 is genuinely difficult**, colour Doppler flow on ultrasound helps (increased in type 1, absent in type 2), and **mixed forms occur — so both treatments are frequently given together.** This is a specialist problem, and **stopping amiodarone does not produce rapid improvement because of the long half-life.**
> `UNVERIFIED — diagnostic criteria and treatment regimens.`

> [!tip] Other situations worth knowing
> **· Immune checkpoint inhibitor thyroiditis** — common, often a transient thyrotoxic phase followed by permanent hypothyroidism requiring lifelong replacement. Cross-refer [[A5]] 0.7.
> **· Lithium** — hypothyroidism and goitre; monitor thyroid function during therapy.
> **· Congenital hypothyroidism** — **detected by newborn screening (the heel prick test) in Australia**, and **early treatment prevents the profound intellectual disability that untreated congenital hypothyroidism causes.** This is one of the clearest examples of the value of newborn screening. Cross-refer [[M4]].
> **· Iodine** — Australia has had mandatory iodine fortification of bread, and **pregnant and breastfeeding women are recommended iodine supplementation.** `UNVERIFIED — current supplementation recommendations.`

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
As per the underlying condition, with pregnancy-appropriate agents.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Multidisciplinary management with obstetrics and endocrinology. **Increase thyroxine promptly on confirmation of pregnancy.**

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Postpartum thyroid function checks** in women with autoimmune thyroid disease. **Neonatal assessment where maternal TRAb is high.** Preconception optimisation for the next pregnancy.
