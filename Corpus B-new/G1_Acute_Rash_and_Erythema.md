## 0.1 Describing a Rash

> [!tip] The vocabulary — because an accurate description gets you a diagnosis over the phone
> **PRIMARY LESIONS:**
> **· MACULE — flat, small, colour change only. PATCH — flat, larger.**
> **· PAPULE — raised, small. PLAQUE — raised, larger, with a flat top. NODULE — raised and DEEPER.**
> **· VESICLE — fluid-filled, small. BULLA — fluid-filled, larger. PUSTULE — pus-filled.**
> **· WHEAL — a transient, oedematous, itchy swelling that moves and resolves within hours (the defining feature of urticaria).**
> **· PURPURA — NON-BLANCHING. PETECHIAE are small; ECCHYMOSES are large.**
> **SECONDARY CHANGES: scale · crust · excoriation · LICHENIFICATION (thickened skin with accentuated markings, from chronic rubbing) · EROSION (loss of epidermis only — heals WITHOUT scarring) · ULCER (extends into dermis — heals WITH a scar) · fissure · atrophy.**
> **CONFIGURATION: annular · TARGETOID · linear · GROUPED/herpetiform · reticulate (net-like) · serpiginous · DERMATOMAL.**
> **DISTRIBUTION: flexural · extensor · PHOTODISTRIBUTED (face, V of neck, dorsal hands, sparing under the chin and behind the ears — the shadowed sites are the clue) · acral · truncal · symmetrical or asymmetrical.**

> [!danger] Two things to do with every rash
> **1. PRESS ON IT. BLANCHING VERSUS NON-BLANCHING is the single most important bedside test in dermatology**, and it separates a benign exanthem from purpura requiring urgent assessment. Use a glass. Cross-refer [[G3]].
> **2. EXAMINE THE WHOLE SKIN, UNDRESSED — plus the MUCOSAE (mouth, eyes, genitals), the NAILS, the SCALP, and the PALMS AND SOLES.**
> **Mucosal involvement changes the diagnosis completely** — it is what distinguishes a straightforward drug rash from Stevens-Johnson syndrome. **A rash examined through a gap in a gown is a rash half seen.**

> [!danger] Erythema is under-recognised in darker skin — and it causes delayed diagnosis
> **Erythema in richly pigmented skin may appear VIOLACEOUS, BROWN, GREY or simply darker rather than red**, and the classic textbook descriptions ("bright red", "salmon pink") are written from white skin.
> **The consequences are documented and serious: DELAYED DIAGNOSIS OF CELLULITIS · under-recognition of erythroderma · missed CYANOSIS and missed pallor · and jaundice detected late.**
> **What to do instead: PALPATE — warmth, induration, tenderness and swelling do not depend on colour · COMPARE with the patient's own unaffected skin, ideally the contralateral side · use good natural light · examine the mucosae, conjunctivae, palms and soles where colour change is easier to appreciate · and ASK the patient what has changed, since they know their own skin.**
> **Also: post-inflammatory HYPER- and HYPOpigmentation are more prominent and more persistent in darker skin, and are a significant cause of distress that should be acknowledged rather than dismissed as cosmetic.**
> **Photograph lesions with consent** — for monitoring, for referral, and because dermatology advice by phone is far better with an image.

## 0.2 The Acutely Unwell Patient with a Rash

> [!danger] The red flags — any of these changes the pace of the assessment
> **· FEVER with a rash.**
> **· A NON-BLANCHING rash.**
> **· MUCOSAL INVOLVEMENT — mouth, eyes, genitals.**
> **· SKIN PAIN OR TENDERNESS OUT OF PROPORTION to what is visible.** *(Necrotising fasciitis, TEN.)*
> **· BLISTERING, skin sloughing, or a POSITIVE NIKOLSKY SIGN (gentle lateral pressure shears the epidermis).**
> **· FACIAL OR PERIORBITAL OEDEMA.** *(DRESS, angioedema.)*
> **· LYMPHADENOPATHY and systemic upset.**
> **· RAPID PROGRESSION over hours.**
> **· Hypotension, tachycardia, confusion — in which case the rash is a clue to a systemic illness, not the problem itself.**

> [!warning] The dermatological emergencies
> **· MENINGOCOCCAL SEPSIS — a non-blanching rash in an unwell patient. Antibiotics immediately, before investigation.** Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.9.
> **· NECROTISING FASCIITIS — PAIN OUT OF PROPORTION to the appearance, systemic toxicity, rapid progression, dusky or grey discolouration, bullae, crepitus, and — a late and ominous sign — ANAESTHESIA over the affected skin as cutaneous nerves are destroyed.** **It is a SURGICAL emergency and the diagnosis is clinical; imaging must not delay theatre.** Cross-refer [[K2]] 0.2.
> **· STEVENS-JOHNSON SYNDROME and TOXIC EPIDERMAL NECROLYSIS** — see 0.3.
> **· DRESS** — see 0.3.
> **· STAPHYLOCOCCAL SCALDED SKIN SYNDROME — superficial blistering with a positive Nikolsky sign, typically in young children, from an exfoliative toxin. The mucosae are SPARED, which distinguishes it from TEN.**
> **· TOXIC SHOCK SYNDROME — diffuse erythroderma, fever, hypotension and multi-organ involvement, with later desquamation of the palms and soles. Look for a retained tampon, packing, a wound or a burn.**
> **· ERYTHRODERMA — cross-refer [[G2]].**
> **· ANAPHYLAXIS with urticaria and angioedema — cross-refer [[K4]] 0.1.**
> **· PURPURA FULMINANS and DIC.**

> [!danger] Eczema herpeticum — the one that gets treated as infected eczema
> **Disseminated herpes simplex infection of eczematous skin.**
> **The appearance: MONOMORPHIC, PUNCHED-OUT, uniform round EROSIONS or vesicles — all the same size and stage — on a background of eczema, most often on the face and neck, with fever and systemic upset and severe pain.**
> **THE DISCRIMINATOR IS MONOMORPHISM: bacterially infected eczema is variable and crusted; eczema herpeticum lesions all look identical to each other.**
> **It is treated as "infected eczema" with antibiotics and topical steroids while it disseminates — and it can cause keratoconjunctivitis, encephalitis and death.**
> **Start ACICLOVIR urgently on suspicion, take viral swabs, and involve dermatology and — if periocular — ophthalmology.** `UNVERIFIED — dosing.`

## 0.3 Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions

> [!danger] Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis
> **A single disease spectrum, separated by the percentage of body surface area with epidermal DETACHMENT — SJS at the lower end, TEN at the upper, with an overlap band between.** `UNVERIFIED — the percentage thresholds.`
> **THE COURSE: a PRODROME of fever, malaise and flu-like symptoms for a few days → PAINFUL skin and a burning sensation in the eyes and mouth → dusky macules and atypical targetoid lesions → blistering, then sheets of epidermis detaching.**
> **MUCOSAL INVOLVEMENT AT TWO OR MORE SITES (oral, ocular, genital) IS CHARACTERISTIC and often PRECEDES the skin changes** — so painful mouth and eyes in a patient on a new drug is the presentation to catch, before the skin declares itself.
> **NIKOLSKY SIGN IS POSITIVE.**
> **THE DRUGS: ALLOPURINOL · ANTICONVULSANTS (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, phenobarbitone) · SULFONAMIDES including co-trimoxazole · NSAIDs (oxicams) · nevirapine · and some antibiotics.**
> **Typical onset is within the first weeks of starting the drug.**
> **PHARMACOGENOMICS: HLA-B\*15:02 predicts carbamazepine-induced SJS/TEN in Han Chinese, Thai and some other Asian populations, and HLA-B\*58:01 predicts allopurinol reactions.** **Testing before prescribing is recommended in those populations — and it also means RELATIVES may carry the same risk allele, which should be mentioned.** Cross-refer [[L1]] 0.3.

> [!warning] Managing SJS/TEN — it is a burns problem
> **· STOP THE CULPRIT DRUG IMMEDIATELY. Delay in stopping is associated with worse outcomes.** Stop all non-essential drugs started in the preceding weeks.
> **· TRANSFER to a burns unit or intensive care — these patients lose the skin's barrier function and need the same care as a burns patient: fluid and electrolyte management, temperature control (they cannot thermoregulate), meticulous wound care, nutrition, analgesia (the pain is severe), and infection prevention with a high index of suspicion for sepsis, since fever is unreliable.**
> **· OPHTHALMOLOGY INVOLVEMENT EARLY AND DAILY.** **OCULAR SEQUELAE ARE THE MAJOR LONG-TERM MORBIDITY — symblepharon, corneal scarring, dry eye and blindness — and early intensive ocular care substantially reduces them.** **This is the single most valuable referral and it is frequently late.**
> **· Urological and gynaecological input for genital involvement, to prevent stricture and adhesions.**
> **· Immunomodulatory treatment (ciclosporin, immunoglobulin, etanercept) is used but remains contested; corticosteroids are debated.**
> **· SCORTEN is a validated prognostic score.**
> **· AFTERWARDS: a clear, prominent ALLERGY ALERT naming the drug and the reaction, counselling about the drug CLASS and cross-reactivity, and a written record the patient carries.**

> [!danger] DRESS — the late one, with organ involvement, that relapses
> **Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms.**
> **THE DEFINING FEATURE IS THE TIMING: onset is LATE, typically TWO TO EIGHT WEEKS after starting the drug — much later than other drug eruptions.** **This is why the culprit is frequently not suspected: the drug has been taken uneventfully for over a month.**
> **The features: FEVER · a widespread rash, often becoming erythrodermic · FACIAL OEDEMA (characteristic and useful) · LYMPHADENOPATHY · EOSINOPHILIA and atypical lymphocytes · and ORGAN INVOLVEMENT — most often HEPATITIS, but also nephritis, pneumonitis, myocarditis and colitis.**
> **The drugs overlap with SJS/TEN: allopurinol, anticonvulsants, sulfonamides, vancomycin, minocycline, and antituberculous drugs.**
> **THREE THINGS THAT CATCH PEOPLE OUT:**
> **1. It requires a PROLONGED corticosteroid taper — and it FLARES if steroids are reduced too quickly, weeks into treatment.**
> **2. It can RELAPSE after apparent recovery, sometimes triggered by an unrelated drug.**
> **3. AUTOIMMUNE SEQUELAE APPEAR MONTHS LATER — particularly THYROID DISEASE, and also type 1 diabetes.** **These patients need follow-up thyroid function tests months after recovery, which is routinely not arranged.**

> [!tip] Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis
> **RAPID onset (within days of the drug, often hours) of numerous small STERILE PUSTULES on a background of oedematous erythema, starting in the flexures, with FEVER and NEUTROPHILIA.**
> **Usually caused by ANTIBIOTICS — aminopenicillins and macrolides in particular.**
> **It looks alarming and infective but is a drug reaction; it resolves over days to weeks after stopping the drug, characteristically with DESQUAMATION.**
> **The differential is generalised pustular psoriasis, which looks similar but has a different history.**

## 0.4 Drug Eruptions

> [!tip] Timing is the most useful diagnostic tool
> **· IMMEDIATE (minutes to about an hour) — IgE-mediated. Urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis, bronchospasm.** **This is true allergy and it is the pattern that carries a risk of anaphylaxis on re-exposure.** Cross-refer [[K4]] 0.1.
> **· DELAYED (days to weeks) — T-cell mediated. Morbilliform eruptions, fixed drug eruption, SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP.**
> **Asking WHEN the reaction occurred relative to the dose separates these two mechanisms and determines the risk of re-exposure**, and it is the first question in any allergy assessment.

> [!info] The common patterns
> **· MORBILLIFORM (exanthematous, maculopapular) DRUG ERUPTION — by far the commonest.** Typically **4 to 14 days** after starting a new drug, beginning on the trunk and spreading outward, symmetrical, itchy, WITHOUT mucosal involvement and WITHOUT systemic upset. **It resolves over one to two weeks after stopping, often with desquamation.**
> **THE CLINICAL TASK IS DISTINGUISHING IT FROM AN EARLY SEVERE REACTION — so check for fever, facial oedema, mucosal involvement, skin tenderness, blistering, lymphadenopathy, and eosinophilia or deranged liver function.** **In their absence, it is a simple drug eruption.**
> **· FIXED DRUG ERUPTION — a well-demarcated, round, dusky or violaceous plaque that RECURS AT PRECISELY THE SAME SITE with each exposure to the drug**, sometimes blistering, and leaving persistent post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. **The recurrence at an identical site is pathognomonic and is a satisfying diagnosis to make from the history alone.**
> **· PHOTOSENSITIVE eruptions — in a photodistribution. DOXYCYCLINE, thiazides, amiodarone, NSAIDs, voriconazole, and St John's wort.** **Worth warning about explicitly in Australia.**
> **· ACNEIFORM — corticosteroids, lithium, and EGFR INHIBITORS (where the rash correlates with treatment response and is managed rather than stopped).**
> **· Lichenoid, psoriasiform, and pigmentary changes (amiodarone's slate-grey discolouration, minocycline pigmentation).**

> [!danger] "Penicillin allergy" — delabelling is a real clinical intervention
> **A large majority of patients labelled penicillin-allergic are NOT allergic** — the label is often applied in childhood after a viral exanthem coinciding with an antibiotic, or after a gastrointestinal side effect, and it is never revisited.
> **THE LABEL CAUSES MEASURABLE HARM: broader-spectrum and less effective antibiotics · more *Clostridioides difficile* infection · more MRSA and VRE · worse surgical site infection rates · longer admissions · and higher mortality in serious infection.**
> **DELABELLING — through a careful history, and where appropriate skin testing or supervised oral challenge — is a genuine and under-used intervention.**
> **What to ask: WHAT happened · WHEN in relation to the dose · HOW LONG AGO · was there urticaria, angioedema, breathing difficulty or hypotension · were there mucosal or systemic features · and has the patient taken any beta-lactam since?**
> **A history of gastrointestinal upset, a family history of allergy, or an unremembered childhood rash is NOT an allergy.**
> **THE AMOXICILLIN-EBV RASH: a widespread morbilliform rash after amoxicillin in infectious mononucleosis is a well-described NON-ALLERGIC phenomenon and does NOT indicate penicillin allergy** — yet it is one of the commonest reasons for a lifelong label. Cross-refer [[K4]] 0.3.

## 0.5 Infectious Exanthems in Adults

> [!danger] Two infections that present as a rash and are missed
> **1. ACUTE HIV SEROCONVERSION.** **Fever · a non-specific maculopapular rash on the trunk · PHARYNGITIS · generalised LYMPHADENOPATHY · MOUTH AND GENITAL ULCERS · myalgia · and headache — a "glandular fever-like" illness two to six weeks after exposure.**
> **This is the single greatest missed opportunity in HIV care: the patient presents, is diagnosed with a viral illness, and leaves — at the point of PEAK INFECTIVITY.**
> **TEST FOR HIV in any adult with a febrile illness with rash, pharyngitis and lymphadenopathy.** **Note that the antibody test may be negative in the window period — request a COMBINED ANTIGEN/ANTIBODY assay or HIV RNA.** Cross-refer [[K3]] 0.5 and [[O6]] 0.2.
> **2. SECONDARY SYPHILIS — "the great imitator".**
> **A widespread, symmetrical, NON-ITCHY maculopapular rash that CHARACTERISTICALLY INVOLVES THE PALMS AND SOLES** — one of very few rashes that does — with malaise, fever, generalised lymphadenopathy, mucous patches, and CONDYLOMATA LATA in flexural areas.
> **It occurs weeks to months after the primary chancre, which may not have been noticed.**
> **Australia has a sustained INFECTIOUS SYPHILIS OUTBREAK, including congenital cases** — so the threshold to test should be low. **Serology is the test, and it is cheap.** Cross-refer [[O6]] 0.1.

> [!warning] The viral exanthems in adults
> **· MEASLES — RESURGING where vaccination coverage has fallen.** Prodrome with cough, coryza and conjunctivitis, KOPLIK SPOTS, then a descending maculopapular rash. **Highly infectious, NOTIFIABLE, and requires immediate isolation and notification ON SUSPICION.** Cross-refer [[M2]] 0.5 and [[PH1]] 0.6.
> **· VARICELLA — significantly MORE SEVERE IN ADULTS than in children, with a substantial risk of VARICELLA PNEUMONIA, particularly in smokers and in pregnancy.** Lesions in CROPS AT DIFFERENT STAGES.
> **· HERPES ZOSTER (shingles) — a painful DERMATOMAL vesicular eruption that does not cross the midline.**
> **HUTCHINSON SIGN — vesicles on the tip, side or root of the NOSE indicate nasociliary nerve involvement and predict OCULAR involvement in herpes zoster ophthalmicus. Refer to ophthalmology.** Cross-refer [[E1]].
> **RAMSAY HUNT SYNDROME — zoster of the geniculate ganglion: facial palsy with vesicles in the ear canal or on the palate, plus vertigo and hearing loss. It has a worse prognosis than Bell palsy and requires antivirals and steroids promptly.** Cross-refer [[D5]] and [[F1]].
> **· PARVOVIRUS B19 in adults — often presenting with an ARTHROPATHY rather than the slapped-cheek rash, and it causes aplastic crisis and fetal hydrops.** Cross-refer [[J2]] 0.1.
> **· EBV, cytomegalovirus, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, and enteroviral exanthems.**

> [!tip] The Australian and travel-related causes
> **· RICKETTSIAL INFECTIONS — Queensland tick typhus, Flinders Island spotted fever, and SCRUB TYPHUS in northern Australia. LOOK FOR AN ESCHAR — a painless black necrotic crust at the bite site, frequently in a skin fold and missed unless specifically sought.** **Treat empirically with doxycycline; serology is retrospective.** Cross-refer [[K1]] 0.4.
> **· ROSS RIVER and BARMAH FOREST VIRUS — fever, rash and prominent POLYARTHRALGIA, which may persist for months.**
> **· DENGUE in returned travellers — fever, severe myalgia, retro-orbital pain, and a rash with characteristic "islands of white in a sea of red" during recovery, with thrombocytopenia.**
> **· SCABIES — intense itch WORSE AT NIGHT, burrows in finger web spaces, wrists, axillae and genitalia, and OTHER HOUSEHOLD MEMBERS ITCHING. Treat all contacts simultaneously.** **CRUSTED (Norwegian) SCABIES in the immunosuppressed, elderly or neurologically impaired is hyperinfestational, extremely contagious, and a significant issue in some remote Australian communities.** Cross-refer [[K2]] 0.6 and [[AU1]] 0.4.

## 0.6 Cellulitis and Its Mimics

> [!danger] Bilateral lower limb "cellulitis" is almost never cellulitis
> **THIS IS THE SINGLE MOST USEFUL DISCRIMINATOR IN THE ASSESSMENT OF RED LEGS.**
> **Cellulitis is essentially always UNILATERAL. Bilateral symmetrical erythema of the lower legs is VENOUS ECZEMA (stasis dermatitis) or LIPODERMATOSCLEROSIS until proven otherwise.**
> **Cellulitis is substantially OVER-DIAGNOSED — a large proportion of patients admitted with a diagnosis of cellulitis have something else ("pseudocellulitis"), receiving unnecessary antibiotics and admission while the actual condition goes untreated.**

> [!tip] The mimics, and how to tell them apart
> **· VENOUS ECZEMA / STASIS DERMATITIS — BILATERAL, ITCHY rather than painful, with scaling, haemosiderin staining, varicose veins and oedema, and no fever. Treated with EMOLLIENTS, TOPICAL STEROID and COMPRESSION — not antibiotics.**
> **· LIPODERMATOSCLEROSIS — chronic induration and fibrosis of the lower leg producing the "INVERTED CHAMPAGNE BOTTLE" appearance, often painful in the acute phase.**
> **· DEEP VEIN THROMBOSIS — and it can coexist with cellulitis.** Cross-refer [[B6]] 0.4 and [[J3]] 0.4.
> **· ACUTE GOUT — periarticular erythema that can look strikingly like cellulitis.** Cross-refer [[L1]] 0.3.
> **· CONTACT DERMATITIS — ITCHY not painful, with SHARP margins corresponding to the contactant and sometimes a geometric shape.**
> **· Ruptured Baker cyst · superficial thrombophlebitis · panniculitis · ERYTHEMA NODOSUM (tender red nodules on the shins, with an important underlying differential including streptococcal infection, sarcoidosis, IBD, TB and drugs) · insect bite reactions · CALCIPHYLAXIS in dialysis patients · INFLAMMATORY BREAST CANCER when the "cellulitis" is on a breast (cross-refer [[O7]] 0.4) · and NECROTISING FASCIITIS.**

> [!warning] If it really is cellulitis — three things that get missed
> **1. FIND AND TREAT THE PORTAL OF ENTRY.** **TINEA PEDIS with macerated interdigital skin is the commonest and most overlooked source of recurrent lower limb cellulitis.** **LOOK BETWEEN THE TOES.** Also: leg ulcers, eczema, trauma, and lymphoedema.
> **Treating the cellulitis without treating the tinea guarantees recurrence.**
> **2. MARK THE BORDER WITH A PEN AND WRITE THE DATE AND TIME ON IT.** It is the only objective way to know, tomorrow, whether the erythema is advancing or receding — and it takes seconds.
> **Note that erythema often appears to worsen in the first 24–48 hours despite effective treatment, as inflammation from bacterial killing continues.**
> **3. ELEVATE THE LIMB.** Genuinely effective, and consistently not done because it requires the patient to actually be in bed with the leg raised, not sitting in a chair.
> **ERYSIPELAS — a more superficial infection with a SHARPLY DEMARCATED, RAISED, advancing border, classically on the face or shin, usually streptococcal.**
> **RECURRENT cellulitis: address lymphoedema, treat tinea, manage venous disease and weight, and consider ANTIBIOTIC PROPHYLAXIS.** `UNVERIFIED — regimens.`

> [!danger] Failure to improve means reconsider the diagnosis, not escalate the antibiotic
> **If a patient with "cellulitis" is not improving after an appropriate period of appropriate antibiotics, the most likely explanation is that IT IS NOT CELLULITIS.**
> **The default response — broadening the antibiotic — is usually wrong.**
> **Reconsider: is it one of the mimics above? Is there an ABSCESS requiring drainage? Is there NECROTISING infection? Is there osteomyelitis or septic arthritis underneath? Is it a drug reaction? Is the patient immunosuppressed with an atypical organism?**
> **Ask for a dermatology or surgical opinion rather than a third antibiotic.**

**Ix:** **Examination of the WHOLE skin undressed, plus mucosae, nails, scalp, palms and soles** (*why:* mucosal involvement changes the diagnosis from a drug rash to a severe cutaneous adverse reaction; *what:* distribution, mucosal lesions, Nikolsky sign). **The blanching test with a glass** (*why:* separates exanthem from purpura; *what:* blanching or not). **A precise DRUG HISTORY covering the preceding 8 WEEKS, including over-the-counter and complementary preparations, with dates of starting** (*why:* DRESS occurs 2–8 weeks in, so a "recent drug" history covering only days misses it; *what:* the temporal relationship). **FBC with DIFFERENTIAL (eosinophils), LFT, UEC, CRP** (*why:* eosinophilia and hepatitis identify DRESS, and organ involvement determines severity; *what:* eosinophilia, transaminitis, renal impairment). Blood cultures where febrile (*why:* sepsis; *what:* organism). **Skin swabs, viral swabs or PCR for suspected herpes simplex, and skin scrapings for fungal microscopy and culture** (*why:* eczema herpeticum needs aciclovir, and tinea is the portal of entry in recurrent cellulitis; *what:* HSV, dermatophyte). **HIV and SYPHILIS serology in an adult with an unexplained febrile rash** (*why:* both are missed at the point where treatment matters most and both are cheap to exclude; *what:* positive serology). **SKIN BIOPSY** where the diagnosis is uncertain or a severe reaction is suspected (*why:* distinguishes the severe cutaneous reactions and vasculitis, and guides management; *what:* histology — **and take it before starting systemic steroids where feasible**). **Photography with consent** (*why:* documents progression objectively and transforms the quality of remote dermatology advice; *what:* the evolution).
