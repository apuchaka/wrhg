## 0.1 Respiratory Failure — Type 1 versus Type 2

**D:** Failure of the respiratory system to maintain adequate gas exchange, classified by whether carbon dioxide is normal or elevated.

**A/P:** The lung performs two separable jobs. **Oxygenation** depends on matching ventilation to perfusion across the alveolar-capillary membrane. **Ventilation** — the clearance of CO₂ — depends almost entirely on minute volume, since CO₂ diffuses far more readily than oxygen. The two fail independently, and which one has failed determines the treatment.

> [!tip] The classification
> **Type 1 — hypoxaemic.** Low PaO₂ with a normal or **low** PaCO₂. The lung's gas exchange surface has failed but the pump is working, often working harder than normal, which is why CO₂ is blown off. Causes: pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE, ARDS, pneumothorax, asthma early.
> **Type 2 — hypercapnic.** Low PaO₂ with a **raised** PaCO₂. The pump has failed — inadequate minute ventilation regardless of how good the lung is. Causes: COPD, severe asthma late, opioid and sedative toxicity, neuromuscular disease, chest wall disease, obesity hypoventilation, exhaustion from any cause.
> `UNVERIFIED — the specific PaO₂ and PaCO₂ threshold values defining each type require verification against a current source; no numbers are stated here.`

> [!warning] Type 1 becomes Type 2 when the patient tires
> This is not a fixed category. A patient with pneumonia hyperventilating to maintain oxygenation has Type 1 failure with a low CO₂. When they exhaust, the CO₂ rises. **The transition is the deterioration**, and it is why a "normalising" CO₂ in a distressed patient is a warning rather than reassurance — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.1.

> [!info] Acute versus chronic — the bicarbonate answers it
> Renal compensation for hypercapnia takes days. So a patient with a high CO₂ and a **high bicarbonate with a near-normal pH** has chronic retention. A high CO₂ with a **normal bicarbonate and a low pH** is acute. Acute-on-chronic shows a raised bicarbonate that is nonetheless insufficient, leaving an acidotic pH. This single comparison determines whether the patient needs NIV now.

> [!tip] Five mechanisms of hypoxaemia — and the one that matters at the bedside
> **V/Q mismatch** — commonest, improves with supplemental oxygen.
> **Shunt** — blood bypasses ventilated alveoli entirely (consolidation, collapse, ARDS, intracardiac). **Does not correct with supplemental oxygen**, and this refractoriness is the discriminating clinical observation.
> **Hypoventilation** — raised CO₂ with a **normal alveolar-arterial gradient**; the lung is fine, the pump is not.
> **Diffusion impairment** — interstitial disease, usually exertional.
> **Low inspired oxygen** — altitude, or a disconnected oxygen supply, which is worth excluding before anything clever.
> `UNVERIFIED — the alveolar gas equation, the A-a gradient formula and its age-adjusted normal range require verification.`

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, accessory muscle use, cyanosis (late and unreliable). Hypercapnia specifically: drowsiness, confusion, headache, warm peripheries with a bounding pulse, and asterixis.

**Ix:** ABG (*why:* the only test that separates Type 1 from Type 2 and acute from chronic, since oximetry cannot detect CO₂ at all; *what:* PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, bicarbonate). VBG where arterial sampling is difficult (*why:* adequate for pH, bicarbonate and trending CO₂, though unreliable for PaO₂; *what:* pH, bicarbonate). Oxygen saturation with the inspired oxygen concentration documented alongside it (*why:* a saturation figure is meaningless without knowing what oxygen the patient is on — 94% on room air and 94% on 15 litres are entirely different patients, and this is routinely omitted from documentation; *what:* saturation with FiO₂). CXR (*why:* separates the parenchymal causes of Type 1 failure from the clear-lunged causes such as PE and pump failure; *what:* consolidation, oedema, pneumothorax, effusion, hyperinflation). ECG (*why:* right heart strain, arrhythmia, ischaemia; *what:* strain pattern). FBC (*why:* anaemia reduces oxygen content even with a normal saturation; *what:* haemoglobin). Serial FVC where neuromuscular cause is suspected (*why:* gases are normal until abrupt decompensation in this group; *what:* declining FVC — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.5).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen titrated to a target appropriate to the patient. Identify which type of failure and treat accordingly: oxygen and treatment of the lung pathology in Type 1, **ventilatory support** in Type 2.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
NIV for Type 2 failure with acidosis. Invasive ventilation where NIV fails or is inappropriate. Cause-specific treatment.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Assessment for home NIV or long-term oxygen therapy where failure persists — see 0.3.

## 0.2 Acute Respiratory Failure

**D:** Respiratory failure developing over minutes to hours, without time for metabolic compensation.

> [!tip] Cause by anatomical level — the framework that avoids missing things
> **Airway:** obstruction, foreign body, anaphylaxis, epiglottitis — cross-refer [[A2_Airway_Compromise__Stridor_and_Tracheostomy_Emergencies]].
> **Lung:** pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE, pneumothorax, ARDS, asthma, COPD, haemorrhage.
> **Pump — chest wall and muscles:** flail chest, neuromuscular disease, diaphragmatic weakness, massive obesity, tense abdomen.
> **Drive — central:** opioids, sedatives, raised intracranial pressure, brainstem lesion, high cervical cord injury.
> **Circulation:** shock of any cause with inadequate oxygen delivery despite normal lungs.

**A/P:** Any of the above reduces alveolar ventilation, impairs gas exchange, or increases the work of breathing beyond what the respiratory muscles can sustain → **the common final pathway is muscle fatigue**, and fatigue converts a compensating patient into an arresting one over a short interval.

> [!danger] The signs of impending arrest
> Exhaustion. Paradoxical abdominal movement — the abdomen moving inward on inspiration as the fatigued diaphragm is drawn up. Falling respiratory rate in a previously tachypnoeic patient. Reduced conscious state. A silent chest. Rising CO₂. **Any of these means the airway should be secured now rather than after another trial of therapy.**

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, intercostal recession, inability to speak in sentences, agitation then drowsiness, sweating, cyanosis.

**Ix:** ABG (*why:* classifies the failure and identifies the ventilatory failure that oximetry conceals; *what:* PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH). CXR (*why:* the highest-yield single imaging test for the parenchymal causes, and it identifies the pneumothorax that requires immediate decompression; *what:* consolidation, oedema, pneumothorax, collapse). ECG (*why:* arrhythmia, ischaemia and right heart strain; *what:* findings). Bedside lung ultrasound where available (*why:* faster than radiography at the bedside and distinguishes pneumothorax, effusion, consolidation and interstitial oedema without moving the patient; *what:* absent lung sliding, B-lines, consolidation). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* separates cardiogenic from non-cardiogenic causes and identifies RV strain from PE; *what:* LV function, RV dilatation). FBC, UEC, CRP, lactate (*why:* infection, organ dysfunction and perfusion; *what:* leucocytosis, raised lactate). CTPA where PE is suspected and the patient is stable enough (*why:* the diagnostic test; *what:* filling defect). Toxicology and bedside glucose (*why:* opioid toxicity and hypoglycaemia are rapidly reversible causes; *what:* pinpoint pupils, low glucose).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E. Oxygen to target. Sit upright unless contraindicated. Treat the identified cause. **Decompress a tension pneumothorax on clinical grounds without waiting for imaging.** NIV or invasive ventilation as indicated. Escalate early.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific, with ICU involvement where support is required.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Rehabilitation, and reassessment of chronic disease management where the failure was an exacerbation of established disease.

## 0.3 Chronic and Acute-on-Chronic Respiratory Failure

**D:** Persistent inadequate gas exchange, with the acute-on-chronic form being an acute deterioration superimposed on established compensation.

**R/Causes:** COPD · obesity hypoventilation syndrome · neuromuscular disease including motor neurone disease and muscular dystrophy · kyphoscoliosis and chest wall restriction · interstitial lung disease · bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis · central hypoventilation.

**A/P:** Chronic hypercapnia → renal bicarbonate retention over days → pH returns toward normal while CO₂ stays high → the patient functions with a compensated respiratory acidosis and a chronically raised bicarbonate. **A superimposed insult — infection, sedation, excessive oxygen, fluid overload — raises CO₂ faster than compensation can follow**, and the pH falls. Chronic hypoxaemia additionally drives pulmonary vasoconstriction → pulmonary hypertension → right ventricular hypertrophy and failure, which is cor pulmonale.

> [!tip] Recognising acute-on-chronic on a single gas
> A raised CO₂ with a **raised bicarbonate and an acidotic pH** is acute-on-chronic — the bicarbonate proves chronicity, the low pH proves an acute change. This is the patient who benefits most from NIV.

> [!warning] Long-term oxygen therapy has strict criteria and is not symptom relief
> LTOT improves survival in chronic hypoxaemia and is prescribed on measured criteria after a period of stability, not during an exacerbation. **It is not given for breathlessness in a non-hypoxaemic patient**, where it does not help. Smoking while on home oxygen is both a safety hazard and a contraindication. `UNVERIFIED — PaO₂ criteria, the required period of stability, and the daily hours of use required for benefit all need verification against current Australian respiratory guidance and the funding criteria in South Australia.`

> [!info] Home NIV
> Considered in chronic hypercapnic failure from neuromuscular disease, chest wall disease, obesity hypoventilation and selected COPD. Assessment is by a respiratory or sleep service.

**S/Smx:** Chronic exertional dyspnoea, morning headache and daytime somnolence from nocturnal hypercapnia, poor sleep quality, ankle oedema and raised JVP in cor pulmonale, weight loss in advanced COPD, cyanosis.

**Ix:** ABG with bicarbonate (*why:* establishes chronicity and the baseline against which acute changes are judged; *what:* raised CO₂ with raised bicarbonate). Overnight oximetry or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring (*why:* nocturnal hypoventilation precedes daytime hypercapnia and is the point at which home NIV is considered; *what:* nocturnal desaturation, rising CO₂). Spirometry with lung volumes and diffusing capacity (*why:* characterises obstructive versus restrictive physiology and quantifies severity; *what:* FEV1/FVC ratio, reduced volumes, reduced DLCO). Echocardiography (*why:* identifies pulmonary hypertension and right heart strain; *what:* RV dilatation, estimated pulmonary artery pressure). FBC (*why:* secondary polycythaemia from chronic hypoxaemia; *what:* raised haemoglobin and haematocrit). BMI and, where relevant, sleep study (*why:* obesity hypoventilation is common, under-diagnosed and treatable; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index, nocturnal hypoventilation). CXR and CT chest (*why:* defines the underlying structural disease; *what:* hyperinflation, fibrosis, bronchiectasis).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
For acute-on-chronic deterioration: controlled oxygen to target with a repeat gas after any change, bronchodilators, treatment of the precipitant, and **early NIV for hypercapnic acidosis.** Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.2.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Optimise the underlying disease. Assess for home NIV and LTOT once stable.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Smoking cessation, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination, nutritional support, and — importantly — **advance care planning.** Patients with severe chronic respiratory failure will face decisions about intubation, and having that conversation in clinic is far better than having it at three in the morning during an exacerbation.

## 0.4 Respiratory Arrest

**D:** Cessation of effective breathing with a pulse still present.

**A/P:** Ventilation stops → PaO₂ falls and PaCO₂ rises → profound hypoxaemia and acidosis → **bradycardia, then pulseless electrical activity, then asystole.** The interval between respiratory arrest and cardiac arrest is short but real, and intervention within it has a far better outcome than intervention after cardiac arrest has occurred. **This is why respiratory arrest is the most rewarding arrest to recognise** — and why paediatric outcomes hinge on it, since most paediatric cardiac arrest is asphyxial in origin.

> [!tip] The rapidly reversible causes — check these while ventilating
> **Opioid toxicity** — pinpoint pupils, low rate before the arrest. **Hypoglycaemia.** **Airway obstruction** — foreign body, oedema, vomit. **Tension pneumothorax.** **Seizure** with post-ictal apnoea. **High cervical cord injury.** **Severe bronchospasm.** Each has a specific and fast intervention.

> [!danger] Effective bag-mask ventilation is the intervention
> Not intubation, which takes time and skill and can wait. **A two-person bag-mask technique with an oropharyngeal airway, good seal and jaw thrust ventilates most patients**, and the commonest reasons it fails are a poor seal and inadequate airway positioning rather than anything requiring advanced equipment. Practise the two-person technique; it is the skill that matters most here.

**S/Smx:** Absent or agonal breathing with a palpable pulse. Cyanosis. Unresponsiveness. Bradycardia developing.

**Ix:** None before intervention. Once ventilating: bedside glucose (*why:* rapidly reversible; *what:* hypoglycaemia). Waveform capnography (*why:* confirms effective ventilation and airway position; *what:* trace). ABG or VBG (*why:* quantifies the hypoxia and acidosis and guides ongoing support; *what:* pH, pCO₂, pO₂). ECG (*why:* bradycardia progressing toward PEA; *what:* rhythm). CXR after airway placement (*why:* tube position, pneumothorax; *what:* findings). Toxicology and pupil examination (*why:* opioid toxicity is the commonest reversible cause on a ward; *what:* pinpoint pupils).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Call for help. Open the airway, bag-mask ventilate with oxygen, insert an airway adjunct. Check pulse — if absent, this is cardiac arrest and follow [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.4. Bedside glucose. Naloxone for suspected opioid toxicity — cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.5. Needle decompression for tension pneumothorax.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Secure the airway if ventilation cannot be sustained. Treat the cause. ICU.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Root cause review — most in-hospital respiratory arrests follow recognisable deterioration or a prescribing error, and both are preventable. Cross-refer [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.1.

## 0.5 Acute Bronchospasm

**D:** Acute reversible narrowing of the lower airways from smooth muscle constriction.

**R/Causes:** Asthma and COPD exacerbation · **anaphylaxis** · drug-induced — beta-blockers including ophthalmic drops, NSAIDs in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, adenosine, contrast media · aspiration · irritant or smoke inhalation · **peri-anaesthetic bronchospasm** on induction or intubation · cold air and exercise.

**A/P:** Trigger → mast cell degranulation or direct irritant receptor stimulation → bronchial smooth muscle contraction with mucosal oedema and mucus secretion → expiratory flow limitation → air trapping and increased work of breathing.

> [!warning] Bronchospasm is a sign, not a diagnosis
> **Wheeze in a patient with no history of airways disease is not asthma until you have excluded anaphylaxis, pulmonary oedema and foreign body.** "Cardiac asthma" — wheeze from pulmonary oedema — is common in older patients and responds to nitrates and NIV, not to salbutamol. Treating it as asthma delays the correct treatment.

> [!danger] Peri-anaesthetic bronchospasm
> Bronchospasm on induction or shortly after intubation should raise anaphylaxis to a neuromuscular blocking agent or antibiotic, alongside the mechanical causes — tube in a bronchus, tube against the carina, inadequate depth of anaesthesia. It is an anaesthetic emergency and the differential is worth knowing because it appears in exams and in theatre.

> [!warning] Beta-blockers
> Non-selective beta-blockers can precipitate severe bronchospasm in asthma. **Ophthalmic beta-blocker drops for glaucoma are systemically absorbed** and are a classic and frequently overlooked cause — check the eye drops, not just the tablets.

**S/Smx:** Widespread polyphonic expiratory wheeze, prolonged expiration, dyspnoea, chest tightness, cough, accessory muscle use. A silent chest indicates severe obstruction.

**Ix:** Clinical assessment of severity (*why:* determines treatment intensity and disposition faster than any test; *what:* speech, work of breathing, saturation, conscious state). Oxygen saturation (*why:* rapid severity marker, preserved until late; *what:* hypoxia). ABG in severe or deteriorating cases (*why:* identifies the rising CO₂ that signals exhaustion; *what:* normalising or raised pCO₂). CXR where the cause is uncertain or complications suspected (*why:* distinguishes pulmonary oedema, pneumothorax, consolidation and foreign body from primary bronchospasm; *what:* findings). ECG and BNP where cardiac cause is plausible (*why:* separates cardiac asthma from true bronchospasm; *what:* ischaemia, elevated BNP). Serum tryptase where anaphylaxis is possible (*why:* retrospective confirmation of a mast cell event; *what:* acute rise). Medication and eye drop review (*why:* identifies the beta-blocker or NSAID trigger that will otherwise recur; *what:* offending agent).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen. Inhaled short-acting beta-agonist, spacer or nebuliser. Ipratropium in moderate to severe. Systemic corticosteroid. **Adrenaline if anaphylaxis is possible** — cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.9. Escalate to magnesium in severe cases. `UNVERIFIED — all doses from eTG and the Australian Asthma Handbook.`

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the underlying cause and remove the trigger. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.1–0.2.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Document the trigger as an allergy or contraindication where drug-induced. Preventer optimisation and action plan.

## 0.6 Hypoxia

**D:** Inadequate oxygen delivery to tissues. Distinct from **hypoxaemia**, which is low oxygen in arterial blood — a patient can be hypoxic with a normal PaO₂, and this distinction is where the diagnostic value lies.

> [!tip] Four categories of tissue hypoxia
> **Hypoxaemic** — low arterial oxygen. The familiar one.
> **Anaemic** — normal PaO₂ and normal saturation, but insufficient haemoglobin to carry oxygen. A severely anaemic patient can have a saturation of 100% and be profoundly tissue-hypoxic. **Carbon monoxide poisoning belongs here** — the haemoglobin is present but occupied.
> **Stagnant or circulatory** — adequate oxygen content but inadequate flow. Shock, cardiac failure, regional ischaemia.
> **Histotoxic** — oxygen delivered but the cell cannot use it. **Cyanide poisoning**, which classically presents with a high lactate, a high mixed venous oxygen saturation and a normal PaO₂.

> [!danger] Pulse oximetry lies in specific and important situations
> **Carbon monoxide poisoning** — carboxyhaemoglobin absorbs similarly to oxyhaemoglobin, so the oximeter reads **normal or high** while the patient is severely hypoxic. Requires a co-oximeter or specific carboxyhaemoglobin measurement.
> **Methaemoglobinaemia** — the reading tends toward a fixed value in the mid-eighties regardless of actual oxygenation, and the blood is characteristically chocolate-brown and does not turn red on exposure to air.
> **Poor peripheral perfusion, shock, hypothermia, vasopressors** — unreliable or absent trace.
> **Nail polish, artificial nails, dyes** — false readings.
> **Skin pigmentation** — pulse oximeters have been shown to overestimate oxygen saturation in patients with darker skin, meaning occult hypoxaemia can be missed. `UNVERIFIED — confirm the current evidence and any device or practice guidance on this, as it is an active area and the magnitude of the effect varies between devices.`

**A/P:** Oxygen delivery is the product of cardiac output and arterial oxygen content, and arterial oxygen content is dominated by **haemoglobin concentration and its saturation**, with dissolved oxygen contributing very little. This is why transfusing an anaemic patient can improve oxygen delivery far more than increasing FiO₂, and why a saturation number alone is an incomplete picture.

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, tachycardia, restlessness and confusion — **agitation is an early sign of hypoxia and is frequently mistaken for anxiety or non-compliance.** Cyanosis is late, requires a certain absolute quantity of deoxygenated haemoglobin, and therefore **may never appear in a severely anaemic patient** while appearing readily in a polycythaemic one.

**Ix:** ABG with co-oximetry (*why:* measures true oxygen content, carboxyhaemoglobin and methaemoglobin, all of which standard oximetry misses; *what:* PaO₂, COHb, MetHb). FBC (*why:* anaemic hypoxia is invisible on a saturation reading; *what:* haemoglobin). Lactate (*why:* the marker of tissue-level oxygen failure regardless of which category caused it; *what:* elevation, clearance). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* identifies the stagnant category — inadequate flow with adequate content; *what:* contractility, filling). CXR (*why:* parenchymal causes; *what:* consolidation, oedema). ECG (*why:* ischaemia both causes and results from hypoxia; *what:* ischaemic change). Carboxyhaemoglobin specifically where there is any suggestion of smoke or fume exposure, or multiple household members with headache (*why:* it is undetectable on standard oximetry and easily missed; *what:* elevated level).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen, titrated to a target — recognising that supplemental oxygen does not correct anaemic, stagnant or histotoxic hypoxia, and that **failure to respond to oxygen should redirect the diagnosis rather than prompt more oxygen.** High-flow oxygen is nonetheless correct in carbon monoxide poisoning, where it accelerates carboxyhaemoglobin clearance.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Category-specific: treat the lung, transfuse, restore circulation, or give the specific antidote.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Address the underlying cause and reassess oxygen requirement once stable.

## 0.7 Tachypnoea

**D:** Respiratory rate above the normal range for age.

> [!danger] The most predictive and least reliably recorded observation
> Respiratory rate rises earlier than heart rate, blood pressure or saturation in almost every form of deterioration, and it is the observation most often estimated rather than counted. **Count it for a full minute**, ideally while appearing to take the pulse, since patients who know they are being watched breathe differently. An accurately recorded respiratory rate is one of the most useful things an intern contributes.

**A/P:** Chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors drive respiratory rate in response to hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, and afferent input from lung stretch and irritant receptors. **A raised respiratory rate is therefore not a respiratory sign — it is a systemic one**, and the commonest non-respiratory causes are metabolic acidosis and sepsis.

> [!tip] Differential — think beyond the lungs
> **Respiratory:** pneumonia, PE, pulmonary oedema, asthma, COPD, pneumothorax, effusion.
> **Metabolic:** any metabolic acidosis — DKA, lactic acidosis, renal failure, salicylate. Deep sighing **Kussmaul** breathing is characteristic. Cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.1.
> **Circulatory:** sepsis, shock, anaemia, heart failure.
> **Neurological:** pain, anxiety, brainstem lesion, raised intracranial pressure.
> **Other:** fever, pregnancy, thyrotoxicosis, drugs including salicylate and stimulants.

> [!warning] Do not diagnose anxiety-related hyperventilation as a first move
> It is a diagnosis of exclusion. PE, DKA, sepsis and salicylate toxicity all present with tachypnoea in a distressed patient, and all have been missed by attributing the breathing to panic.

**S/Smx:** Increased rate, with the pattern often informative — deep and sighing in metabolic acidosis, shallow and rapid in restrictive or painful conditions, laboured with accessory muscle use in obstruction.

**Ix:** Counted respiratory rate over a full minute (*why:* the estimate is unreliable and the trend across the observation chart is the most predictive information available; *what:* rate and trend). VBG or ABG with lactate (*why:* distinguishes respiratory from metabolic drive, which is the first branch in the differential; *what:* acidosis with a low CO₂ indicates compensation for a metabolic problem). Bedside glucose and ketones (*why:* DKA presents as tachypnoea, sometimes without any respiratory symptoms at all; *what:* hyperglycaemia with ketosis). Oxygen saturation on documented FiO₂ (*why:* separates hypoxaemic from non-hypoxaemic causes; *what:* saturation with inspired oxygen). CXR (*why:* parenchymal and pleural causes; *what:* findings). FBC, UEC, CRP (*why:* anaemia, renal failure, infection; *what:* haemoglobin, creatinine, inflammatory markers). D-dimer or CTPA where PE is plausible (*why:* PE frequently presents with isolated tachypnoea and a clear chest radiograph; *what:* filling defect). Salicylate level where the picture is a mixed acid-base disorder (*why:* easily missed; *what:* level — cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.8).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess for the causes above rather than treating the number. Oxygen if hypoxaemic. Escalate if the rate is rising.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Where the cause is genuinely anxiety or dysfunctional breathing, breathing retraining and psychological therapy — after organic causes have been excluded.

## 0.8 Bradypnoea

**D:** Respiratory rate below the normal range for age.

**R/Causes:** **Opioids and sedatives** including benzodiazepines and gabapentinoids · raised intracranial pressure · brainstem lesion or stroke · hypothyroidism and myxoedema · hypothermia · severe hypercapnia with CO₂ narcosis · high cervical cord injury · neuromuscular disease · post-ictal state · **exhaustion in a previously tachypnoeic patient.**

> [!danger] A falling respiratory rate in a distressed patient is exhaustion, not improvement
> The asthmatic whose rate falls from 40 to 20 while remaining distressed and drowsy is not getting better. **This is the single most dangerous misreading of an observation chart**, because the number moves toward normal while the patient moves toward arrest. Interpret respiratory rate alongside work of breathing, conscious state and CO₂ — never in isolation.

> [!warning] Bradypnoea on a ward is opioid toxicity until proven otherwise
> Check the medication chart, look at the pupils, and remember that **saturation on supplemental oxygen can remain normal in a patient who is dangerously hypercapnic.** Sedation score and respiratory rate are the monitoring parameters. Cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.5.

**A/P:** Reduced respiratory drive or an inability to execute it → falling minute ventilation → hypercapnia → CO₂ narcosis further reduces drive → a self-reinforcing loop terminating in apnoea.

**S/Smx:** Slow shallow breathing, drowsiness, small pupils in opioid toxicity, warm flushed peripheries and bounding pulse in hypercapnia, asterixis, and eventually apnoea.

**Ix:** ABG or VBG (*why:* the definitive test — quantifies hypercapnia, which oximetry cannot detect and which supplemental oxygen conceals; *what:* raised pCO₂ with respiratory acidosis). Bedside glucose (*why:* reversible mimic; *what:* hypoglycaemia). Pupil examination and medication chart review (*why:* opioid toxicity is the commonest ward cause and the answer is usually written on the chart; *what:* pinpoint pupils, cumulative opioid and sedative dose). Non-contrast CT brain where there is any focal sign or unexplained persistence (*why:* raised intracranial pressure and brainstem lesions cause bradypnoea and are not survivable to miss; *what:* haemorrhage, mass, hydrocephalus). TSH and core temperature (*why:* myxoedema coma and hypothermia both cause bradypnoea and both are reversible; *what:* raised TSH, low temperature). UEC and calcium (*why:* metabolic causes of reduced conscious state; *what:* derangement).

### 0.8.1 Mx – Immediate
Airway positioning, oxygen, and **bag-mask ventilation if ventilation is inadequate — ventilate before reaching for a reversal agent.** Bedside glucose. Naloxone titrated to adequate respiration where opioid toxicity is likely, with the caveats in [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.5. Escalate.

### 0.8.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the cause. Where hypercapnia is the driver, ventilatory support rather than reversal.

### 0.8.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Review and adjust sedative and opioid prescribing. Document the event. Cross-refer [[F0-4_Resuscitation_Algorithms_and_Emergency_Procedures]] 0.9 for opioid stewardship.
