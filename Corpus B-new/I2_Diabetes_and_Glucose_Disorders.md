## 0.1 Diagnosis and Classification

> [!tip] The diagnostic tests, and the rule that governs them
> Diabetes is diagnosed by **fasting plasma glucose, a two-hour value on oral glucose tolerance testing, HbA1c, or a random glucose in the presence of classic symptoms.**
> **In an ASYMPTOMATIC person, a single abnormal result is not sufficient — it must be confirmed on a separate occasion**, ideally with the same test.
> **In a SYMPTOMATIC person with an unequivocally raised random glucose, no confirmation is needed.**
> `UNVERIFIED — all diagnostic threshold values, and the separate thresholds defining impaired fasting glucose and impaired glucose tolerance.`

> [!danger] HbA1c is unreliable in several common situations
> It reflects average glycaemia over the lifespan of the red cell, so **anything that alters red cell turnover distorts it:**
> **· Anaemia of any cause, haemolysis, recent transfusion, and haemoglobinopathies** (thalassaemia and haemoglobin variants — relevant in Australia's diverse population).
> **· Chronic kidney disease** and erythropoietin therapy.
> **· Pregnancy.**
> **· Rapidly evolving hyperglycaemia — as in new type 1 diabetes — where HbA1c lags behind the actual glucose and can be misleadingly reassuring in a patient who is about to develop DKA.**
> **In these situations use glucose-based testing.**

> [!info] Classification — and why getting it right matters
> **· Type 1** — autoimmune beta cell destruction. **Antibodies (GAD, IA-2, ZnT8) positive, C-peptide low, ketosis-prone, requires insulin from diagnosis.**
> **· Type 2** — insulin resistance with progressive relative insulin deficiency.
> **· Gestational** — see 0.7.
> **· Other specific types** — **pancreatic (type 3c: chronic pancreatitis, pancreatectomy, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis — cross-refer [[C7_Pancreatobiliary_Disease]] 0.3), endocrinopathies (Cushing syndrome, acromegaly, phaeochromocytoma), drug-induced (corticosteroids, antipsychotics, immunosuppressants), and monogenic diabetes.**

> [!danger] Two forms adults are misclassified as
> **· LADA (latent autoimmune diabetes in adults)** — **adult onset, often lean, initially appearing to be type 2, but antibody-positive with progressive beta cell failure and early failure of oral agents.** **Suspect it in an adult diagnosed with "type 2" who is not overweight, has other autoimmune disease, or deteriorates rapidly despite treatment.** **Check antibodies and C-peptide.** Misclassification leads to DKA in someone who was thought not to need insulin.
> **· MODY (maturely-onset diabetes of the young)** — **young onset (often under 25), a strong family history across multiple generations, non-obese, antibody-negative, and not ketosis-prone.** It is monogenic. **Some subtypes respond very well to low-dose sulfonylureas rather than insulin, and one subtype needs no treatment at all** — so the diagnosis genuinely changes management and has implications for the whole family. It is frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 and the patient placed on insulin for years.
> **The practical rule: if the diabetes does not fit the expected phenotype, question the classification.**

> [!warning] The Australian context
> **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people experience substantially higher diabetes prevalence, onset at younger ages, and higher rates of complications**, which is why **screening begins at a younger age in this population.** Cross-refer [[AU1_Australian_Health_Context_and_ATSI_Health]] and [[GER3_Preventive_and_Occupational_Health]].
> **The National Diabetes Services Scheme (NDSS)** provides subsidised access to consumables — test strips, needles, and continuous glucose monitoring for eligible groups. **Registering a newly diagnosed patient is a concrete practical action**, and it is frequently overlooked at diagnosis. `UNVERIFIED — current NDSS eligibility and what is subsidised.`

**S/Smx:** Classic osmotic symptoms — **polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, blurred vision, fatigue, recurrent infections including candidiasis** — or asymptomatic detection on screening. **Type 1 presents more acutely, often with DKA; type 2 is frequently present for years before diagnosis, which is why complications may be present at the time of diagnosis.**

**Ix:** **Fasting glucose, HbA1c, or OGTT** (*why:* the diagnostic tests, with confirmation required in asymptomatic people; *what:* values against threshold). **Islet autoantibodies (GAD, IA-2, ZnT8) and C-peptide where classification is uncertain** (*why:* distinguishes type 1 and LADA from type 2, and misclassification risks DKA; *what:* positive antibodies, low C-peptide). **Ketones where hyperglycaemia is marked or the patient is unwell** (*why:* identifies impending or actual DKA — cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.3; *what:* beta-hydroxybutyrate). At diagnosis of type 2, a **complication and comorbidity screen — UEC and eGFR, urine albumin-creatinine ratio, lipids, LFT, blood pressure, retinal screening and foot examination** (*why:* **complications are frequently already present at diagnosis in type 2 because of the preceding asymptomatic period**; *what:* baseline complication status). TFTs and coeliac serology in type 1 (*why:* associated autoimmune conditions cluster; *what:* associated disease). Genetic testing where MODY is suspected (*why:* changes treatment and has family implications; *what:* pathogenic variant).

## 0.2 Type 2 Diabetes — Management

> [!danger] Individualise the glycaemic target — tight control is not universally good
> **A tighter target is appropriate in: younger patients, newly diagnosed disease, long life expectancy, no established cardiovascular disease, and where it can be achieved without hypoglycaemia.**
> **A looser target is appropriate in: older and frail patients, limited life expectancy, established complications, hypoglycaemia unawareness, and where treatment burden outweighs benefit.**
> **Over-treating an elderly frail patient causes hypoglycaemia, falls, fractures and death, and the benefit of tight control accrues over a timeframe they may not have.** **Deintensifying diabetes treatment in the elderly is an active and appropriate intervention**, not neglect. Cross-refer [[GER1_Comprehensive_Geriatric_Assessment]] 0.4.
> `UNVERIFIED — the HbA1c targets for each group.`

> [!tip] Lifestyle first, and remission is now a realistic goal
> **Substantial weight loss can induce remission of type 2 diabetes**, particularly early in the disease — through intensive dietary intervention or bariatric surgery. **This is a genuine change in how the disease is framed: it is not necessarily lifelong and progressive.**
> Alongside: physical activity (both aerobic and resistance), smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, and sleep — **including treating obstructive sleep apnoea, which is very common and worsens glycaemic control.**

> [!info] Pharmacotherapy — and the shift from "treat the number" to "treat the patient"
> **· Metformin — first-line for most.** Reduces hepatic glucose output. **Practical points: gastrointestinal side effects (start low, take with food, extended-release helps) · long-term use causes VITAMIN B12 DEFICIENCY, which should be checked periodically and is a genuinely missed cause of neuropathy in diabetics · withhold in acute illness, before contrast, and with significant renal impairment.** Lactic acidosis is rare but occurs with accumulation — cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.5.
> **· SGLT2 inhibitors — now prescribed for cardiovascular and renal protection, independent of glycaemic need.** **Indicated in established cardiovascular disease, heart failure and chronic kidney disease regardless of HbA1c.** Cross-refer [[CV-X_Chronic_Heart_Failure]] 0.3 and [[H3_Acute_Kidney_Injury_and_CKD]].
> **Risks to counsel: euglycaemic DKA (cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.3), genital mycotic infection, volume depletion, and rare Fournier gangrene.** **Sick day rules and perioperative withholding are essential.**
> **· GLP-1 receptor agonists** — weight loss and cardiovascular benefit. Gastrointestinal side effects are common. **They delay gastric emptying, and there is emerging concern about aspiration risk during anaesthesia and endoscopy, prompting recommendations to withhold before procedures.** `UNVERIFIED — the withholding recommendations, which are recent and evolving.`
> **· Sulfonylureas** — effective and cheap, but **cause hypoglycaemia, which is prolonged and dangerous in the elderly and in renal impairment.** Longer-acting agents are worse.
> **· DPP-4 inhibitors** — weight-neutral, low hypoglycaemia risk, modest efficacy.
> **· Pioglitazone** — fluid retention; **avoid in heart failure.**
> **· Insulin** — when other agents are insufficient, or in acute illness and marked hyperglycaemia.
> `UNVERIFIED — all agents, doses, sequencing and PBS criteria.`

> [!warning] Choose the agent by comorbidity, not just by HbA1c
> **This is the central modern principle.** A patient with heart failure or chronic kidney disease should be on an SGLT2 inhibitor because of the organ protection, **even if their glucose is already at target.** A patient whose main problem is obesity benefits from a GLP-1 agonist. A frail elderly patient at risk of hypoglycaemia should not be on a sulfonylurea.
> **The old approach of adding agents in a fixed sequence to chase a number has been superseded.**

**Ix:** **HbA1c at intervals determined by stability and treatment change** (*why:* monitors control, with the caveats in 0.1; *what:* trend). Blood pressure at every visit (*why:* the intervention with the largest absolute benefit for complications; *what:* control). **Lipids** (*why:* cardiovascular risk management; *what:* profile). **UEC, eGFR and urine albumin-creatinine ratio annually** (*why:* detects nephropathy at the albuminuria stage when it is modifiable; *what:* albuminuria, declining eGFR). **B12 in patients on long-term metformin** (*why:* deficiency causes neuropathy that is otherwise attributed to diabetes itself; *what:* low B12). LFT (*why:* metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is very common; *what:* transaminitis). **Retinal screening and foot examination at the recommended intervals** (*why:* both detect asymptomatic disease at a treatable stage; *what:* retinopathy, neuropathy, ischaemia). Assessment for sleep apnoea and depression (*why:* both are common, both worsen control, and both are treatable; *what:* symptoms).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Only for hyperglycaemic emergencies — cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]].

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Individualised targets, lifestyle intervention, and pharmacotherapy chosen by comorbidity.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**The annual cycle of care in Australian general practice** — HbA1c, blood pressure, lipids, kidney function and albuminuria, eyes, feet, weight, smoking, physical activity, medication review, immunisation, and psychological wellbeing. **Diabetes educator, dietitian and podiatry referral, and NDSS registration.**

## 0.3 Type 1 Diabetes — Management

> [!tip] The regimen
> **Basal-bolus (multiple daily injections)** — a long-acting basal insulin plus rapid-acting boluses with meals, with **carbohydrate counting**, an insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio, and a correction factor.
> **Insulin pumps and hybrid closed-loop systems** — increasingly standard, particularly in younger patients, automating basal delivery in response to continuous glucose data.
> **Continuous or flash glucose monitoring** has largely replaced fingerprick testing for many patients. **"Time in range" is now used alongside HbA1c as a measure of control**, and it captures variability and hypoglycaemia in a way that HbA1c cannot — a patient with a good HbA1c achieved by swinging between hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia is not well controlled. **CGM is NDSS-subsidised for eligible groups in Australia.** `UNVERIFIED — eligibility.`

> [!danger] Never omit basal insulin — including when the patient is not eating
> **This is the single most important inpatient point in type 1 diabetes.**
> **A person with type 1 diabetes produces NO endogenous insulin. Basal insulin is required continuously to suppress ketogenesis, regardless of oral intake.**
> **Withholding basal insulin because the patient is nil by mouth, fasting for a procedure, or vomiting causes DKA** — and it happens on wards regularly, because the reasoning "they're not eating so they don't need insulin" is intuitive and wrong.
> **Bolus insulin is adjusted or withheld with meals. Basal insulin continues.** Cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.3.

> [!warning] Sick day management — teach it, and check they remember it
> **· Never stop insulin.** Requirements usually **increase** during illness because of counter-regulatory hormones.
> **· Monitor glucose more frequently, and CHECK KETONES** — every person with type 1 diabetes should have ketone testing available and know when to use it.
> **· Maintain carbohydrate and fluid intake**, using sugary fluids if solid food is not tolerated.
> **· Know when to seek help** — persistent vomiting, rising ketones, inability to keep fluids down, or drowsiness.
> **This education prevents DKA admissions**, and it should be revisited rather than given once at diagnosis.

> [!warning] The psychological dimension, which is substantial and under-addressed
> **Diabetes distress** — the emotional burden of relentless self-management — is common, distinct from depression, and responds to different support.
> **Disordered eating and deliberate insulin omission for weight control ("diabulimia")** occurs, particularly in young women, and causes recurrent DKA and accelerated complications. **Recurrent DKA in a young person should prompt gentle exploration of insulin omission, psychological factors and social circumstances rather than repeated protocol-driven admissions.** Cross-refer [[N8_Eating_Disorders]].
> **Transition from paediatric to adult services is a high-risk period** for disengagement and deterioration, and needs active management rather than a referral letter.

**Ix:** HbA1c and **time in range from CGM data** (*why:* HbA1c alone conceals variability and hypoglycaemia; *what:* both metrics). Ketones during illness or hyperglycaemia (*why:* DKA prevention; *what:* beta-hydroxybutyrate). **Screening for associated autoimmune disease — thyroid function, coeliac serology, and consideration of adrenal insufficiency** (*why:* they cluster with type 1 diabetes, and coeliac disease and Addison disease both cause unexplained hypoglycaemia and poor control; *what:* associated conditions). Complication screening as per 0.5–0.6. **Psychological screening** (*why:* diabetes distress, depression and disordered eating are common and materially affect outcomes; *what:* symptoms).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
DKA management — cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.3.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Basal-bolus or pump therapy with structured education, carbohydrate counting and CGM.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Multidisciplinary care, complication screening, psychological support, and **driving and occupational advice** — cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.6.

## 0.4 Hypoglycaemia

**D:** Defined by **Whipple triad — symptoms consistent with hypoglycaemia, a low measured plasma glucose, and resolution of symptoms when the glucose is corrected.** `UNVERIFIED — the glucose thresholds used to define and grade hypoglycaemia.`

> [!tip] The symptoms occur in a physiological sequence
> **· Autonomic first (as counter-regulation activates): sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, anxiety, pallor.** These are the **warning symptoms.**
> **· Neuroglycopenic second (as the brain runs out of substrate): confusion, difficulty concentrating, slurred speech, behavioural change, drowsiness, seizure, coma, focal neurological deficit mimicking stroke.**
> **Hypoglycaemia can produce a focal deficit and is a stroke mimic — which is why glucose is checked in every suspected stroke.** Cross-refer [[D3_Stroke_and_Focal_Neurological_Deficit]] 0.1.

> [!danger] Hypoglycaemia unawareness — and the fact that it is reversible
> **Recurrent hypoglycaemia blunts the counter-regulatory response, so the autonomic warning symptoms are lost and the first manifestation becomes neuroglycopenia — confusion or collapse without warning.**
> Contributors: **recurrent hypoglycaemia, long diabetes duration, autonomic neuropathy, and beta-blockers** (which mask the adrenergic symptoms, though sweating is preserved).
> **Crucially, it is REVERSIBLE — strict avoidance of hypoglycaemia for a period restores awareness.** This means the correct response to unawareness is to **raise the glycaemic targets deliberately** for weeks to months, not to push harder for control.
> **Hypoglycaemia unawareness has major driving implications** and must be reported — cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.6.

> [!warning] Causes in a person with diabetes
> **Too much insulin or sulfonylurea (dose error, wrong insulin, injection into a lipohypertrophic site with erratic absorption) · missed or delayed meal · exercise (which can cause delayed hypoglycaemia many hours later) · ALCOHOL (which inhibits gluconeogenesis, and the hypoglycaemia may be delayed and prolonged and is easily mistaken for intoxication) · weight loss · gastroparesis with mismatched insulin timing · renal or hepatic impairment reducing drug clearance · new adrenal insufficiency or coeliac disease · and drug interactions.**
> **A patient whose insulin requirement suddenly falls has a reason** — weight loss, renal decline, coeliac disease, adrenal insufficiency, or resolving infection.

> [!danger] Hypoglycaemia in a person WITHOUT diabetes always needs explanation
> **· Critical illness — sepsis, liver failure, renal failure, cardiac failure, malnutrition.**
> **· Adrenal insufficiency** — cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.6.
> **· Alcohol**, particularly with poor nutrition.
> **· Drugs** — quinine, quinolones, tramadol, beta-blockers, and **inadvertent or deliberate administration of a sulfonylurea or insulin.**
> **· Insulinoma** — fasting hypoglycaemia with inappropriately raised insulin.
> **· Post-bariatric surgery** — late postprandial hypoglycaemia from exaggerated incretin response.
> **· Non-islet cell tumour hypoglycaemia** (IGF-2 secreting).
> **· Factitious or malicious administration.**

> [!tip] The C-peptide discriminates the cause
> **When investigating hypoglycaemia, take the samples DURING the hypoglycaemic episode — insulin, C-peptide, pro-insulin, beta-hydroxybutyrate and a sulfonylurea screen — before treating.** Afterwards they are uninterpretable.
> **· High insulin with HIGH C-peptide = ENDOGENOUS insulin → insulinoma, OR a sulfonylurea** (which stimulates endogenous secretion) — **hence the sulfonylurea screen, which distinguishes the two and is frequently forgotten.**
> **· High insulin with LOW C-peptide = EXOGENOUS insulin** → therapeutic, factitious or malicious administration. **C-peptide is cleaved from proinsulin in equimolar amounts with endogenous insulin, and is absent from manufactured insulin — which is precisely why it works as the discriminator.**
> **· Low insulin with low C-peptide and raised ketones = an appropriate response** → the cause is not hyperinsulinaemic: think adrenal insufficiency, liver failure, starvation, or critical illness.
> A supervised **72-hour fast** is the formal test for insulinoma.

> [!danger] Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia is prolonged and recurrent
> **Do not treat a sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycaemia, see the glucose normalise, and discharge the patient.** The drug's effect long outlasts the glucose bolus, and hypoglycaemia recurs — sometimes fatally.
> **These patients require admission, a glucose infusion, prolonged observation, and consideration of octreotide** to suppress the sulfonylurea-driven insulin release.
> This is a recurring and preventable error, particularly with long-acting agents in older patients with renal impairment.

> [!warning] Treatment, and two important caveats
> **· Conscious and able to swallow:** rapid-acting oral carbohydrate, **followed by longer-acting carbohydrate** to prevent recurrence.
> **· Unconscious or unable to swallow:** intravenous glucose, or **intramuscular glucagon.**
> **Glucagon works by mobilising hepatic glycogen — so it FAILS in patients with depleted glycogen stores: those with alcohol-related hypoglycaemia, liver failure, prolonged starvation, or malnutrition.** In those patients, glucose is required.
> **· Give thiamine before or with glucose in the alcohol-dependent or malnourished**, to avoid precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy. Cross-refer [[C2_Nausea_and_Vomiting]] 0.7.

**Ix:** **Bedside glucose immediately, and formal laboratory glucose** (*why:* confirms and quantifies; *what:* low glucose). **Insulin, C-peptide, pro-insulin, beta-hydroxybutyrate and sulfonylurea screen — collected DURING the episode** (*why:* the only time they are interpretable, and the C-peptide relationship identifies the mechanism; *what:* the pattern above). **Morning cortisol or short synacthen test** (*why:* adrenal insufficiency causes hypoglycaemia and is frequently missed; *what:* low cortisol). UEC, LFT (*why:* renal and hepatic impairment reduce drug clearance and gluconeogenesis; *what:* organ dysfunction). Coeliac serology and TFTs in a person with type 1 diabetes and new hypoglycaemia (*why:* both reduce insulin requirement and are treatable; *what:* associated disease). Review of the insulin regimen, injection technique and injection sites (*why:* **lipohypertrophy at overused injection sites causes erratic absorption and unpredictable hypoglycaemia — and it is found by examining the sites, which is rarely done**; *what:* lipohypertrophy). Imaging for insulinoma where biochemically confirmed (*why:* localisation for surgery; *what:* pancreatic lesion).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat as above. **Take the diagnostic bloods first if the patient is stable and the cause is unexplained.** **Admit sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia.**

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Adjust the regimen, treat the underlying cause, or remove the offending drug.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Restore hypoglycaemia awareness by deliberately relaxing targets.** Education, CGM with alarms, glucagon supply for household members, medical alert identification, and **driving advice** — cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.6.

## 0.5 Microvascular Complications

> [!tip] Retinopathy
> Progresses from **non-proliferative (microaneurysms, dot and blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton wool spots) to proliferative (neovascularisation, with the risk of vitreous haemorrhage and tractional retinal detachment).**
> **Diabetic MACULOPATHY — macular oedema — is the commonest cause of vision loss in type 2 diabetes**, and it can occur at any stage of retinopathy, including early. **It threatens central vision and is treated with intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy.**
> **Screening is by dilated fundoscopy or retinal photography at defined intervals**, and it is asymptomatic until advanced — **which is the entire justification for screening a patient who reports normal vision.** `UNVERIFIED — screening intervals for type 1, type 2 and pregnancy.`
> **Rapid improvement in glycaemic control can transiently WORSEN retinopathy** — an important and counterintuitive point. It matters when intensifying treatment in someone with longstanding poor control, and in pregnancy, and it is a reason for retinal assessment before and during intensification rather than a reason not to improve control.

> [!tip] Nephropathy
> Progresses from **albuminuria (initially moderately increased, formerly "microalbuminuria") to declining eGFR.**
> **Screen annually with a urine albumin-creatinine ratio AND eGFR** — **either can be abnormal first, and checking only creatinine misses the albuminuric phase when intervention is most effective.**
> **Management: ACE inhibitor or ARB (but not both), blood pressure control, glycaemic control, and — now core — an SGLT2 INHIBITOR, which has substantial renal protective effect independent of glucose lowering.** Non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists have an emerging role. Cross-refer [[H3_Acute_Kidney_Injury_and_CKD]].
> **A rise in creatinine after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB is expected and acceptable within limits — it is not a reason to stop.** Cross-refer [[CV-X_Chronic_Heart_Failure]] 0.3.

> [!warning] Neuropathy
> **· Distal symmetrical polyneuropathy** — the commonest, glove-and-stocking, and **the loss of protective sensation is what costs feet.** Cross-refer [[D4_Weakness__Neuropathy_and_Radiculopathy]] 0.2.
> **· Autonomic neuropathy** — and it is under-recognised: **gastroparesis** (cross-refer [[C2_Nausea_and_Vomiting]] 0.4), **postural hypotension**, erectile dysfunction, bladder dysfunction, gustatory sweating, **hypoglycaemia unawareness**, and **SILENT MYOCARDIAL ISCHAEMIA — which is why a diabetic with unexplained breathlessness, fatigue or vomiting warrants an ECG.**
> **· Mononeuropathies** — cranial nerve palsies, classically a **pupil-sparing third nerve palsy** (cross-refer [[D7_Cranial_Nerves_and_Special_Senses]] 0.3), and entrapment neuropathies.
> **· Diabetic amyotrophy** — proximal thigh pain and wasting, often asymmetric, with weight loss.
> **· And remember metformin-associated B12 deficiency as a treatable contributor** — see 0.2.

**Ix:** **Dilated retinal examination or retinal photography at screening intervals** (*why:* asymptomatic and treatable; *what:* retinopathy grade, maculopathy). **Urine ACR and eGFR annually** (*why:* detects nephropathy at the modifiable stage; *what:* albuminuria, eGFR trend). **Monofilament testing and vibration sense** (*why:* identifies loss of protective sensation, which determines foot risk category; *what:* absent protective sensation). **B12** on long-term metformin (*why:* a treatable cause of neuropathy attributed to diabetes; *what:* deficiency). Postural blood pressure (*why:* autonomic neuropathy, and it contributes to falls; *what:* postural drop). ECG (*why:* silent ischaemia; *what:* ischaemic change).

## 0.6 Macrovascular Risk and the Diabetic Foot

> [!warning] Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in diabetes
> Management is **the whole cardiovascular risk package, not just glucose**: **blood pressure control, statin therapy, smoking cessation, and antiplatelet therapy where there is established cardiovascular disease.**
> **Glycaemic control has a much larger effect on MICROvascular than on MACROvascular complications** — which is why blood pressure and lipid management deliver more cardiovascular benefit than intensifying glucose control does. Cross-refer [[B2_Hypertension_Spectrum]] 0.1 and [[GER3_Preventive_and_Occupational_Health]].

> [!danger] The diabetic foot — three problems that combine
> **NEUROPATHY (loss of protective sensation) + ISCHAEMIA (peripheral arterial disease) + INFECTION.**
> **The neuropathy is what makes it dangerous, because the injury is PAINLESS** — a stone in the shoe, a new pair of shoes, a burn from a heater, or a nail cut too short goes unnoticed until an ulcer is established.
> **Annual foot examination: sensation (monofilament), pulses, deformity, callus, skin condition, and FOOTWEAR — including looking inside the shoes.**
> **Ulcer management requires offloading, debridement, infection management, revascularisation assessment, and a multidisciplinary foot service.** **Probe-to-bone suggests osteomyelitis.**
> **The strongest predictor of amputation is a previous amputation** — the contralateral limb is at high risk and needs active protection. Cross-refer [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.5–0.6 and [[D4_Weakness__Neuropathy_and_Radiculopathy]] 0.2.

> [!danger] Charcot neuroarthropathy — the hot foot that is not cellulitis
> **A hot, swollen, erythematous foot in a person with diabetic neuropathy, often WITHOUT an ulcer and often with little or no pain.**
> **It is routinely misdiagnosed as cellulitis, gout or a sprain**, treated with antibiotics or elevation, and the patient keeps walking on it — **which destroys the architecture of the foot permanently, producing the rocker-bottom deformity and a limb at high risk of ulceration and amputation.**
> **The treatment is IMMEDIATE OFFLOADING and non-weight-bearing, with urgent referral.** **A hot swollen foot in a neuropathic diabetic should be assumed to be Charcot until proven otherwise, and the patient taken off it.**

> [!tip] The other comorbidities worth screening for
> **· Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)** — very common, and can progress to fibrosis and cirrhosis. Cross-refer [[C3_Jaundice_and_Liver_Disease]] 0.5.
> **· Obstructive sleep apnoea** — common, worsens control, and treatable.
> **· Depression** — twice as common in diabetes, worsens self-management and outcomes.
> **· Erectile dysfunction** — common, distressing, rarely volunteered, and **a marker of vascular disease that should prompt cardiovascular assessment.**
> **· Periodontal disease** — bidirectional relationship with glycaemic control; **dental review is a legitimate part of diabetes care and is almost never mentioned.**
> **· Increased infection risk** — including candidiasis, urinary tract infection, skin and soft tissue infection, and **specific severe infections such as emphysematous pyelonephritis, malignant otitis externa and mucormycosis.**

**Ix:** Blood pressure, lipids, and absolute cardiovascular risk assessment (*why:* these deliver more macrovascular benefit than glucose control; *what:* risk category). **Annual foot assessment with monofilament, pulses and footwear inspection** (*why:* stratifies risk and detects the asymptomatic at-risk foot; *what:* neuropathy, ischaemia, deformity). **Ankle-brachial index, with toe pressures where calcification is likely** (*why:* **medial calcification in diabetes makes the ABI falsely normal or high** — cross-refer [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.5; *what:* perfusion). Imaging for osteomyelitis where an ulcer probes to bone or fails to heal (*why:* changes duration and may require surgery; *what:* bone involvement on MRI). LFT and liver fibrosis assessment (*why:* MASLD; *what:* transaminitis, fibrosis score). Sleep study and depression screening where indicated (*why:* both common and treatable; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index, depressive symptoms).

## 0.7 Diabetes in Special Situations

> [!danger] Pregnancy — pre-existing diabetes
> **Preconception planning is the intervention that matters most**, because organogenesis is complete before many women present.
> **· Optimise glycaemic control BEFORE conception** — the risk of congenital malformation relates to periconceptional HbA1c.
> **· High-dose folic acid.**
> **· STOP teratogenic and contraindicated medications — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, statins**, and review other agents.
> **· Retinal assessment before and during pregnancy** — retinopathy can progress rapidly in pregnancy.
> **· Insulin requirements change markedly through pregnancy** and fall abruptly after delivery.

> [!tip] Gestational diabetes
> **Screened for with an oral glucose tolerance test at a defined gestation**, with earlier testing in high-risk women. `UNVERIFIED — timing, thresholds and risk criteria, which differ between guidelines and have been contested.`
> **Risks: macrosomia and shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, polyhydramnios, pre-eclampsia, and increased caesarean rates.**
> **Management: dietary and lifestyle intervention first, then metformin or insulin.**
> **The point most often missed: a postpartum OGTT is required, and these women carry a substantially increased LIFELONG risk of type 2 diabetes and need ongoing periodic screening and lifestyle intervention.** Gestational diabetes is a window into future risk, and the follow-up is frequently not arranged. Cross-refer [[O2_Later_Pregnancy_and_Fetal]].

> [!danger] The hospital inpatient — where the errors happen
> **· NEVER stop basal insulin in type 1 diabetes** — see 0.3.
> **· "Sliding scale" correction insulin ALONE is inadequate** — it treats hyperglycaemia after it has occurred rather than preventing it, and it produces oscillation. **Use a basal-bolus regimen with correction doses.**
> **· Steroid-induced hyperglycaemia** — corticosteroids cause a characteristic **predominantly postprandial and afternoon/evening hyperglycaemia**, so morning fasting glucose can look deceptively normal. **Check afternoon or pre-dinner glucose in patients on steroids**, and anticipate it when prescribing them.
> **· Enteral feeding** requires an insulin regimen matched to the feed, and **an interrupted feed with the insulin still running causes hypoglycaemia.**
> **· Hyperglycaemia in hospital is associated with worse outcomes**, but so is hypoglycaemia — the aim is stable moderate control, not tight control.

> [!warning] Perioperative and procedural
> **· Withhold SGLT2 inhibitors before surgery** — because of the risk of **euglycaemic DKA**, which is easily missed perioperatively since the glucose looks acceptable. `UNVERIFIED — the recommended withholding period.`
> **· Metformin and contrast or acute illness** — withhold per local policy.
> **· Consider withholding GLP-1 receptor agonists before procedures** because of delayed gastric emptying and aspiration risk. `UNVERIFIED — this recommendation is recent and evolving.`
> **· Type 1 diabetes fasting for surgery needs basal insulin continued and usually a variable rate insulin infusion**, with first-on-the-list scheduling where possible.

> [!tip] Sick day rules — for both types
> **· Do not stop insulin.** **· Monitor glucose more frequently. · Check ketones (essential in type 1, and in type 2 on an SGLT2 inhibitor). · Maintain hydration and carbohydrate intake. · Withhold the "sick day medications" — metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics and NSAIDs — during acute dehydrating illness, because they precipitate acute kidney injury and, for SGLT2 inhibitors, ketoacidosis. · Know when to seek help.**
> **Every patient with diabetes should have written sick day guidance**, and it should be checked rather than assumed.

> [!tip] Living with diabetes — the practical matters
> **Driving** — glucose checking before driving, carrying carbohydrate, and the reporting obligations, which differ for private and commercial licences and are affected by hypoglycaemia unawareness. Cross-refer [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] 0.6.
> **Employment, insurance, travel** (insulin in hand luggage, time zone adjustment, letters).
> **Religious fasting including Ramadan** — requires planning, risk stratification and regimen adjustment, and patients frequently do not raise it. **Asking about it is better than assuming.**

**Ix:** OGTT for gestational diabetes screening and postpartum reassessment (*why:* both diagnosis and the identification of lifelong risk; *what:* glucose values). Ketones in any unwell patient with diabetes, **including those on SGLT2 inhibitors with normal glucose** (*why:* euglycaemic DKA; *what:* beta-hydroxybutyrate). **Afternoon or pre-dinner glucose in patients on corticosteroids** (*why:* the pattern of steroid hyperglycaemia is missed by fasting testing alone; *what:* postprandial hyperglycaemia). UEC during acute illness (*why:* acute kidney injury from the sick day medications; *what:* rising creatinine).
