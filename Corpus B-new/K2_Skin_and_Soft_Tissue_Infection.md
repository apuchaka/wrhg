## 0.1 Cellulitis and Erysipelas

**D:** **Cellulitis** is acute bacterial infection of the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue. **Erysipelas** involves the superficial dermis and lymphatics, and is **more sharply demarcated with a raised, palpable border**, more often affecting the face.

**A/P:** A breach in the skin barrier permits bacterial entry → spreading infection through subcutaneous tissue → local inflammation with erythema, warmth, swelling and pain, plus lymphatic involvement and systemic features. **Organisms are usually *Streptococcus pyogenes* in non-purulent cellulitis, and *Staphylococcus aureus* where there is purulence, an abscess or a penetrating injury** — which is the basis for the antibiotic choice.

> [!tip] Find and treat the portal of entry — it is what prevents recurrence
> **Tinea pedis with maceration between the toes is a very common and very treatable portal of entry for lower limb cellulitis.**
> **Look between the toes in every patient with leg cellulitis, and treat the tinea.** Patients get recurrent cellulitis for years because nobody looked at their feet.
> Other portals: **venous or arterial ulcers, eczema, insect bites, cracked heels, lymphoedema-related skin changes, injecting sites, and toenail disease.**

> [!warning] Mark the border and write the time on the skin
> A dated, timed pen line around the erythema converts a subjective impression into an objective measurement of progression or response. **It costs seconds and it settles the question of whether the patient is improving overnight**, which is otherwise contested between shifts.
> **Rapid extension beyond a marked border in hours should raise necrotising infection** — see 0.2.

> [!danger] A substantial proportion of "cellulitis" is not cellulitis
> **Bilateral lower limb "cellulitis" is almost never cellulitis** — bilateral simultaneous infection is rare, and the far commoner explanation is **venous stasis dermatitis or lipodermatosclerosis.** Cross-refer [[B6_Oedema__Fatigue__Weakness_and_Undifferentiated_Presentations]] 0.2. These patients receive repeated antibiotic courses and admissions while the venous disease goes untreated.
> **The other mimics worth knowing:**
> **· Deep vein thrombosis** — and it can coexist.
> **· Gout and pseudogout** — a hot, red, exquisitely tender joint with surrounding erythema and fever, frequently high.
> **· Contact or irritant dermatitis** — itchy rather than painful, with a sharp geometric border matching the contactant.
> **· Superficial thrombophlebitis** — a tender cord along a vein.
> **· Lymphoedema** with inflammatory change.
> **· Erythema nodosum and panniculitis.**
> **· Drug reactions.**
> **· Calciphylaxis** — in dialysis patients, with exquisitely painful necrotic plaques.
> **· Necrotising fasciitis** — the mimic that matters most.
> **If it is not improving on appropriate antibiotics, the commonest reason is that it was never cellulitis.**

> [!warning] Cellulitis is a clinical diagnosis — most investigations add little
> **Blood cultures have low yield in uncomplicated cellulitis** and are not routinely indicated. Take them if the patient is systemically unwell, immunocompromised, has lymphoedema, or if an unusual exposure is suspected.
> **Skin swabs of intact skin are useless.** Swab only if there is a wound, ulcer or discharge.
> **Imaging is for suspected abscess, retained foreign body, osteomyelitis or necrotising infection**, not for routine cellulitis.

**S/Smx:** Erythema, warmth, swelling, tenderness and a poorly demarcated border (sharply demarcated in erysipelas), with fever, rigors, malaise and regional lymphadenopathy. Lymphangitic streaking may be present.

**Ix:** FBC and CRP (*why:* baseline and to track response; *what:* leucocytosis, raised CRP). Blood cultures **where systemically unwell, immunocompromised or atypical** (*why:* yield is low in uncomplicated disease but matters when bacteraemia is plausible; *what:* organism). Wound swab where there is a breach or discharge (*why:* identifies the organism and detects MRSA; *what:* growth and sensitivities). **Glucose and HbA1c** (*why:* undiagnosed or poorly controlled diabetes predisposes to and complicates soft tissue infection; *what:* hyperglycaemia). Doppler ultrasound where DVT is a consideration (*why:* it mimics and coexists; *what:* thrombus). Ultrasound of the affected area (*why:* identifies a drainable collection, which changes management from antibiotics to drainage; *what:* abscess, foreign body). Plain radiography or MRI where osteomyelitis is suspected — particularly in the diabetic foot and over pressure areas (*why:* bone involvement changes duration and may require surgery; *what:* bone destruction, marrow oedema). **Examination of the feet and toe web spaces** (*why:* finds the portal of entry that prevents recurrence; *what:* tinea, fissures, ulcer).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Antibiotics, **elevation of the affected limb** — genuinely effective and consistently under-emphasised — analgesia, and marking of the border. `UNVERIFIED — oral versus intravenous criteria, agents and duration from eTG Antibiotic.`

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Complete the antibiotic course, drain any abscess, and **treat the portal of entry.**
**If not improving at 48 hours, reconsider: is it actually cellulitis · is there an undrained abscess · is the organism resistant · is there a deeper infection (osteomyelitis, necrotising) · or is the limb ischaemic?** Cross-refer [[B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease]] 0.5.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Address the predisposing factors — lymphoedema management with compression, venous insufficiency, tinea, weight, glycaemic control, and skin care with emollients.**
**Antibiotic prophylaxis is considered for recurrent cellulitis**, though it works only while it is being taken, so the underlying factors must be addressed too. `UNVERIFIED — prophylaxis regimens and the recurrence threshold.`

## 0.2 Necrotising Soft Tissue Infection

**D:** Rapidly progressive infection causing necrosis of fascia, subcutaneous tissue and sometimes muscle. **A surgical emergency with high mortality, in which delay to operation is the strongest modifiable determinant of death.**

> [!danger] The skin looks better than the patient — that discordance IS the diagnosis
> **Pain out of proportion to the visible findings** is the cardinal early feature, in a patient who is **systemically far more unwell than the appearance of the skin explains** — tachycardic, febrile, delirious, with a rising lactate over an area that looks like modest cellulitis.
> **The skin changes are LATE**, because the necrosis begins in the fascial plane and works outward. By the time you see **dusky or purple discolouration, haemorrhagic bullae, crepitus, or frank necrosis**, the disease is advanced.
> **Anaesthesia over the affected area** — from destruction of cutaneous nerves — is a useful and specific late sign: an area that should be exquisitely painful but is numb.
> **Rapid progression over hours, extending beyond a marked border**, is the other key feature.

> [!tip] The types, and who gets them
> **· Type I — polymicrobial**, mixed aerobes and anaerobes. **Diabetics, the elderly, post-operative patients, and the perineum.** **Fournier gangrene** is the perineal form, and it progresses with terrifying speed.
> **· Type II — monomicrobial, usually group A *Streptococcus*** with or without *S. aureus*. **Often in previously healthy younger people, after minor trauma, a bruise, an injection, or varicella in children.** Frequently accompanied by **streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.**
> **· Type III — *Vibrio vulnificus*** after seawater exposure or raw shellfish, particularly in those with **chronic liver disease, haemochromatosis or immunocompromise** — a genuine consideration in coastal Australia. **Clostridial myonecrosis (gas gangrene)** after contaminated traumatic wounds or injecting drug use.

> [!danger] Imaging must not delay surgery, and a scoring system must not exclude the diagnosis
> **The definitive diagnostic test is surgical exploration** — the finding of grey, dishwater-coloured, non-bleeding fascia that separates easily on blunt dissection.
> **CT may show fascial thickening, fluid tracking or gas, and supports the diagnosis — but a normal CT does NOT exclude it**, and sending an unstable patient to the scanner rather than to theatre costs lives.
> **The LRINEC score** combines laboratory parameters to stratify risk. **It has poor sensitivity and must never be used to rule out necrotising infection.** A low score in a patient with pain out of proportion and systemic toxicity means the score is wrong, not the clinician. `UNVERIFIED — the score components and thresholds.`
> **If you are seriously considering the diagnosis, call the surgeon now.**

**S/Smx:** Severe pain disproportionate to findings, rapid progression, systemic toxicity, fever, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental state, and — late — skin discolouration, bullae, crepitus, anaesthesia and necrosis.

**Ix:** **Urgent surgical review — the investigation is the operation** (*why:* direct inspection of the fascia is diagnostic and the debridement is the treatment; *what:* necrotic fascia). VBG with **lactate** (*why:* systemic toxicity and hypoperfusion out of proportion to a modest-looking skin lesion is a key clue; *what:* raised lactate, acidosis). FBC, UEC, CRP, CK, coagulation, glucose (*why:* the LRINEC parameters and evidence of muscle involvement and organ dysfunction; *what:* leucocytosis, hyponatraemia, renal impairment, raised CK, coagulopathy). **Blood cultures and intra-operative tissue for culture and Gram stain** (*why:* directs antimicrobial therapy, and tissue is far more informative than a surface swab; *what:* organism). CT **only if it does not delay theatre** (*why:* may show gas and fascial fluid and helps define extent; *what:* fascial gas, fluid — with a normal scan not excluding). Plain radiograph (*why:* may show soft tissue gas, though its absence means nothing; *what:* subcutaneous gas).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
**Resuscitate, take cultures, give broad-spectrum antibiotics including an agent that suppresses toxin production, and get the patient to theatre.** ICU involvement. `UNVERIFIED — all regimens, and the role of intravenous immunoglobulin in streptococcal toxic shock, which remains debated.`

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
**Radical surgical debridement, repeated as required** — a single operation is rarely sufficient, and planned relook procedures are the norm. Amputation where limb-threatening. Reconstruction later.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Prolonged rehabilitation, wound and reconstructive management, and psychological support — these patients survive a catastrophic illness with major disfigurement. **Contact tracing and chemoprophylaxis are considered for close contacts in invasive group A streptococcal disease.** `UNVERIFIED — the indications and regimen.`

## 0.3 Abscess, Boils and Deep Space Infection

> [!danger] Incision and drainage is the treatment — antibiotics alone will not cure an abscess
> **An abscess is a closed collection of pus with no blood supply to the interior. Antibiotics cannot reach the centre.**
> **Antibiotics are an adjunct**, indicated where there are systemic features, significant surrounding cellulitis, immunocompromise, a high-risk site (face, hand, perineum), or a large or multiple abscesses. **They are not a substitute for drainage, and "let's try antibiotics first" is a recurring error that delays the definitive treatment.**

> [!tip] Recurrent boils — think beyond the individual lesion
> **Recurrent furunculosis suggests *S. aureus* colonisation** — nasal, axillary, perineal — and **decolonisation of the patient and sometimes household contacts** is what breaks the cycle.
> Consider **community-acquired MRSA**, including **Panton-Valentine leukocidin-producing strains**, which cause recurrent aggressive skin abscesses in otherwise healthy people and cluster in households and close-contact settings.
> **Community-acquired MRSA prevalence varies substantially across Australia and is notably higher in remote communities and in northern Australia**, which affects empirical choice. `UNVERIFIED — local prevalence and empirical recommendations from eTG and local antibiograms.`
> **Screen for diabetes.**

> [!danger] Hidradenitis suppurativa is not an infection, and is treated as one for years
> Recurrent painful nodules, abscesses, sinus tracts and scarring in **the axillae, groin, inframammary and perianal regions** — the intertriginous, apocrine-bearing areas.
> **It is a chronic inflammatory follicular occlusion disease, not primarily infective.** Patients are frequently given repeated courses of antibiotics and repeated incisions over years before it is recognised.
> **The clue is the distribution, the recurrence in the same sites, the sinus tracts and the scarring.** Management involves long-term medical therapy, smoking cessation, weight management and dermatological or surgical input — not just drainage. Cross-refer [[G5_Lumps__Ulcers_and_Nails]].

> [!warning] Hand infections are surgical emergencies more often than they look
> **· Flexor tenosynovitis** — recognised by **Kanavel's signs: a finger held in slight flexion · fusiform swelling of the whole digit · tenderness along the flexor tendon sheath · and pain on passive extension.** **This requires urgent hand surgical drainage** — delay causes tendon necrosis and permanent loss of function.
> **· Felon** — a closed-space pulp infection of the fingertip, which needs drainage before it causes osteomyelitis.
> **· Paronychia** — around the nail fold; drainage where fluctuant.
> **· Any hand wound over the MCP joints — the "fight bite"** — cross-refer [[A7_Burns__Chemical_Injury__Wounds_and_Crush_Injury]] 0.5.
> **The hand has closed compartments and functionally critical structures. Refer early rather than watching.**

> [!tip] Site-specific abscesses worth knowing
> **· Perianal and perirectal** — cross-refer [[C6_Dyspepsia__Oesophageal_and_Anorectal_Disease]] 0.4. Drainage, and consider Crohn disease and diabetes.
> **· Breast** — lactational (usually *S. aureus*; **continue breastfeeding or expressing, which aids drainage**) and non-lactational (associated with smoking and periductal mastitis, and **any breast abscess in a non-lactating woman warrants follow-up to exclude inflammatory or underlying malignancy**). Cross-refer [[O7_Breast_Disease]].
> **· Pilonidal.**
> **· Dental** — a common occult source of fever and of deep neck space infection. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.10.
> **· In people who inject drugs** — groin and deep abscesses, with a lower threshold for imaging, and **consider the associated complications: DVT, mycotic aneurysm, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, tetanus and wound botulism.**

**Ix:** **Ultrasound** (*why:* confirms a drainable collection versus cellulitis alone, and can guide aspiration — it changes management directly; *what:* fluid collection, foreign body). **Pus for culture and sensitivities at the time of drainage** (*why:* far more informative than a surface swab, identifies MRSA, and guides therapy if the patient does not settle; *what:* organism). Blood cultures where systemically unwell (*why:* bacteraemia; *what:* organism). Glucose and HbA1c (*why:* undiagnosed diabetes; *what:* hyperglycaemia). CT or MRI for deep, perianal or hand infections (*why:* defines the extent and relationship to critical structures for surgical planning; *what:* collection, tract, bone involvement). **HIV and immune screening in recurrent or unusual abscesses** (*why:* underlying immunodeficiency; *what:* positive result).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
**Drain it.** Analgesia and local or general anaesthesia as appropriate. Antibiotics as an adjunct where indicated. **Urgent hand surgery referral for flexor tenosynovitis.**

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Adequate drainage with packing or a drain where appropriate, and follow-up to ensure healing.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Decolonisation for recurrent staphylococcal disease, diabetes screening and management, and **recognition and appropriate management of hidradenitis suppurativa rather than repeated courses of antibiotics.**

## 0.4 Lymphangitis, Lymphadenitis and the Lymphoedema Cycle

> [!tip] Lymphangitis
> **Tender erythematous streaks extending proximally from a site of infection toward the regional lymph nodes**, usually streptococcal, with regional lymphadenopathy and systemic features. Treated as cellulitis with the same organism assumptions.

> [!warning] Nodular lymphangitis — a distinct pattern with a specific differential
> **A chain of subcutaneous nodules ascending along lymphatic channels**, rather than a diffuse red streak, has a short and specific differential:
> **· Sporotrichosis** — classically after a **rose thorn or plant material injury in a gardener** ("rose gardener's disease").
> **· Atypical mycobacteria — *Mycobacterium marinum*** after **fish tank or aquarium exposure** ("fish tank granuloma").
> **· *Nocardia*** after soil exposure.
> **· Leishmaniasis** and **tularaemia** in relevant geography.
> **These do not respond to standard cellulitis antibiotics**, which is why recognising the pattern matters — the "cellulitis" that will not settle in a gardener or an aquarium owner is the classic presentation.

> [!danger] The lymphoedema-cellulitis vicious cycle
> **Lymphoedema predisposes to cellulitis, and each episode of cellulitis damages lymphatics further, worsening the lymphoedema and increasing the risk of the next episode.**
> **Breaking the cycle requires treating the lymphoedema, not just the infection: compression, complex decongestive therapy, meticulous skin care and emollients, prompt treatment of any breach, and antibiotic prophylaxis in those with frequent recurrence.**
> **Referral to a lymphoedema service is the intervention that reduces future admissions**, and it is rarely made from an acute cellulitis admission. Cross-refer [[B6_Oedema__Fatigue__Weakness_and_Undifferentiated_Presentations]] 0.3.

> [!tip] Suppurative lymphadenitis and the causes worth considering
> **· Bacterial** — *S. aureus*, group A *Streptococcus*.
> **· Mycobacterial** — **tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis ("scrofula")** with matted, non-tender nodes and possible sinus formation, and **non-tuberculous mycobacterial lymphadenitis in young children**, which is managed differently (often excision rather than prolonged antibiotics).
> **· *Bartonella henselae* (cat scratch disease)** — regional lymphadenopathy weeks after a cat scratch or bite, often in children, usually self-limiting.
> **· Melioidosis** in northern Australia. Cross-refer [[K1_Fever_Workup]] 0.4.
> **· Malignancy** — a persistent, hard, fixed or matted node is not an abscess. Cross-refer [[B6_Oedema__Fatigue__Weakness_and_Undifferentiated_Presentations]] 0.8 and [[F4_Neck_Lumps_and_Facial_Pain]].

**Ix:** As per 0.1, plus **detailed exposure history — gardening, aquariums, animals, soil, travel** (*why:* the nodular lymphangitis differential is entirely determined by exposure, and the diagnosis will not be considered without asking; *what:* the exposure). **Tissue biopsy with culture including mycobacterial and fungal media, and histology** (*why:* the atypical organisms will not grow on routine culture and require specific request and prolonged incubation; *what:* organism, granulomas). Ultrasound of the node (*why:* distinguishes reactive node from abscess and guides aspiration; *what:* architecture, collection). **Excision biopsy rather than fine needle aspiration where lymphoma is a consideration** (*why:* nodal architecture is needed and FNA is frequently non-diagnostic; *what:* histology). Cross-refer [[B6_Oedema__Fatigue__Weakness_and_Undifferentiated_Presentations]] 0.8.

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat as cellulitis where the picture is typical. **Withhold empirical treatment and pursue tissue where the pattern is nodular or atypical**, because the treatment differs.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Organism-specific, often prolonged for mycobacterial and fungal causes.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Lymphoedema management and referral**, skin care, and prophylaxis where recurrent.

## 0.5 Bites, Wounds and Australian Exposures

> [!tip] Bites — the organism depends on the mouth
> **· Cat bites** — deep, narrow puncture wounds that inoculate organisms deep and seal over. **High infection rate. *Pasteurella multocida* causes rapid-onset cellulitis, often within 12–24 hours.** **Prophylactic antibiotics are indicated for essentially all cat bites**, and hand bites in particular warrant a low threshold for surgical review.
> **· Dog bites** — more crushing and tearing, lower infection rate than cat bites, but **greater tissue damage.** ***Capnocytophaga canimorsus* causes fulminant sepsis in asplenic, immunocompromised and alcohol-dependent patients** — a small dog bite can be lethal in that group, and it warrants prophylaxis and vigilance.
> **· Human bites** — ***Eikenella corrodens* and oral anaerobes.** **A "fight bite" over the MCP joint is a joint and tendon injury until proven otherwise** — cross-refer [[A7_Burns__Chemical_Injury__Wounds_and_Crush_Injury]] 0.5.
> **· All bites** — irrigate copiously, debride, **generally do NOT close primarily** (particularly hand and puncture wounds), check tetanus status, and consider bloodborne virus exposure for human bites. Cross-refer [[A7_Burns__Chemical_Injury__Wounds_and_Crush_Injury]] 0.5 and [[K3_Exposure__Tuberculosis__HIV_and_Immunodeficiency]].

> [!danger] Australian bat lyssavirus — any bat contact
> **Any bite, scratch or mucous membrane exposure from ANY bat in Australia — flying foxes and insectivorous microbats alike — is a potential Australian bat lyssavirus exposure.**
> **ABLV is closely related to rabies, is almost universally fatal once symptomatic, and there is no effective treatment — but post-exposure prophylaxis is highly effective.**
> **Wash the wound thoroughly and immediately with soap and water for a prolonged period, apply a virucidal agent, and arrange urgent post-exposure prophylaxis with rabies immunoglobulin and vaccine.** Contact the public health unit.
> **Advise that members of the public should never handle bats** — injured bats should be reported to wildlife services, and only vaccinated handlers should touch them. `UNVERIFIED — the washing duration, the PEP schedule and immunoglobulin indications, from the Australian Immunisation Handbook and state health guidance.`
> This is genuinely Australian, genuinely examinable, and genuinely lethal if mismanaged.

> [!warning] *Mycobacterium ulcerans* — Buruli / Bairnsdale ulcer
> **An increasing problem in coastal Victoria, with expanding geographic distribution.**
> Presents as a **painless nodule or papule** that progresses over weeks to months to an **ulcer with characteristically undermined edges and a necrotic base**, typically on exposed limbs. **The painlessness is the striking feature** and is why it is often present for a long time before presentation.
> **It is repeatedly misdiagnosed as an insect bite, a spider bite, or ordinary cellulitis, and treated with courses of standard antibiotics that do nothing.**
> **Diagnosis is by PCR on a swab or biopsy of the ulcer edge**, and treatment is **prolonged specific antimycobacterial therapy**, sometimes with surgery. **Ask about travel to or residence in endemic coastal areas.** `UNVERIFIED — the current endemic areas, diagnostic method and treatment regimen from Victorian and national guidance.`

> [!tip] Other exposure-specific organisms
> **· Marine and seawater** — ***Vibrio vulnificus*** (severe, necrotising, high mortality in chronic liver disease and haemochromatosis — see 0.2), ***Mycobacterium marinum*** (fish tank granuloma), ***Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae*** in fish and meat handlers.
> **· Soil and freshwater** — **tetanus**, **melioidosis** in northern Australia, ***Nocardia***, **clostridial infection**, and **leptospirosis.**
> **· Occupational** — abattoir and farm work (**Q fever**, brucellosis, orf, erysipeloid), gardening (**sporotrichosis, tetanus**), and healthcare (bloodborne viruses — cross-refer [[K3_Exposure__Tuberculosis__HIV_and_Immunodeficiency]]).
> **· Tetanus prophylaxis** — assess immunisation status and wound type for every wound. Cross-refer [[A7_Burns__Chemical_Injury__Wounds_and_Crush_Injury]] 0.5.

**Ix:** **A detailed exposure history — animal, marine, soil, occupational, geographic and recreational** (*why:* it determines the organism and therefore the antibiotic, and none of these diagnoses will be made without it; *what:* the exposure). Wound culture, with **specific request for atypical organisms where the exposure suggests them** (*why:* *M. marinum*, *M. ulcerans*, *Nocardia* and fungi require specific media, prolonged incubation or PCR and will not be found on a routine swab; *what:* organism). **PCR or biopsy of the ulcer edge where Buruli ulcer is suspected** (*why:* the diagnostic test; *what:* *M. ulcerans* DNA). Radiography where a retained foreign body, fracture or joint involvement is possible (*why:* penetrating injuries and bites frequently involve deeper structures; *what:* foreign body, air in a joint, fracture). Bloodborne virus baseline serology after human bites and needlestick (*why:* baseline for exposure management; *what:* HIV, hepatitis B and C status — cross-refer [[K3_Exposure__Tuberculosis__HIV_and_Immunodeficiency]]). Tetanus immunisation history (*why:* determines vaccine and immunoglobulin; *what:* status).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
**Wash and irrigate copiously.** Debride. Leave high-risk wounds open. Prophylactic antibiotics for cat bites, hand bites, human bites and the immunocompromised. **Tetanus prophylaxis. Urgent post-exposure prophylaxis for bat exposure.**

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Organism-specific therapy, surgical exploration where deep structures are involved, and prolonged therapy for mycobacterial disease.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Occupational review and vaccination where relevant — **Q fever vaccination for at-risk occupations**, and rabies pre-exposure vaccination for bat handlers. Wound and scar management.

## 0.6 Infestations

> [!tip] Scabies
> ***Sarcoptes scabiei* var. *hominis*** burrows in the stratum corneum. **The intense itch is a delayed hypersensitivity response to the mite and its products, which is why it takes weeks to develop on a first infestation but hours on re-infestation.**
> **Characteristic features: intense itch, markedly worse at night · burrows in the finger web spaces, wrists, axillae, periumbilical skin, genitals and, in infants, the palms, soles and scalp · and OTHER HOUSEHOLD MEMBERS ITCHING.** That last point is often the most useful part of the history.
> **Treatment principles that determine whether it works:**
> **· Treat ALL household and close contacts SIMULTANEOUSLY, whether or not they are symptomatic** — otherwise reinfestation is guaranteed. This is the commonest reason treatment fails.
> **· Apply topical treatment to the WHOLE body from the neck down** (and including the head in infants and the elderly), including under the nails, and leave for the specified duration.
> **· Repeat the treatment after an interval** to catch newly hatched mites.
> **· Wash bedding, clothing and towels in hot water, or seal them for a period.**
> **· Warn that the ITCH PERSISTS FOR WEEKS after successful treatment** — this is post-scabetic eczema, not treatment failure, and patients otherwise return convinced the treatment did not work and are re-treated unnecessarily.
> `UNVERIFIED — agents, application instructions, repeat intervals and age restrictions.`

> [!danger] Crusted (Norwegian) scabies — and its Australian public health significance
> **A hyperinfestation with an enormous mite burden — thousands to millions — occurring in the immunocompromised, the elderly, those with cognitive or physical impairment, and those unable to scratch or respond to the itch.**
> **It presents as thick, crusted, hyperkeratotic plaques, and the itch may be absent**, which is why it is missed and mistaken for eczema or psoriasis.
> **It is extraordinarily contagious** — shed skin scales carry viable mites, and outbreaks occur in residential care facilities and households. **It requires combined topical and oral treatment, isolation, environmental decontamination, and contact management.**
> **The Australian public health dimension matters:** scabies and impetigo carry a very high burden in some remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, and **scabies-related skin breakdown is a major driver of group A streptococcal skin infection, which in turn drives acute rheumatic fever, rheumatic heart disease and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.**
> **So treating scabies in these settings is not a cosmetic intervention — it is cardiac and renal prevention.** Community-wide treatment programs exist for this reason. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.10 and [[AU1_Australian_Health_Context_and_ATSI_Health]]. `UNVERIFIED — current programs and treatment protocols.`

> [!tip] Impetigo
> Superficial infection with **group A *Streptococcus* and/or *S. aureus*** — non-bullous with golden crusting, or bullous.
> **In remote Australian communities the prevalence in children is among the highest in the world**, and the same ARF/RHD and glomerulonephritis link applies. **Treatment thresholds and regimens differ in high-risk populations** — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.10. `UNVERIFIED — regimens from eTG and the ARF guideline.`

> [!tip] The other infestations
> **· Head lice** — itch and nits adherent to hair shafts. Treat with a pediculicide or wet combing; **treat contacts; no need to exclude children from school under current Australian practice.** `UNVERIFIED — current exclusion policy.`
> **· Pubic lice** — **consider it a marker of sexual contact and offer a full STI screen.** Cross-refer [[O6_Sexual_and_Reproductive_Health]].
> **· Body lice** — associated with homelessness and poor access to laundry; a vector for trench fever and other infections. **Treatment is as much about clothing and laundering as about the person.**
> **· Cutaneous larva migrans** — an intensely itchy, serpiginous, migrating track, typically on the feet or buttocks after **walking barefoot or lying on contaminated sand in tropical areas.** Self-limiting but treatable.
> **· Tungiasis, myiasis** — in returning travellers.

**Ix:** **Skin scraping or dermoscopy for scabies** (*why:* visualising mites, eggs or faecal pellets confirms the diagnosis, and dermoscopy of a burrow shows the characteristic "delta wing" appearance — but **treatment is frequently and reasonably empirical on the clinical picture plus itchy contacts**; *what:* mites, ova). **Examination of household contacts** (*why:* finding itch in other household members is diagnostically powerful and determines who needs treatment; *what:* symptomatic contacts). Bacterial swab where impetiginised (*why:* secondary infection is common and may require systemic treatment; *what:* organism). **STI screen for pubic lice** (*why:* it is a sexual contact marker; *what:* concurrent infection). Screening for immunosuppression in crusted scabies (*why:* it occurs in the immunocompromised, including HTLV-1 which has particular relevance in central Australia; *what:* HIV, HTLV-1, other causes).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat, and **treat contacts simultaneously.** Isolate and involve infection control for crusted scabies.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Complete the treatment course with the repeat application, and manage environmental decontamination.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Explain the persistent post-treatment itch** to prevent unnecessary re-treatment. Address overcrowding, access to washing facilities and community-level control where relevant — **these are the determinants that drive recurrence, and in the Australian remote community context they are the actual problem.**
