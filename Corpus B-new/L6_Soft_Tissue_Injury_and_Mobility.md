## 0.1 Soft Tissue Injury and Healing

**A/P:** Injury to **tendon (muscle to bone), ligament (bone to bone), muscle, fascia, bursa or cartilage**, healing through three overlapping phases:
**1. INFLAMMATORY — the first days.** Haematoma, cytokine release, cellular recruitment.
**2. PROLIFERATIVE / REPAIR — days to weeks.** Fibroblast activity laying down disorganised type III collagen.
**3. REMODELLING — months to a year or more.** Collagen reorganises along lines of stress and matures into type I.

> [!danger] Load is the stimulus for healing, not the enemy of it
> **Connective tissue adapts to the load placed on it. Complete rest causes atrophy, disorganised collagen, stiffness and weakness; appropriate progressive load drives organised remodelling and restores strength.**
> **This inverts the intuitive approach** — patients (and clinicians) reach for rest, immobilisation and avoidance, which produce a weaker tissue and a longer recovery.
> **The clinical task is finding the level of load that stimulates without re-injuring**, and progressing it. That is what physiotherapy actually does.

> [!warning] Set the timeframe honestly at the first consultation
> **Tendon and ligament have poor blood supply and remodel slowly. Full remodelling takes MONTHS TO A YEAR.**
> **Patients who are told "six weeks" and are still symptomatic at three months conclude something is wrong, seek imaging, and enter the investigation cycle.** **Telling them the real timeframe at the outset prevents that**, and is one of the more valuable things said in the consultation.

> [!tip] What impairs healing
> **Age · DIABETES · smoking · corticosteroids (systemic and injected) · FLUOROQUINOLONE antibiotics · poor nutrition and low protein intake · peripheral vascular disease · obesity · and prolonged immobilisation.**
> **Diabetes and metabolic syndrome are genuinely associated with tendinopathy and with poorer healing**, which is worth knowing when a tendinopathy is not responding as expected.

## 0.2 Acute Soft Tissue Injury Management

> [!danger] RICE has been superseded — the current framework is PEACE and LOVE
> **PEACE — in the first days after injury:**
> **· PROTECT** — unload and restrict movement briefly, **for days rather than weeks.**
> **· ELEVATE.**
> **· AVOID ANTI-INFLAMMATORIES** — see the caveat below.
> **· COMPRESS.**
> **· EDUCATE** — set expectations, and steer away from passive treatments and unnecessary imaging.
> **LOVE — after the first days:**
> **· LOAD** — progressive mechanical loading as symptoms allow.
> **· OPTIMISM** — **patient expectations independently predict outcome**, and this is not a soft add-on. Catastrophising and fear-avoidance measurably prolong recovery.
> **· VASCULARISATION** — early pain-free cardiovascular activity.
> **· EXERCISE** — restoring mobility, strength and proprioception.

> [!warning] The NSAID and ice question is genuinely contested
> **The rationale for avoiding early NSAIDs and prolonged ice is that inflammation is a necessary part of tissue repair, and there is evidence that NSAIDs may impair tendon, ligament, muscle and bone healing.**
> **But this is not settled**, and **adequate analgesia matters** — a patient in severe pain will not mobilise, and immobility causes its own harm.
> **A pragmatic position: prefer paracetamol and topical agents first, use oral NSAIDs at the lowest effective dose for the shortest period where analgesia genuinely requires it, and do not use them routinely for weeks. Avoid opioids.**
> `UNVERIFIED — this is an area of active debate; check current sports medicine guidance rather than treating either position as settled.`

> [!danger] Prolonged immobilisation causes harm
> **For most soft tissue injuries, early controlled movement produces better outcomes than immobilisation** — less stiffness, less muscle loss, faster return to function.
> **Prolonged use of slings, boots, casts and collars beyond what the injury requires produces joint stiffness, muscle atrophy and, in the shoulder, adhesive capsulitis.**
> **Where immobilisation is genuinely required — a fracture, a complete tendon rupture — it should be for a defined period with a plan for what follows.**

> [!tip] When to image, and when to refer
> **Image for: a decision rule indicating fracture risk, suspicion of complete rupture, inability to weight bear, significant deformity, neurovascular deficit, or failure to progress as expected.**
> **Refer for: complete tendon or ligament rupture, joint instability, neurovascular compromise, open injury, suspected compartment syndrome, and failure of appropriate conservative management.**
> **Do NOT image a straightforward sprain that is following the expected course.**

**Ix:** Radiographs per decision rules (*why:* excludes fracture where indicated; *what:* fracture). Ultrasound (*why:* dynamic assessment of tendon and muscle integrity, accessible and without radiation; *what:* tear, gap, tendinopathy). MRI (*why:* ligamentous, cartilage and deeper structures where the diagnosis will change management; *what:* the structural injury).

## 0.3 Tendinopathy

> [!danger] It is a degenerative process, not an inflammatory one — the name matters
> **Histology of chronic tendinopathy shows COLLAGEN DISORGANISATION, increased ground substance, NEOVASCULARISATION and cellular changes — with FEW OR NO INFLAMMATORY CELLS.**
> **This is why anti-inflammatory approaches disappoint, why "tendinitis" is the wrong word, and why the treatment is mechanical rather than pharmacological.**
> A useful model describes a **continuum: reactive tendinopathy (acute overload, reversible) → tendon disrepair → degenerative tendinopathy (structural change, less reversible)** — with the practical implication that **early recognition and load management can reverse it, while established degenerative change cannot be undone but can be worked around.**

**S/Smx:** **Load-related pain** with a characteristic pattern — **pain at the start of activity that "warms up" and eases, then returns worse in the hours afterwards and the next morning.** Localised tenderness at the tendon, pain on resisted contraction, and sometimes thickening.

> [!tip] Load is the treatment
> **Progressive loading is the best-evidenced management, and it works across sites.**
> A typical progression: **ISOMETRIC loading first (which is often analgesic in the reactive phase) → heavy slow resistance / isotonic loading → energy-storage and sport-specific loading.**
> **It takes months and adherence is the main determinant of success** — which is why explaining the rationale matters. A patient who believes rest is the treatment will not do the exercises.
> **Alongside: load management (reducing the aggravating volume without stopping entirely), footwear and ergonomic modification, and addressing metabolic contributors.**

> [!danger] What does not work, and what causes harm
> **· COMPLETE REST** — deconditions the tendon and the whole kinetic chain, and symptoms recur immediately on return.
> **· REPEATED CORTICOSTEROID INJECTION** — short-term relief with **worse long-term outcomes**, most clearly demonstrated in lateral epicondylalgia. Cross-refer [[L5]] 0.3.
> **· NEVER inject corticosteroid INTO the Achilles or patellar tendon** — it risks rupture in a load-bearing tendon.
> **· Passive modalities alone** — ultrasound therapy, massage and taping may provide symptomatic relief but do not produce tendon adaptation.

> [!danger] Fluoroquinolone-associated tendinopathy and rupture
> **Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, moxifloxacin) cause tendinopathy and tendon RUPTURE, most often of the Achilles, and it can occur within days of starting and up to months after stopping.**
> **Risk is higher with: concurrent CORTICOSTEROIDS, age over 60, renal impairment, and transplant recipients.**
> **If a patient on a fluoroquinolone develops tendon pain, STOP THE DRUG immediately, avoid loading the tendon, and review.**
> **This should also inform prescribing** — a fluoroquinolone is a poor choice in an older patient on prednisolone with a history of Achilles problems, and there is usually an alternative. Cross-refer [[L5]] 0.7.

**Ix:** **Usually a clinical diagnosis.** Ultrasound (*why:* confirms tendinopathy and excludes a tear where the diagnosis is uncertain — **but structural change on imaging correlates poorly with symptoms and is common in asymptomatic tendons**, so it should not drive management; *what:* thickening, hypoechoic change, neovascularity, tear). HbA1c and lipids (*why:* metabolic factors are associated with tendinopathy and poor response; *what:* diabetes, dyslipidaemia). **Medication review for fluoroquinolones and corticosteroids** (*why:* a reversible and dangerous contributor; *what:* culprit drug).

## 0.4 Muscle Injury

> [!tip] Strains and the sites that matter
> Graded by the extent of fibre disruption and the presence of a palpable defect. `UNVERIFIED — the grading system.`
> **· HAMSTRING** — the commonest sporting muscle injury, and it has a **high recurrence rate** because athletes return before rehabilitation is complete. **Return should be criteria-based, not time-based** — see 0.5.
> **· Medial gastrocnemius ("tennis leg")** — sudden calf pain on push-off, with bruising tracking to the ankle over days. **Must be distinguished from DVT and from Achilles rupture** — do the calf squeeze test and consider imaging, because the three coexist in the differential and a calf that is swollen and painful after an injury can still have a DVT.
> **· Quadriceps and adductor strains.**

> [!warning] Myositis ossificans
> **Heterotopic bone formation within a muscle after significant blunt contusion — classically the quadriceps after a direct knee to the thigh.**
> Presents as **persistent pain, a hard palpable mass and restricted range weeks after the injury**, and it can be mistaken radiologically for a sarcoma.
> **Aggressive early massage, forceful stretching and heat are thought to increase the risk** — so a large thigh contusion is managed with gentle early range of motion in flexion, not vigorous physiotherapy.

> [!tip] Delayed onset muscle soreness versus injury
> **DOMS: bilateral or diffuse, appears 24–48 hours after unaccustomed eccentric exercise, peaks at 48–72 hours, and improves with gentle activity.** It is normal.
> **Injury: immediate onset, focal, with a specific mechanism, and worse with activity.**
> **Distinguishing them prevents both unnecessary investigation and inappropriate reassurance.**

> [!danger] The two muscle emergencies
> **· ACUTE COMPARTMENT SYNDROME — pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment.** **Pulses are present and the limb is pink until very late — waiting for pulselessness means waiting too long.** Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.6.
> **· RHABDOMYOLYSIS** — after crush injury, prolonged immobility, extreme exertion, seizures, or drug toxicity. Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.6.
> **CHRONIC EXERTIONAL compartment syndrome** is different — exercise-induced pain and tightness that resolves predictably with rest, diagnosed by compartment pressure testing, and managed with load modification, gait retraining or fasciotomy.

**Ix:** Ultrasound or MRI where a significant tear is suspected (*why:* grading affects prognosis and return timeline, and identifies a complete rupture requiring surgery; *what:* tear extent, haematoma). **Doppler ultrasound where calf injury could be DVT** (*why:* they mimic each other and coexist; *what:* thrombus). CK and renal function where rhabdomyolysis is possible (*why:* acute kidney injury; *what:* raised CK, rising creatinine). **Compartment pressure measurement** in suspected chronic exertional compartment syndrome (*why:* the diagnostic test, performed with exercise; *what:* elevated post-exercise pressures).

## 0.5 Rehabilitation and Return to Activity

> [!tip] The sequence
> **1. Restore RANGE of motion and reduce pain and swelling.**
> **2. Restore STRENGTH — progressively, including eccentric loading.**
> **3. Restore PROPRIOCEPTION and neuromuscular control** — **this is the step most often skipped, and it is what prevents re-injury**, particularly after ankle sprain and ACL injury.
> **4. Sport- or task-SPECIFIC training** — replicating the demands the tissue will face.
> **5. Return, with ongoing maintenance.**

> [!danger] Return should be criteria-based, not time-based
> **"Six weeks" is not a criterion. The questions are: is the range full? is strength symmetrical? can they perform the specific tasks required? is there pain during or after?**
> **Time-based return is the main reason hamstring and ankle injuries recur.**
> **Psychological readiness matters too** — after ACL reconstruction, fear of re-injury is a significant and measurable predictor of failure to return to sport, independent of physical recovery.

> [!warning] Rapid increases in load cause overuse injury
> **The commonest cause of overuse injury is a sudden increase in training volume, intensity, frequency or surface — a new running program, a return after a break, a change of footwear, or a competition block.**
> **Ask about what changed in the weeks before the symptoms started** — it is usually there, and modifying it is the treatment.
> **Graded progression, and avoidance of large week-to-week jumps in load, is the preventive principle.**

> [!tip] Certification and return to work
> **Certificates should specify what the person CAN do, not only what they cannot** — restricted duties, modified hours, avoiding specific tasks — because **prolonged complete absence from work worsens outcomes and reduces the likelihood of ever returning.**
> **Early graded return to work is protective**, and the same fear-avoidance principles apply as in back pain. Cross-refer [[L4]] 0.1, [[A10]] 0.6 and [[GER3]].
> In South Australia, work-related injury involves **ReturnToWorkSA** processes. `UNVERIFIED — current certification requirements and forms.`

## 0.6 Immobility, Mobility Aids and Functional Assessment

> [!danger] Immobility is a diagnosis with its own complications, and most of them are preventable
> **Muscle loss begins within DAYS of bed rest and is disproportionately severe in older people.**
> **The consequences: falls · pressure injury · venous thromboembolism · pneumonia and atelectasis · constipation · urinary retention and infection · delirium · bone loss · orthostatic intolerance · and permanent functional decline leading to residential placement.**
> **On every ward round, ask: has this patient been out of bed today, and what is preventing it?** Cross-refer [[GER2]] 0.4.

> [!warning] Fitting a walking stick — three things that are commonly wrong
> **1. HELD IN THE OPPOSITE HAND to the affected leg.** This is counterintuitive and is the most frequent error. Holding it on the affected side does almost nothing; holding it opposite widens the base of support and genuinely offloads the affected limb.
> **2. HEIGHT — the handle should be at the level of the WRIST CREASE (or the ulnar styloid) when the person stands upright with the arm relaxed at their side**, giving about 15–20 degrees of elbow flexion.
> **3. TECHNIQUE — the stick and the affected leg move TOGETHER**, then the good leg steps through.
> **A stick that is too tall or too short, or held in the wrong hand, increases falls risk rather than reducing it — an incorrectly fitted aid is worse than none.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the precise fitting measurements.`

> [!tip] The other aids
> **· Axillary crutches — weight must go through the HANDS, not the axilla.** **Weight-bearing through the axilla causes crutch palsy — a radial nerve injury with wrist drop.** There should be a two-finger gap between the axilla and the crutch top.
> **· Forearm (elbow) crutches** — better for longer-term use and for those with adequate upper limb strength.
> **· Pick-up frame** — provides most stability but must be lifted, so it requires upper limb strength and interrupts gait rhythm.
> **· Wheeled frame or four-wheel walker** — better for those who cannot lift a frame, allows continuous gait, and a seat allows rest. **Requires adequate cognition and braking ability** — a four-wheel walker given to someone who cannot manage the brakes is a hazard.
> **Aids should be prescribed and fitted by a physiotherapist where possible**, and reviewed as function changes.

> [!info] Weight-bearing status — know the terms
> **· NWB — non-weight-bearing:** no contact with the ground at all.
> **· TTWB / TDWB — touch or toe-touch weight-bearing:** the foot rests on the ground for balance only, taking minimal load.
> **· PWB — partial weight-bearing:** a specified proportion of body weight.
> **· WBAT — weight-bearing as tolerated.**
> **· FWB — full weight-bearing.**
> **Getting this right matters: an order for non-weight-bearing in an older patient often means they cannot mobilise at all**, with all the consequences above — so it should be specified deliberately by the surgical team and questioned if it seems inconsistent with the injury.

> [!tip] Functional assessment — the measures that are quick and predictive
> **· Timed Up and Go** — time to rise from a chair, walk a short distance, turn, return and sit. **Quick, repeatable, and predicts falls.**
> **· Gait speed** — one of the best single predictors of adverse outcomes in older people.
> **· Sit-to-stand test** — lower limb strength and functional capacity.
> **· ADL and IADL assessment** — cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.3, remembering that **instrumental activities decline first.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the cut-off values for each measure.`

> [!warning] Rehabilitation settings and who benefits
> **· Inpatient rehabilitation** — for those with rehabilitation potential and goals who cannot yet manage at home.
> **· Geriatric evaluation and management (GEM)** — for older patients with multiple problems.
> **· Transition care** — time-limited support after hospital, allowing recovery before permanent decisions are made. **Under-used, and it prevents premature residential placement.** Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.3.
> **· Community and outpatient rehabilitation, and home-based programs.**
> **The key principle: assess rehabilitation potential rather than assuming it from age**, and **do not make permanent placement decisions from an acute bed.**

**Ix:** **Physiotherapy and occupational therapy assessment** (*why:* they determine aid prescription, home safety and rehabilitation potential more accurately than a medical assessment does; *what:* mobility, transfers, ADL performance, equipment needs). **Home visit or home assessment** (*why:* function in a corridor bears little relation to function at home with stairs, thresholds, bathrooms and no handrails; *what:* hazards, feasibility). Timed Up and Go and gait speed (*why:* objective baseline and falls risk; *what:* the values). Cognitive assessment (*why:* determines the safety of certain aids and the capacity for rehabilitation; *what:* impairment).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Mobilise. Remove unnecessary tethers — catheters, lines, telemetry. Analgesia adequate to permit movement.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Rehabilitation matched to need, correctly fitted aids, and home modification.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Ongoing exercise, falls prevention, review of aids as function changes, and carer support. Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.5.
