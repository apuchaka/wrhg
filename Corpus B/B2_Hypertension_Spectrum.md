## 0.1 Elevated Blood Pressure

**D:** Persistently raised arterial blood pressure, diagnosed on repeated measurements or on ambulatory or home monitoring rather than on a single clinic reading.

> [!danger] Most "hypertension" is measured badly
> Measurement error is large enough to change management. The common faults, each of which can shift a reading substantially:
> **· Cuff too small** — the commonest error, and it **overestimates**. Cuff too large underestimates.
> **· Arm unsupported or below heart level** — overestimates. **· Back or feet unsupported** — overestimates.
> **· Talking during measurement**, by patient or clinician — overestimates.
> **· Legs crossed · full bladder · caffeine, smoking or exercise beforehand · inadequate rest period.**
> **A diagnosis of hypertension should never rest on one reading in one arm on one visit.** `UNVERIFIED — required rest period, number of readings and the interval between visits required for diagnosis.`

> [!tip] Measure both arms at least once
> A **persistent inter-arm difference** suggests subclavian stenosis, coarctation or aortic dissection, and once identified, the **arm with the higher reading** is the one used thereafter.

> [!info] White coat and masked hypertension
> **White coat hypertension** — raised in clinic, normal outside. Not entirely benign, but does not warrant the same treatment.
> **Masked hypertension** — normal in clinic, raised outside. **This is the dangerous one**, because it carries the cardiovascular risk of hypertension and goes untreated.
> **Ambulatory (ABPM) or home (HBPM) monitoring is the diagnostic standard**, and it is what distinguishes these. Out-of-office thresholds are **lower** than clinic thresholds — using clinic numbers to interpret home readings over-diagnoses. `UNVERIFIED — clinic, ABPM daytime, ABPM 24-hour and HBPM diagnostic thresholds all differ and require verification.`

> [!warning] Australia treats on absolute cardiovascular risk, not on blood pressure alone
> The Australian approach calculates **absolute 5-year cardiovascular disease risk** — incorporating age, sex, smoking, lipids, diabetes, blood pressure and other factors — and uses that to guide treatment. **A modestly raised blood pressure in a high-risk patient warrants treatment where the same reading in a low-risk young person may not.**
> The calculator was revised in recent years and the current Australian tool has different inputs and outputs from the older Framingham-derived version. It also includes **specific considerations for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults**, who are recommended for risk assessment from a younger age. `UNVERIFIED — the current calculator, its risk category thresholds, the age at which assessment begins for different populations, and the treatment thresholds for each category all require verification.`

**A/P:** Blood pressure is the product of cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. Essential hypertension involves a shift in the renal pressure-natriuresis relationship, increased sympathetic tone, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation, and arterial stiffening with age. Sustained elevation causes **arteriolar hyalinosis and hypertrophy**, accelerated atherosclerosis, left ventricular hypertrophy, and endothelial injury — producing the end-organ damage in brain, heart, kidney, retina and large vessels that is the actual reason for treating.

**S/Smx:** Almost always asymptomatic — which is the whole difficulty. Headache attributed to hypertension is usually coincidental at ordinary levels. Symptoms suggest either very severe elevation or a secondary cause.

**Ix:** Confirmatory ABPM or HBPM (*why:* distinguishes true, white coat and masked hypertension, and out-of-office readings predict outcomes better than clinic readings; *what:* average out-of-office pressure). UEC with eGFR and urine albumin-creatinine ratio (*why:* the kidney is both a cause and a target of hypertension, and albuminuria is an early marker of end-organ damage and a strong cardiovascular risk marker; *what:* creatinine, eGFR, albuminuria). Fasting lipids and HbA1c or fasting glucose (*why:* required to calculate absolute cardiovascular risk, which drives the treatment decision; *what:* lipid profile, glycaemia). ECG (*why:* left ventricular hypertrophy indicates established end-organ damage and raises risk; *what:* LVH, prior infarction, arrhythmia). FBC (*why:* anaemia and polycythaemia; *what:* haemoglobin). TFTs (*why:* thyroid disease affects blood pressure and is easily excluded; *what:* TSH). Fundoscopy (*why:* hypertensive retinopathy grades end-organ damage and, at the severe end, defines a hypertensive emergency; *what:* arteriolar narrowing, AV nipping, haemorrhages, exudates, papilloedema). Echocardiography where LVH is suspected or symptoms suggest heart failure (*why:* quantifies hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction; *what:* wall thickness, diastolic parameters). Screening for secondary causes where indicated — see 0.4 (*why:* secondary hypertension is more common than historically appreciated and often specifically treatable; *what:* aldosterone-renin ratio, metanephrines, imaging).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Not usually required. Severe asymptomatic elevation is managed as 0.2, **not** as an emergency.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
**Lifestyle intervention for everyone**, and it is genuinely effective: sodium reduction, weight loss, regular physical activity, alcohol reduction, smoking cessation, and dietary pattern change. Pharmacotherapy guided by absolute risk and by comorbidity — ACE inhibitors or ARBs, calcium channel blockers, and thiazide-like diuretics are the mainstays, with agent choice influenced by diabetes, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, gout, ethnicity and pregnancy potential.
**Do not combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARB** — the combination increases renal impairment, hyperkalaemia and hypotension without cardiovascular benefit.
**In pregnancy or planned pregnancy, ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated** and the agents used are different. Cross-refer `TODO:link — O2 Later pregnancy & fetal (unbuilt)`. `UNVERIFIED — all agents, doses, combinations and blood pressure targets from eTG Cardiovascular and the Heart Foundation guideline.`

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Adherence support — **non-adherence is the leading cause of apparently resistant hypertension**, and it is more usefully explored non-judgementally than assumed absent. Single-pill combinations improve adherence. Home monitoring engages patients. Regular review of renal function and electrolytes. ## 0.2 Hypertensive Urgency

**D:** Severely elevated blood pressure **without acute end-organ damage.**

> [!danger] The defining principle: do not lower it quickly
> **Asymptomatic severe hypertension without end-organ damage does not require intravenous therapy, does not require rapid reduction, and in most cases does not require admission.**
> Rapid lowering is actively harmful. Chronic hypertension **shifts the cerebral autoregulation curve to the right** — the brain has adapted to perfusing at a higher pressure, and its lower autoregulatory limit has risen with it. **Dropping the pressure abruptly to "normal" therefore drops it below that shifted limit and causes cerebral hypoperfusion**, with watershed infarction, blindness from optic nerve ischaemia, myocardial ischaemia and acute kidney injury. The same physiology explains why the target is gradual.
> Treating the number rather than the patient is one of the most common avoidable harms in this area.

> [!tip] Look for why it is high today
> **Non-adherence or running out of medication** — by far the commonest reason, and worth asking about directly and without judgement.
> **Pain · anxiety · urinary retention** — all raise blood pressure substantially and all resolve with treatment of the cause. **Check for a full bladder**; it is a genuinely common and instantly reversible cause of alarming readings in inpatients.
> **Drugs** — NSAIDs, decongestants, stimulants, cocaine, venlafaxine, corticosteroids, oral contraceptives, liquorice.
> **Withdrawal** — abrupt cessation of clonidine or a beta-blocker causes rebound hypertension; alcohol withdrawal likewise.
> **White coat effect in a distressed patient.**

**S/Smx:** By definition, none attributable to end-organ damage. The clinical task is a careful search for the features that would reclassify this as an emergency — see 0.3.

**Ix:** Repeat measurement after a period of rest, with correct technique (*why:* a substantial proportion of alarming readings fall considerably on repeat once the patient has sat quietly, and acting on the first reading leads to over-treatment; *what:* repeat value). Targeted assessment for end-organ damage — neurological examination, **fundoscopy**, cardiovascular examination, ECG, UEC and urinalysis (*why:* this is the assessment that separates urgency from emergency and therefore determines the entire management pathway; *what:* focal deficit, retinal haemorrhage or papilloedema, pulmonary oedema, ischaemic ECG change, rising creatinine, proteinuria or haematuria). Medication history including adherence and recent cessation (*why:* identifies the reversible cause in most cases; *what:* missed doses, withdrawal, new interacting drug). Bladder scan in an inpatient (*why:* retention is common, painful and instantly reversible; *what:* retained volume). Pregnancy test in a woman of reproductive age (*why:* pre-eclampsia is managed entirely differently and is a genuine emergency; *what:* positive test).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Rest, analgesia, relieve retention, address anxiety, and **recheck.** Restart or adjust oral antihypertensive therapy. **Avoid short-acting nifedipine capsules** and other agents causing abrupt uncontrolled falls.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Oral therapy with **gradual reduction over days to weeks**, not hours. Outpatient follow-up arranged before discharge, with a clear review interval.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Adherence support, secondary cause screening if indicated, and cardiovascular risk management. Ensure the patient leaves with a prescription they can actually fill and a follow-up appointment they can actually attend — cost and access are frequent contributors.

## 0.3 Hypertensive Emergency

**D:** Severe hypertension **with acute, ongoing end-organ damage.** The organ damage defines the emergency; the number alone does not.

> [!tip] The syndromes, and how they differ in management
> | Syndrome | Features | Management nuance |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Hypertensive encephalopathy** | Headache, confusion, visual disturbance, seizures, papilloedema; posterior changes on MRI (PRES) | Controlled gradual reduction |
> | **Acute ischaemic stroke** | Focal deficit | **Permissive hypertension** — lowering worsens penumbral perfusion, *unless* thrombolysis is planned, when a lower threshold applies |
> | **Intracerebral haemorrhage** | Focal deficit, headache, reduced GCS | Earlier and more active lowering than in ischaemic stroke |
> | **Acute pulmonary oedema (SCAPE)** | Severe dyspnoea, hypertensive, distressed | **Nitrates and NIV**, not primarily diuresis — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.3 |
> | **Acute coronary syndrome** | Chest pain, ECG change | Nitrates, beta-blockade |
> | **Aortic dissection** | Tearing pain, BP differential | **The exception — rapid, aggressive reduction. Rate control first, then vasodilator** |
> | **Acute kidney injury / malignant hypertension** | Rising creatinine, haematuria, proteinuria, retinopathy | Controlled reduction; renal function may worsen transiently |
> | **Pre-eclampsia / eclampsia** | Pregnancy, proteinuria, hyperreflexia, seizures | **Magnesium sulfate and delivery** — cross-refer `TODO:link — O2 Later pregnancy & fetal (unbuilt)` |
> | **Phaeochromocytoma crisis / sympathomimetic** | Paroxysmal, diaphoresis, palpitations | **Alpha blockade before beta blockade** — see 0.4 |

> [!danger] Fundoscopy is the examination that most often makes the diagnosis
> **Malignant (accelerated) hypertension** is defined by severe hypertension with **retinal haemorrhages, cotton wool spots, hard exudates or papilloedema.** It is the finding that converts an alarming number into an emergency, it is available at the bedside for free, and it is the assessment most often skipped. Look at the fundi in any patient with severe hypertension.

> [!warning] The rate of reduction is as important as the target
> Outside aortic dissection, the principle is a **controlled, partial reduction over the first hour, then a further gradual reduction over the following day**, using a **titratable intravenous agent in a monitored setting** so that an overshoot can be corrected within minutes. `UNVERIFIED — the percentage reduction targets for the first hour and the subsequent 24 hours, the specific targets in aortic dissection and in stroke with and without thrombolysis, and all agent choices and doses are omitted. Obtain from eTG and your health network's protocol.`
> Agents used include labetalol, glyceryl trinitrate, hydralazine, sodium nitroprusside and clevidipine. **Sodium nitroprusside accumulates as thiocyanate and cyanide with prolonged use or renal impairment**, which limits its duration of use and mandates monitoring.

> [!danger] Malignant hypertension can cause a microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia
> Severe hypertension damages endothelium and shears red cells, producing **anaemia with schistocytes and thrombocytopenia** — a picture that mimics thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura or haemolytic uraemic syndrome. Recognising hypertension as the cause avoids plasma exchange for a condition that responds to blood pressure control. Cross-refer `TODO:link — J1 Cytopenias & marrow failure (unbuilt)`.

**S/Smx:** Determined by the organ involved — headache, confusion, seizures, visual loss, focal deficit, chest pain, dyspnoea, oliguria, or the features of pre-eclampsia.

**Ix:** **Fundoscopy** (*why:* as above — grades retinopathy and may be the finding that establishes the emergency; *what:* haemorrhages, exudates, papilloedema). ECG and troponin (*why:* acute coronary syndrome and LV strain; *what:* ischaemic change, troponin rise). CXR (*why:* pulmonary oedema and widened mediastinum; *what:* oedema, mediastinal width). UEC with eGFR, and urinalysis with microscopy (*why:* acute kidney injury and an active urinary sediment establish renal involvement; *what:* rising creatinine, proteinuria, haematuria, red cell casts). **FBC with blood film and LDH** (*why:* identifies the microangiopathic haemolysis above; *what:* schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, raised LDH). CT or MRI brain (*why:* distinguishes ischaemic stroke, haemorrhage and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, each of which has a different blood pressure target; *what:* infarct, haemorrhage, posterior white matter change). CT aortogram where dissection is suspected (*why:* it is the exception to every other management rule here; *what:* intimal flap). Pregnancy test and, if positive, pre-eclampsia workup (*why:* entirely different management; *what:* proteinuria, LFTs, platelets, urate). Urinary or plasma metanephrines where paroxysmal features (*why:* phaeochromocytoma crisis requires alpha blockade first; *what:* raised metanephrines). Toxicology (*why:* stimulant-induced hypertensive crisis is managed with benzodiazepines rather than beta-blockade; *what:* cocaine, amphetamines).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Monitored setting with arterial line where feasible. **Identify the syndrome first**, because it determines the target and the agent. Titratable IV therapy with controlled reduction. Treat the specific condition — magnesium and delivery in eclampsia, nitrates and NIV in SCAPE, rate then pressure control in dissection, permissive hypertension in ischaemic stroke.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific, with ICU or HDU involvement, transition to oral therapy once stable, and specialty referral.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Screen for a secondary cause** — hypertensive emergency is considerably more likely than routine hypertension to have one. Establish long-term control with follow-up, and address the adherence or access problem that frequently underlies it.

## 0.4 Paroxysmal Hypertension and Secondary Causes

**D:** Episodic or sustained hypertension caused by an identifiable underlying condition rather than essential hypertension.

> [!tip] When to suspect a secondary cause
> **· Onset before roughly age 30, or new onset after 60** · **severe or resistant hypertension** — uncontrolled despite three agents including a diuretic · **sudden worsening** in previously stable hypertension · **hypokalaemia**, spontaneous or diuretic-provoked · end-organ damage disproportionate to the recorded pressure · **paroxysmal symptoms** · a hypertensive emergency · an abdominal bruit · features of an endocrine syndrome.
> `UNVERIFIED — the definition of resistant hypertension and the age thresholds require verification.`

> [!warning] Primary aldosteronism is far more common than traditionally taught
> Historically considered rare, it is now recognised in a substantial minority of hypertensive patients, and a larger proportion of resistant hypertension. **Hypokalaemia is present in only a minority — its absence does not exclude the diagnosis**, which is the single most important updated point here.
> Screening is by the **aldosterone-to-renin ratio**, and interpretation is complicated because many antihypertensives interfere with it — **beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics and particularly mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists** all affect the result, so the drugs a hypertensive patient is already on are exactly the ones that confound the test. `UNVERIFIED — which drugs must be withheld and for how long before testing, and the ratio thresholds, require verification and specialist input.`
> It matters because it is **specifically treatable** — surgically curable if unilateral, and highly responsive to mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism if bilateral — and because it carries excess cardiovascular risk beyond that of the blood pressure alone.

> [!danger] Phaeochromocytoma — alpha blockade before beta blockade
> **The classic triad is episodic headache, palpitations and diaphoresis**, often with pallor rather than flushing, anxiety and a sense of doom, occurring in paroxysms against a background of sustained or labile hypertension.
> **Giving a beta-blocker first is dangerous.** Blocking β2-mediated vasodilatation leaves catecholamine-driven **unopposed alpha vasoconstriction**, precipitating a hypertensive crisis. **Alpha blockade is established first, and beta blockade is added afterwards** for reflex tachycardia. This is the same unopposed-alpha principle that appears in cocaine toxicity and coronary vasospasm, and it is examined repeatedly.
> Screening is by **plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary metanephrines**, which are more sensitive than catecholamines. False positives are common with tricyclics, levodopa, stimulants, and physiological stress, so pre-test conditions matter. Associated with familial syndromes — **MEN2, von Hippel-Lindau, neurofibromatosis type 1, SDH mutations** — so a positive result warrants genetic consideration and family screening.

> [!tip] The other secondary causes worth knowing
> **Renal parenchymal disease** — the commonest secondary cause overall. Chronic kidney disease of any aetiology.
> **Renovascular** — **atherosclerotic** in older patients with vascular disease, and **fibromuscular dysplasia** in younger women, classically with a "string of beads" appearance on angiography. Suspect with an abdominal bruit, or **a significant rise in creatinine after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB**, which suggests bilateral disease.
> **Obstructive sleep apnoea** — very common, under-diagnosed, and a major contributor to resistant hypertension.
> **Cushing syndrome** — including **exogenous corticosteroids**, which are the commonest cause and are on the medication list.
> **Coarctation of the aorta** — upper limb hypertension with **radiofemoral delay** and weak femoral pulses. Classically diagnosed in young adults after years of "essential" hypertension because nobody felt the femoral pulses. **Feel the femorals in any young hypertensive patient.**
> **Thyroid disease** — both directions. **Hyperparathyroidism.** **Acromegaly.**
> **Drugs** — NSAIDs, combined oral contraceptives, venlafaxine and other SNRIs, corticosteroids, decongestants, stimulants, ciclosporin, erythropoietin, and **liquorice**, which causes an apparent mineralocorticoid excess.

**S/Smx:** Paroxysmal headache, palpitations, sweating and pallor in phaeochromocytoma. Muscle weakness, cramps and polyuria in aldosteronism with hypokalaemia. Snoring and daytime somnolence in OSA. Cushingoid features. Otherwise asymptomatic.

**Ix:** **Plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary metanephrines** (*why:* the sensitive screening test for phaeochromocytoma, which must be excluded before any surgical or invasive management and before beta blockade; *what:* markedly raised metanephrines). **Aldosterone-to-renin ratio** with attention to interfering medications (*why:* screens for primary aldosteronism, which is common, treatable and frequently missed; *what:* raised ratio requiring confirmatory testing). UEC with potassium, and acid-base (*why:* hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis supports mineralocorticoid excess, though its absence does not exclude it; *what:* low potassium, alkalosis). Renal ultrasound with Doppler, or CT/MR angiography (*why:* identifies renal parenchymal disease, size asymmetry and renal artery stenosis; *what:* small kidney, stenosis, fibromuscular changes). Overnight dexamethasone suppression test or 24-hour urinary free cortisol (*why:* screens for Cushing syndrome; *what:* failure of suppression). TFTs and calcium (*why:* thyroid and parathyroid disease; *what:* TSH, calcium, PTH). Sleep study (*why:* OSA is a common and treatable contributor to resistant hypertension; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index). Echocardiography and **femoral pulse examination**, with CT or MR aortography if coarctation is suspected (*why:* coarctation is curable and is diagnosed by an examination finding that costs nothing; *what:* radiofemoral delay, aortic narrowing). Adrenal imaging **only after biochemical confirmation** (*why:* adrenal incidentalomas are common and imaging first leads to investigating a non-functioning nodule while missing the real cause; *what:* adenoma, hyperplasia, mass).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
For a phaeochromocytoma crisis: **alpha blockade first**, then beta blockade, in a monitored setting. For stimulant-induced crisis: benzodiazepines and vasodilators, avoiding beta-blockade.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific and often curative — adrenalectomy after adequate alpha blockade for phaeochromocytoma, adrenalectomy or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist for primary aldosteronism, revascularisation or medical therapy for renovascular disease, CPAP for OSA, repair of coarctation, cessation of the causative drug. Endocrinology and hypertension specialist involvement.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Genetic testing and family screening** for phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma, given the substantial proportion with a heritable syndrome. Long-term surveillance for recurrence. Continued cardiovascular risk management, since the vascular damage accumulated before diagnosis does not reverse with cure of the cause.
