## 0.1 Airway Compromise — Recognition and Localisation

**D:** Partial or complete obstruction to airflow anywhere from the nose to the terminal bronchioles, threatening oxygenation and ventilation.

**A/P:** Airflow through a narrowing becomes turbulent → turbulence generates sound → **the character and timing of the sound localises the level of obstruction**, because extrathoracic and intrathoracic airways behave differently across the respiratory cycle. Extrathoracic airways (above the thoracic inlet) collapse further during inspiration, when intraluminal pressure falls below atmospheric → inspiratory noise. Intrathoracic airways are compressed during expiration by rising pleural pressure → expiratory noise.

> [!tip] The noise tells you the level
> | Noise | Level | Timing | Typical causes |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Stertor** (snoring, coarse) | Above the larynx — nose, pharynx | Inspiratory | Reduced consciousness, tonsillar hypertrophy, OSA, soft tissue swelling |
> | **Stridor** (harsh, high-pitched) | Larynx, subglottis, trachea | Inspiratory if extrathoracic; expiratory or biphasic if intrathoracic or fixed | Croup, epiglottitis, foreign body, anaphylaxis, tumour |
> | **Wheeze** (musical, polyphonic) | Lower airways | Expiratory | Asthma, COPD, bronchiolitis |
> | **Grunting** | Not obstruction — expiration against a partly closed glottis to generate auto-PEEP | Expiratory | Any significant respiratory distress in a child; a serious sign |

> [!danger] Red flags demanding immediate senior and anaesthetic involvement
> Drooling and inability to swallow saliva · tripod or sniffing posture · refusal to lie flat · voice change or muffled voice · agitation or exhaustion · **a silent chest or diminishing stridor with worsening effort** — the noise disappears when airflow becomes insufficient to generate it, so a quietening patient is deteriorating, not improving · cyanosis, a late sign.

> [!danger] Do not distress the patient with suspected supraglottic obstruction
> In suspected epiglottitis or severe croup, particularly in a child: **no throat examination, no cannulation, no forced positioning, no separation from the parent.** Distress increases oxygen demand and can precipitate complete obstruction in an airway held open by muscle tone alone. Keep the child upright on the parent's lap, give oxygen if tolerated, and get anaesthetics and ENT to the bedside for examination in a controlled environment — theatre, not the resus bay.

**S/Smx:** Noisy breathing as above, accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, intercostal and subcostal recession, nasal flaring, restlessness then drowsiness, and a rising then falling respiratory rate as fatigue develops.

**Ix:** Clinical assessment, prioritised over any test (*why:* obstruction progresses faster than investigations return, and a patient sent to radiology can obstruct in the scanner; *what:* level, severity, trajectory). Oxygen saturation (*why:* useful but reassuring for too long — it is preserved until obstruction is severe; *what:* hypoxia as a late sign). Nasendoscopy by ENT (*why:* directly visualises supraglottic and glottic pathology and is the definitive assessment of the upper airway; *what:* oedema, mass, cord movement, level of narrowing). Lateral soft tissue neck radiograph **only in a stable patient** (*why:* supportive for epiglottitis and retropharyngeal abscess, but must never delay airway management or move an unstable patient away from help; *what:* thumbprint sign, widened prevertebral soft tissue, steeple sign). CT neck with contrast in the stable patient (*why:* defines deep neck space collections, masses and tracheal narrowing; *what:* collection, mass, luminal calibre). ABG, reserved for the deteriorating (*why:* rising CO₂ indicates ventilatory failure, though obtaining it should not delay definitive airway management; *what:* hypercapnia).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen. Position of comfort — do not force supine. **Call for anaesthetic and ENT help at the moment of recognition.** Prepare for a difficult airway including a surgical airway. Treat the reversible cause: adrenaline for anaphylaxis, nebulised adrenaline and steroid for croup, removal of a foreign body.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Definitive airway secured in a controlled setting — usually inhalational induction in theatre with ENT scrubbed and ready for a surgical airway. Cross-refer [[F0.4]] 0.6.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Treat the underlying cause and document the airway difficulty encountered.

## 0.2 Acute Upper Airway Obstruction

**D:** Sudden obstruction at or above the level of the trachea, threatening complete airway loss.

**R/Causes:** Foreign body · anaphylaxis · angioedema, including the bradykinin-mediated form caused by ACE inhibitors and hereditary angioedema · epiglottitis and supraglottitis · deep neck space infection including Ludwig's angina and retropharyngeal abscess · **expanding post-operative neck haematoma** after thyroidectomy or carotid endarterectomy · inhalation and thermal airway injury · laryngeal trauma · bilateral vocal cord palsy · tumour with acute decompensation · reduced conscious state with loss of tone.

**A/P:** Oedema, mass or blood narrows the airway → resistance to flow rises steeply as radius falls, since flow varies with the fourth power of the radius → **a small further reduction in an already narrowed airway produces a disproportionate and often sudden deterioration.** This non-linearity is why patients appear to compensate and then collapse abruptly rather than declining smoothly.

> [!danger] Expanding post-operative neck haematoma
> After thyroidectomy or carotid endarterectomy, an expanding haematoma compresses the airway and obstructs venous drainage, causing laryngeal oedema that persists even once the clot is removed. **This is one of the few situations where a junior doctor should open the wound at the bedside** — remove the clips or sutures, evacuate the clot, and call for help simultaneously. Waiting for theatre has killed patients. Know where the clip removers are kept on your surgical ward before you need them.

> [!warning] ACE inhibitor angioedema does not behave like anaphylaxis
> It is **bradykinin-mediated, not histamine-mediated**, so adrenaline, antihistamines and steroids are largely ineffective — though adrenaline is still given where anaphylaxis cannot be excluded. It characteristically involves the lips, tongue and larynx without urticaria, can occur after years of uneventful use, and progresses over hours. The management is airway protection and cessation of the drug. `UNVERIFIED — the role and availability of specific bradykinin-pathway agents (icatibant, C1 esterase inhibitor) in Australian practice requires verification; cross-refer [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Hereditary angioedema.`

> [!danger] Inhalation injury — intubate early, not later
> Facial burns, singed nasal hair or eyebrows, carbonaceous sputum, soot in the oropharynx, hoarse voice, stridor, or burns sustained in an enclosed space. Airway oedema develops progressively over hours and an airway that is manageable now may be impossible in two hours. **Early elective intubation is the correct decision**, and deferring it is the recognised error. Cross-refer [[A7]] Burns.

**S/Smx:** Stridor, voice change or loss, drooling, dysphagia and odynophagia, tripod posture, agitation, accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, and neck swelling or asymmetry.

**Ix:** Assessment at the bedside with anaesthetics and ENT present (*why:* the airway decision is clinical and urgent, and investigations that move the patient away from help are dangerous; *what:* level and severity). Flexible nasendoscopy (*why:* directly visualises the supraglottis and cords, distinguishing oedema from mass from cord palsy, and is safe in a cooperative adult; *what:* supraglottic oedema, mass, cord movement). Wound and neck inspection with drain output review in the post-operative patient (*why:* identifies the expanding haematoma requiring immediate decompression; *what:* tense swelling, high drain output, then a drain that suddenly stops — an ominous sign of clot). Serum tryptase if anaphylaxis is possible (*why:* retrospective confirmation; *what:* acute rise — cross-refer [[F0.1]] 0.9). CT neck with contrast in the stable patient (*why:* defines deep neck space infection requiring drainage; *what:* rim-enhancing collection, airway deviation). C1 esterase inhibitor level and function where recurrent angioedema without urticaria (*why:* identifies hereditary angioedema, which requires entirely different treatment; *what:* low level or function).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen, position of comfort, immediate anaesthetic and ENT involvement, and preparation for a surgical airway. Adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Open the wound for post-operative neck haematoma. Nebulised adrenaline may temporise laryngeal oedema. `UNVERIFIED — all doses from ASCIA and eTG.`

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Definitive airway in theatre. Drainage of collections. Cessation of the causative drug. Surgical exploration for haematoma.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Document the cause and the airway difficulty. Permanent medical alert and allergy documentation. ACE inhibitor angioedema is a class contraindication for life — document it prominently, since re-prescription is a recognised harm.

## 0.3 Chronic Upper Airway Obstruction

**D:** Progressive narrowing of the upper airway developing over weeks to years, often presenting only when a further insult tips it into acute failure.

**R/Causes:** Laryngeal, hypopharyngeal or thyroid malignancy · large or retrosternal goitre · **post-intubation or post-tracheostomy tracheal stenosis** · bilateral vocal cord palsy, most often after thyroid surgery · obstructive sleep apnoea · granulomatosis with polyangiitis · in children, laryngomalacia, subglottic stenosis and vascular rings.

**A/P:** Gradual narrowing permits physiological adaptation → the patient reduces activity, alters their breathing pattern and normalises their symptoms → **presentation is late and is often precipitated by a viral illness, secretions or sedation** that removes the remaining margin. This is why an apparently sudden presentation may have a long history behind it.

> [!warning] Tracheal stenosis is under-recognised
> A patient treated repeatedly for "asthma" that does not respond to bronchodilators, with **biphasic stridor or a fixed monophonic wheeze** and a history of previous intubation or tracheostomy, has tracheal stenosis until proven otherwise. Flow-volume loops distinguish fixed extrathoracic, variable extrathoracic and intrathoracic obstruction, and are useful precisely when the diagnosis is being missed.

> [!tip] Bilateral vocal cord palsy after thyroid surgery
> Unilateral palsy gives hoarseness. **Bilateral palsy gives a near-normal voice with stridor**, because the cords sit in the midline — which is exactly why it is missed. New stridor after thyroidectomy with a preserved voice is bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve injury until excluded.

**S/Smx:** Progressive exertional dyspnoea, stridor initially only on exertion, voice change, dysphagia, cough, reduced exercise tolerance, weight loss and neck mass in malignancy, and snoring with witnessed apnoeas in OSA.

**Ix:** Flexible nasendoscopy (*why:* the definitive assessment of the larynx and cord movement, and it is an outpatient procedure; *what:* mass, cord palsy, narrowing level). CT neck and chest (*why:* defines the level, length and cause of narrowing and stages malignancy; *what:* stenosis, mass, extrinsic compression). Flow-volume loops (*why:* characterises fixed versus variable and extrathoracic versus intrathoracic obstruction, which no other pulmonary function measure does, and identifies the "asthma" that is not asthma; *what:* flattened inspiratory or expiratory limb). Thyroid function and ultrasound where goitre is present (*why:* determines whether the mass is functional and whether it extends retrosternally; *what:* nodule, retrosternal extension). Sleep study where OSA is suspected (*why:* confirms and grades OSA and determines CPAP need; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index — cross-refer [[13_05b_ENT_-_Stridor__Croup__Epiglottitis__Laryngomalacia__OSA]]). Biopsy of any mass (*why:* directs oncological management; *what:* histology).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
If presenting acutely decompensated, manage as per 0.2. Otherwise oxygen, avoid sedatives, and refer.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific: tumour resection or oncological management, tracheal dilatation or resection, cord lateralisation or tracheostomy for bilateral palsy, thyroidectomy for compressive goitre, CPAP for OSA.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Flag the difficult airway prominently in the medical record**, since these patients present for unrelated surgery and an unanticipated difficult airway in that setting is dangerous.

## 0.4 Lower Airway Obstruction

**D:** Obstruction distal to the trachea, at bronchial or bronchiolar level.

**R/Causes:** Asthma and COPD (the great majority) · bronchiolitis in infants · **inhaled foreign body** · anaphylaxis · endobronchial tumour · mucus plugging · aspiration.

**A/P:** Narrowing of small airways → expiratory flow limitation, since intrathoracic airways are compressed during expiration → air trapping and hyperinflation → the diaphragm flattens and works at mechanical disadvantage → increased work of breathing and V/Q mismatch.

> [!danger] Inhaled foreign body
> **Unilateral wheeze or reduced air entry in a child is a foreign body until proven otherwise**, particularly with a history of choking while eating — though the choking episode is unwitnessed or forgotten in a substantial proportion. Peanuts and small toy parts are typical. The classic radiographic finding is **unilateral hyperinflation on an expiratory film**, from a ball-valve effect that lets air in but not out; the inspiratory film may be entirely normal. A normal chest radiograph does not exclude it, and bronchoscopy is both the diagnostic and therapeutic step.

> [!warning] Not all wheeze is asthma
> Monophonic wheeze localised to one area suggests fixed obstruction — foreign body or tumour — rather than the polyphonic widespread wheeze of asthma. Wheeze that has never responded to a bronchodilator deserves a different diagnosis rather than a higher dose.

**S/Smx:** Expiratory wheeze, prolonged expiratory phase, cough, dyspnoea, hyperinflated chest, accessory muscle use. In infants: recession, grunting, poor feeding, apnoea.

**Ix:** Auscultation for the pattern and symmetry of wheeze (*why:* asymmetry is the finding that redirects you from asthma toward foreign body or tumour; *what:* unilateral versus widespread). CXR, with inspiratory and expiratory films where foreign body is suspected (*why:* the expiratory film reveals the air trapping that the inspiratory film conceals, and most inhaled objects are radiolucent; *what:* unilateral hyperinflation, mediastinal shift, collapse). Oxygen saturation and, in severe cases, blood gas (*why:* severity and rising CO₂; *what:* hypoxia, hypercapnia). Peak flow or spirometry where feasible (*why:* objectively grades reversible obstruction; *what:* reduced values with bronchodilator response). Flexible or rigid bronchoscopy (*why:* diagnostic and therapeutic for foreign body and endobronchial lesions; *what:* object, tumour, plug). Nasopharyngeal aspirate for viral PCR in infants (*why:* confirms bronchiolitis and supports cohorting; *what:* RSV and other viruses).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Bronchodilators and steroids for asthma and COPD — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.1 and 0.2. Adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Urgent ENT or thoracic referral for suspected foreign body — **do not attempt blind finger sweeps or postural drainage in a stable patient with a partial obstruction**, as this can convert partial to complete obstruction.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Bronchoscopic removal of foreign body. Disease-specific management otherwise.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Preventer optimisation, inhaler technique, action plan, and choking prevention advice for families of young children.

## 0.5 Acute Stridor

**D:** New harsh inspiratory or biphasic noise indicating laryngeal, subglottic or tracheal narrowing.

> [!tip] Differential by age — the highest-yield framing
> **Neonate:** laryngomalacia (usually chronic, positional, improves prone), subglottic stenosis, vocal cord palsy, vascular ring, laryngeal web.
> **6 months – 3 years:** **croup** is the commonest by a wide margin · inhaled foreign body · bacterial tracheitis · retropharyngeal abscess · anaphylaxis.
> **Any child:** epiglottitis — now rare in immunised populations following Hib vaccination, but consider it in the unimmunised · anaphylaxis · burns and inhalation injury.
> **Adult:** anaphylaxis and angioedema · **adult supraglottitis, which is now more common than paediatric epiglottitis** · tumour · post-extubation oedema · vocal cord dysfunction · deep neck space infection.

> [!info] Croup versus epiglottitis
> | | Croup | Epiglottitis |
> |---|---|---|
> | Onset | Gradual over 1–2 days, worse at night | Rapid, hours |
> | Prodrome | Coryza | Little or none |
> | Cough | **Barking, seal-like** | Absent or minimal |
> | Voice | Hoarse | **Muffled, "hot potato"** |
> | Drooling | No | **Yes** |
> | Posture | Any | **Tripod, sitting forward** |
> | Appearance | Usually well between spasms | Toxic, still, frightened |
> | Action | Steroid, nebulised adrenaline if severe | **Do not examine; theatre with ENT and anaesthetics** |

> [!warning] Vocal cord dysfunction
> Inspiratory stridor with normal oxygen saturation, no response to bronchodilators, and often a history of "asthma" that never improves. Frequently exercise- or stress-related, and diagnosed on nasendoscopy during an episode. It is a genuine and under-diagnosed cause, and treating it as asthma leads to years of unnecessary steroids.

**S/Smx:** Inspiratory stridor if extrathoracic, biphasic if fixed or subglottic. Associated features per the table. Severity is graded on stridor at rest versus on exertion, recession, agitation, and oxygen saturation.

**Ix:** Clinical assessment without distressing the patient (*why:* examination can precipitate complete obstruction in supraglottic disease; *what:* severity grade, level). Oxygen saturation obtained non-invasively if tolerated (*why:* preserved until late, so its normality is not reassurance; *what:* hypoxia). Flexible nasendoscopy in a controlled setting (*why:* directly visualises the level and cause; *what:* epiglottic swelling, cord movement, mass). Lateral neck radiograph **only in a stable patient and never in place of airway management** (*why:* supportive for epiglottitis and retropharyngeal abscess; *what:* thumbprint sign, widened prevertebral tissue). Inspiratory and expiratory CXR where foreign body is suspected (*why:* reveals air trapping; *what:* unilateral hyperinflation). Blood cultures where bacterial tracheitis or epiglottitis is suspected, **taken after the airway is secured** (*why:* organism identification, but venepuncture distresses a child and can precipitate obstruction; *what:* organism). Immunisation history (*why:* Hib status materially changes the probability of epiglottitis; *what:* completed schedule or not).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Keep the patient calm and upright with a parent if a child. Oxygen if tolerated without distress. **Corticosteroid for croup**, with nebulised adrenaline for moderate to severe. Adrenaline for anaphylaxis. For suspected epiglettitis, bacterial tracheitis or severe obstruction: senior anaesthetic and ENT to the bedside, and airway secured in theatre. `UNVERIFIED — dexamethasone and prednisolone dosing for croup, nebulised adrenaline dose and repeat interval, and severity grading criteria are omitted; obtain from RCH and local paediatric guidelines.`

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Antibiotics for bacterial causes. Bronchoscopy for foreign body. Drainage of abscess.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Croup is recurrent in some children and parents should have written advice on recognition and when to return. Immunisation catch-up where incomplete. Speech pathology referral for vocal cord dysfunction.

## 0.6 Chronic Stridor

**D:** Persistent or recurrent stridor over weeks to months.

**R/Causes:** In infants, **laryngomalacia is by far the commonest** — inspiratory stridor from birth or the first weeks, worse when supine, feeding, crying or with intercurrent illness, and better prone. Also: subglottic stenosis (particularly following neonatal intubation), vocal cord palsy, vascular ring or sling, subglottic haemangioma (which may enlarge over the first months), laryngeal cleft or web, recurrent respiratory papillomatosis. In adults: tumour, tracheal stenosis, bilateral cord palsy, external compression by goitre or mediastinal mass.

**A/P:** A structurally narrowed or dynamically collapsing airway produces turbulent flow at rest or on exertion. In laryngomalacia, immature and floppy supraglottic tissues are drawn inward during inspiration → intermittent obstruction that improves as the cartilage stiffens with growth.

> [!warning] Which infant with stridor needs investigation rather than reassurance
> Laryngomalacia is common and usually self-limiting, but reassurance is only safe when the pattern fits. **Investigate if there is: poor weight gain or feeding difficulty, cyanotic episodes, apnoea, biphasic or expiratory stridor rather than inspiratory, stridor that is worsening rather than plateauing, a weak or absent cry, or onset after the neonatal period.** Any of these warrants ENT referral and nasendoscopy rather than watchful waiting.

**S/Smx:** Persistent stridor with the positional and feeding pattern described, feeding difficulty, failure to thrive in significant cases, and in adults progressive exertional dyspnoea and voice change.

**Ix:** Flexible nasendoscopy (*why:* the diagnostic test — visualises the supraglottis dynamically and confirms laryngomalacia or identifies an alternative; *what:* omega-shaped epiglottis with inspiratory collapse, or a structural lesion). Microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy under anaesthesia (*why:* assesses below the cords, which nasendoscopy cannot, and identifies subglottic and tracheal pathology; *what:* stenosis, haemangioma, cleft). Growth chart plotting (*why:* faltering growth converts a benign-sounding diagnosis into one requiring intervention; *what:* weight trajectory crossing centiles). Sleep study or overnight oximetry (*why:* quantifies nocturnal obstruction and desaturation; *what:* desaturation episodes). Echocardiography and CT or MR angiography where vascular ring is suspected (*why:* identifies a surgically correctable extrinsic compression; *what:* vascular ring or sling). CT neck and chest in adults (*why:* identifies tumour or compression; *what:* mass, luminal narrowing).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Rarely needed. Manage intercurrent illness that decompensates the airway.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Observation for uncomplicated laryngomalacia, which typically resolves over the first one to two years. Supraglottoplasty for severe cases. Surgical or endoscopic management of structural lesions.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Growth and feeding monitoring, parental education on when to return, and ENT follow-up.

## 0.7 Noisy Breathing — The Discriminating Approach

**D:** An undifferentiated presentation, common in general practice and paediatrics, where the diagnostic task is to identify which noise is present before considering causes.

> [!tip] Work through it in this order
> **1. When in the cycle?** Inspiratory, expiratory or both.
> **2. What quality?** Coarse and snoring (stertor), harsh and high-pitched (stridor), musical (wheeze), or a short expiratory grunt.
> **3. Where is it loudest?** Over the neck suggests upper airway; over the chest suggests lower.
> **4. Is it positional?** Improved prone suggests laryngomalacia; worse supine suggests pharyngeal collapse or OSA.
> **5. Is the patient distressed?** A noisy but comfortable, feeding, growing child is a very different problem from a noisy child with recession and poor feeding.

> [!warning] The two errors this framework prevents
> **Calling stridor "wheeze"** and treating an upper airway obstruction with bronchodilators, which delays the correct diagnosis and can be fatal in an evolving obstruction.
> **Dismissing grunting as noise.** Grunting is not an airway sound at all — it is expiration against a partially closed glottis to generate auto-PEEP, and in a child it signals significant respiratory distress or sepsis. A grunting infant needs urgent assessment.

**S/Smx:** As above, with the associated features determining urgency: feeding, growth, sleep disruption, exercise tolerance, voice, and the presence of recession or distress.

**Ix:** Structured history including a recording of the noise on the parent's phone (*why:* the noise is frequently absent in clinic, and a recording is genuinely diagnostic in distinguishing stertor from stridor from wheeze — this is a practical and underused step; *what:* character and timing). Growth chart (*why:* faltering growth signals a significant airway problem regardless of how benign the noise sounds; *what:* trajectory). Oxygen saturation, awake and ideally during sleep (*why:* identifies the nocturnal desaturation that daytime assessment misses; *what:* desaturation). Flexible nasendoscopy where stridor or stertor is persistent (*why:* the definitive upper airway assessment; *what:* level and cause). CXR where lower airway pathology is suspected (*why:* foreign body, consolidation, cardiomegaly; *what:* findings). Sleep study where OSA is suspected (*why:* confirms and grades; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Only where distress or hypoxia is present — then manage per the level identified.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific per 0.3 to 0.6.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Reassurance with clear return criteria where benign, and referral where the warning features in 0.6 are present.

## 0.8 Tracheostomy and Laryngectomy Emergency

**D:** Airway emergency in a patient with a surgical airway — obstruction, displacement, bleeding, or failure to ventilate.

> [!danger] The single most important question: is there a patent upper airway?
> **Tracheostomy** — the larynx is intact and the upper airway is patent. The patient **can** be oxygenated via the face and **can** usually be intubated orally.
> **Laryngectomy** — the larynx has been removed and the trachea is brought to the skin as an end stoma. **There is no connection between the mouth and the trachea.** Oxygen applied to the face achieves nothing, oral intubation is impossible, and all airway management must be via the stoma.
> Getting this wrong is fatal. Australian and UK hospitals use **bed-head signs** stating which the patient has, along with tube type and size — look for it, and if there is no sign, treat the stoma as the airway while checking.

> [!info] The emergency sequence
> Based on the National Tracheostomy Safety Project algorithms, which are the standard resource and should be the version you follow. `UNVERIFIED — obtain the current NTSP tracheostomy and laryngectomy emergency algorithms and your hospital's version; the sequence below is the principle, not a substitute for the algorithm.`
> **1.** Call for help — anaesthetics, ENT, ICU.
> **2.** Apply oxygen to **both** the face and the stoma in a tracheostomy patient; to the stoma only in a laryngectomy patient.
> **3.** Assess patency — look, listen, feel, and use waveform capnography, which is the most reliable indicator of a patent and correctly positioned tube.
> **4.** **Remove the inner cannula** — blockage by dried secretions is the commonest cause and this alone resolves many events.
> **5.** Pass a suction catheter. If it passes, suction. If it will not pass, the tube is blocked or displaced.
> **6.** Deflate the cuff — this may allow the patient to breathe around the tube if the upper airway is patent.
> **7.** If still obstructed, **remove the tube.** Then oxygenate and ventilate via the face for a tracheostomy patient, or via the stoma for a laryngectomy patient.

> [!warning] Bleeding from a tracheostomy
> Minor bleeding is common. **Significant or sentinel bleeding may herald a tracheo-innominate artery fistula** — a rare, catastrophic and time-critical event, most likely between roughly one and six weeks post-insertion. Any brisk bleed from a tracheostomy demands immediate senior surgical and ENT involvement, not observation. `UNVERIFIED — the emergency manoeuvres described for tracheo-innominate fistula, including cuff overinflation and digital compression, require verification against a current surgical source.`

> [!danger] A displaced tube in a fresh tracheostomy
> A tracheostomy less than about a week old has an immature tract that has not yet formed a stable channel. **Blind reinsertion can create a false passage into the pretracheal tissues**, which is often fatal and looks initially like a successful placement. In a fresh tracheostomy, reinsertion should be attempted only by someone competent to do so, ideally over a guide, and oxygenation via the upper airway is the safer immediate route where anatomy permits.

**S/Smx:** Respiratory distress, inability to pass a suction catheter, absent or abnormal capnography trace, visible tube displacement, surgical emphysema, bleeding, agitation, desaturation, and reduced or absent air movement at the tube.

**Ix:** Waveform capnography (*why:* the most reliable indicator that the tube is patent and in the trachea, and it distinguishes a blocked tube from a displaced one faster than any other test; *what:* trace present or absent). Attempted passage of a suction catheter (*why:* a simple bedside test of tube patency that also clears secretions; *what:* passes freely, or obstructs). Direct inspection and removal of the inner cannula (*why:* identifies the commonest and most easily reversible cause; *what:* secretions, crusting). Fibreoptic scope through the tube where available (*why:* confirms position within the trachea and excludes a false passage; *what:* tracheal rings and carina visible). CXR after any tube change or reinsertion (*why:* confirms position and excludes pneumothorax and surgical emphysema; *what:* tube position, pneumothorax). Bed-head sign and operation note (*why:* establishes tracheostomy versus laryngectomy, tube type, size and whether the tract is mature — the information every subsequent decision depends on; *what:* airway type and history).

### 0.8.1 Mx – Immediate
Follow the NTSP algorithm. Call for help first. Oxygen to the right places. Inner cannula out, suction catheter, cuff down, tube out if needed.

### 0.8.2 Mx – Definitive
Tube replacement by a competent operator, or an alternative airway. Surgical management of bleeding.

### 0.8.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Ensure the bed-head sign, emergency box and appropriate spare tubes are at the bedside at all times — checking this on your ward round is a genuinely useful intern habit. Tracheostomy care education for patient and carers. Cross-refer [[F0.4]] 0.6.
