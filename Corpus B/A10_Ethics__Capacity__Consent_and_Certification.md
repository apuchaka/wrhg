## 0.1 Capacity Assessment

**D:** The ability to make a particular decision at a particular time — not a global attribute of a person.

> [!tip] The four functional elements
> Capacity requires the person to be able to:
> **1. Understand** the information relevant to the decision.
> **2. Retain** it long enough to make the decision.
> **3. Use or weigh** it as part of the process of deciding.
> **4. Communicate** the decision.
> Failure of **any one** element, and only where it is caused by an impairment of mind or brain, means the person lacks capacity **for that decision.**

> [!danger] An unwise decision is not incapacity
> **Adults are presumed to have capacity, and a competent adult may make a decision that is unwise, irrational, contrary to medical advice, or fatal.** Disagreeing with the treating team is not evidence of incapacity, and "the patient lacks capacity because they are refusing an obviously beneficial treatment" is circular reasoning that appears frequently on wards and in exams.
> The trigger for assessment should be a **reason to doubt capacity** — confusion, an impairment of mind or brain, an inability to explain their reasoning — not the direction of the answer.

> [!warning] Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific
> A patient may have capacity to consent to a blood test and not to a laparotomy, because the second requires weighing more complex information. A patient with fluctuating delirium may have capacity in the morning and not in the evening. **Assess for the specific decision, at the time it needs to be made, and reassess.**

> [!tip] Optimise before you assess
> Before concluding a patient lacks capacity: treat pain, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, sepsis and delirium · provide hearing aids and glasses · use a **professional interpreter, not a family member** · choose the best time of day · reduce noise and distraction · use plain language, written information and diagrams · involve someone the patient trusts. A substantial proportion of apparent incapacity is remediable, and documenting that you attempted to remediate it is part of a defensible assessment.

> [!warning] Children and young people
> Capacity in minors is assessed by **maturity and understanding rather than by age alone** — the principle usually taught as Gillick competence. South Australia additionally has **statutory provisions on the age at which a young person may consent to medical treatment**, which differ from the common law position and from other states. `UNVERIFIED — the specific age thresholds, and the circumstances in which a second practitioner's opinion is required, must be verified against the Consent to Medical Treatment and Palliative Care Act 1995 (SA). Do not assume the position in another state applies.`
> Note that in most Australian jurisdictions a competent minor's **consent** and their **refusal** are not treated identically, which is a genuine legal complexity rather than an inconsistency.

**Ix:** Structured capacity assessment documented against the four elements (*why:* a conclusion recorded as "patient lacks capacity" without the reasoning is not defensible and cannot be reviewed by anyone else; *what:* what the patient was told, what they understood, how they reasoned, and the conclusion for that specific decision). Cognitive screening where relevant (*why:* supports but does not determine the assessment — **a low score on a cognitive screen does not by itself establish incapacity**, and a normal score does not exclude it; *what:* cognitive impairment as context). Delirium screen and search for reversible causes (*why:* delirium is the commonest cause of acute incapacity and is frequently reversible; *what:* infection, hypoxia, medication, metabolic derangement, urinary retention, constipation, pain). Collateral history (*why:* establishes baseline cognition, values and previously expressed wishes; *what:* usual function, prior statements about treatment). Medication review (*why:* sedatives, opioids and anticholinergics impair capacity and can be adjusted; *what:* contributing agents). Specialist psychiatric or geriatric assessment in complex or contested cases (*why:* difficult assessments benefit from expertise, though **capacity assessment is the responsibility of the treating clinician, not automatically a psychiatric referral**; *what:* opinion).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Optimise, assess for the specific decision, document the reasoning. Where capacity is present, the patient decides. Where it is absent, move to 0.3.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat reversible causes and **reassess**, since incapacity is often temporary and decisions that can wait, should.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Where incapacity is likely to persist, formal substitute decision-making arrangements and, where appropriate, an application to the **South Australian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (SACAT)** for guardianship. `UNVERIFIED — process and jurisdiction.`

## 0.2 Consent and Treatment Refusal

**D:** Valid consent requires that the patient has **capacity**, is **informed**, and decides **voluntarily**. Absence of any one invalidates it.

> [!info] The Australian standard for disclosure — Rogers v Whitaker
> Australian law uses a **patient-centred** rather than doctor-centred standard. A risk is **material**, and must be disclosed, if:
> **(a)** a **reasonable person in the patient's position** would be likely to attach significance to it; **or**
> **(b)** the doctor is or should reasonably be aware that **this particular patient** would attach significance to it.
> Limb (b) is the one that matters clinically: it means disclosure is tailored to the individual. A small risk to a hand is material to a concert pianist in a way it is not to most people, and the obligation follows from what you know about the patient. `UNVERIFIED — confirm the current statutory and common law position, including any civil liability legislation modifying it.`
> Practically: discuss the condition, the proposed treatment and what it involves, the material risks, the alternatives **including doing nothing**, and the likely outcome without treatment. Document it, and use an interpreter where needed.

> [!danger] A competent adult may refuse treatment that would save their life
> **This is settled and it applies even where the refusal appears irrational to the treating team, and even where death is the certain consequence.** Overriding it is an assault. The clinical obligations are to ensure the refusal is **informed** (they understand what will happen), **current**, **voluntary** (free from coercion — including from family), and made **with capacity**.
> What you can do: explore the reasons, address remediable concerns such as pain, fear or misinformation, offer alternatives, involve someone they trust, and revisit. What you cannot do: treat anyway.

> [!warning] Emergency treatment without consent
> Where a patient lacks capacity and treatment is **urgently necessary** to save life or prevent serious harm, and consent cannot be obtained in time, treatment may lawfully proceed. **Give no more than is necessary**, and seek proper consent or substitute consent as soon as practicable. `UNVERIFIED — the statutory basis and its limits in South Australia require verification against the Consent to Medical Treatment and Palliative Care Act 1995.`
> This does **not** apply where there is a valid, applicable and current refusal — an advance care directive refusing the specific treatment, for example.

> [!danger] A Mental Health Act does not authorise treatment of physical illness
> Detention or a treatment order under the **Mental Health Act 2009 (SA)** permits treatment **of the mental illness.** It does not authorise appendicectomy, transfusion or dialysis. If a detained patient lacks capacity to consent to physical treatment, the authority for that comes from the guardianship and consent framework, not from the mental health legislation. **Conflating the two is a common and serious error.** `UNVERIFIED — the precise scope of treatment authorised under the SA Mental Health Act requires verification.`

**Ix:** Capacity assessment as per 0.1 (*why:* consent and refusal are only valid with capacity, so this precedes everything; *what:* capacity for this decision). Exploration of the reasons for refusal (*why:* refusals frequently rest on a correctable misunderstanding, an unaddressed fear, uncontrolled pain, or a previous bad experience — and addressing those changes more decisions than argument does; *what:* the patient's actual reasoning). Assessment for coercion (*why:* consent must be voluntary, and family pressure — in either direction — is not always visible with relatives in the room; *what:* speak to the patient alone). Review for a valid advance care directive (*why:* a refusal recorded in a valid ACD is binding and may already answer the question; *what:* existence, currency and applicability of the directive). Documentation of the discussion (*why:* the record of what was discussed is the evidence that consent was informed; *what:* risks discussed, alternatives offered, patient's response).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Establish capacity, ensure the refusal is informed and voluntary, address remediable factors, and **document thoroughly.** Involve a senior clinician — this should not be an intern-alone decision.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Respect the decision of a competent patient. Offer alternatives and ongoing care — **refusal of one treatment is not refusal of all care**, and patients who decline the recommended option should still be offered symptom control, follow-up and an open door.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Encourage advance care planning so future refusals are documented in advance. Maintain the therapeutic relationship — a patient who refuses today may accept later, and will not return if the refusal was met with hostility.

## 0.3 Substitute Decision-Making and Advance Care Directives

**D:** The framework for who decides when an adult lacks capacity.

> [!tip] The order of authority — in principle
> **1. A valid, applicable advance care directive** made by the person when they had capacity. A **refusal** of treatment in a valid ACD is binding.
> **2. A substitute decision-maker appointed by the person** in that directive.
> **3. A guardian appointed by the tribunal**, with the scope of authority the order specifies.
> **4. A "person responsible"** under statute — typically a defined hierarchy of spouse or domestic partner, carer, relative or close friend.
> `UNVERIFIED — the exact hierarchy, the terminology, the documents that are valid in South Australia, and whether an interstate or older-format directive is recognised all require verification against the Advance Care Directives Act 2013 (SA) and the Guardianship and Administration Act 1993 (SA). Terminology differs substantially between states — "enduring guardian", "medical treatment decision maker", "person responsible" are not interchangeable across borders.`

> [!warning] Substitute decision-makers decide as the patient would have, not as they would
> The standard is **substituted judgement** — what would this person have wanted, based on their known values, beliefs and previously expressed wishes — not what the decision-maker wants, and not what the family finds easiest. Where the person's wishes are unknown, the standard shifts toward their **best interests.**
> This framing genuinely helps in difficult family conversations: **"What would your mother have said if she were sitting here?"** is a better and kinder question than "What do you want us to do?", which places an unbearable and inappropriate burden on the family.

> [!danger] Families do not consent to withholding futile treatment
> **A treatment that will not achieve its physiological purpose is not offered, and consent is not sought for not offering it.** Framing a decision not to escalate as though the family are choosing to end their relative's life causes lasting harm and is not an accurate account of the decision. The conversation is about **what will and will not help**, communicated clearly, with the family's views on the person's values genuinely sought and weighed.
> Disagreement is common and usually reflects communication failure, unrealistic expectations or unresolved grief. Escalate to senior clinicians, offer a second opinion, involve palliative care, and use formal dispute resolution or the tribunal where genuine conflict persists.

> [!info] Voluntary assisted dying
> Voluntary assisted dying is lawful in South Australia under the **Voluntary Assisted Dying Act 2021 (SA)**, with strict eligibility criteria and a defined process, and specific rules about who may raise it and how. `UNVERIFIED — eligibility criteria, the process, the practitioner training requirements, and the rules about initiating a discussion all require verification against the current Act and SA Health guidance. This is an area where the law is recent, detailed, and where getting the procedure wrong has serious consequences.` **Conscientious objection provisions exist**, and a practitioner who does not wish to participate still has obligations regarding information and non-obstruction.

**Ix:** Search for an existing advance care directive (*why:* it is binding if valid and applicable and frequently exists without the treating team knowing — check My Health Record, the GP, the residential aged care facility and the family; *what:* directive, its currency and scope). Identification of the correct substitute decision-maker (*why:* obtaining consent from the wrong person is not valid consent, and the statutory hierarchy is not simply "the next of kin"; *what:* appointed decision-maker, guardianship order, or person responsible). Collateral history about the person's values and previously expressed wishes (*why:* this is the substance of substituted judgement; *what:* prior statements, what mattered to them, previous decisions). Documentation of the goals-of-care discussion (*why:* it is the record that guides after-hours and weekend teams who were not present; *what:* agreed ceiling of care, resuscitation status, and the reasoning).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Identify the decision-maker. In an emergency, treat under the emergency provision while doing so.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Structured goals-of-care conversation with the decision-maker, documented on the relevant form. Involve palliative care early where the trajectory is poor — **early palliative care involvement improves symptom control and, in some contexts, survival**, and is not an admission of defeat.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Encourage completion of advance care directives in patients with chronic progressive illness, ideally in the outpatient setting rather than during an acute deterioration.

## 0.4 Neurologically Determined Death

**D:** Death determined by the **irreversible cessation of all function of the brain, including the brainstem** — legally and clinically death, equivalent to circulatory death.

**A/P:** Catastrophic brain injury → raised intracranial pressure exceeding mean arterial pressure → cerebral perfusion pressure falls to zero → global infarction of the brain including the brainstem → irreversible loss of consciousness, brainstem reflexes and the capacity to breathe. Cardiac activity continues only because the heart is not neurally dependent and is being artificially ventilated and supported.

> [!warning] The preconditions must be satisfied before testing — this is where errors occur
> Clinical determination is invalid unless: there is a **known cause** sufficient to explain irreversible brain damage · an adequate period has elapsed to exclude recovery · and all **confounders are excluded** — hypothermia, hypotension, **sedative and neuromuscular blocking drugs** (including residual effect in renal or hepatic impairment), severe metabolic or endocrine derangement, and severe hypoxia or hypercapnia at the time of testing.
> **Residual sedation is the commonest reason testing must be deferred**, and a nerve stimulator should confirm the absence of neuromuscular blockade before any assessment of motor response.

> [!info] The clinical testing, in principle
> Performed by **two appropriately qualified medical practitioners, independently**, each examining separately. Assessment of the absence of all brainstem reflexes — pupillary light, corneal, oculovestibular, gag and cough — and an **apnoea test** demonstrating no respiratory effort despite a documented rise in arterial CO₂ to a defined threshold with adequate oxygenation maintained.
> Where clinical testing cannot be completed — severe facial trauma, high cervical injury, pre-existing pupillary abnormality, inability to perform the apnoea test — **imaging demonstrating absent intracranial blood flow** is used instead.
> **The time of death is the time the second examination is completed**, or the time the imaging demonstrates absent flow.
> `UNVERIFIED — the required observation period, the CO₂ threshold and technique for the apnoea test, the qualifications and independence requirements of the two examining practitioners, and the accepted imaging modalities are ALL omitted from this file. The authoritative Australian source is the ANZICS Statement on Death and Organ Donation; obtain the current edition.`

> [!warning] Spinal reflexes persist and distress families
> Spinally mediated movements — limb withdrawal, the "Lazarus sign", deep tendon reflexes, triple flexion — **occur after brain death and do not indicate brain function.** Families and junior staff who witness them without warning find them profoundly distressing and may conclude the diagnosis is wrong. **Warn the family in advance that these movements can happen and explain what they are.**

> [!danger] Separate the determination of death from the organ donation conversation
> These are two distinct conversations, deliberately kept apart. **Death is determined first, communicated clearly, and the family given time.** The donation conversation follows, and is best conducted by a **trained requester** in collaboration with the **DonateLife** agency rather than by the treating team alone — the evidence is that this produces better outcomes for families regardless of the decision they make.
> **Use clear language.** "We have done tests that show that Sam has died" — not "there is no brain activity", "the machine is breathing for him", or "there is no hope", all of which leave the family believing their relative is alive and that they are being asked to choose otherwise.
> Note also the distinction between **donation after brain death (DBD)** and **donation after circulatory death (DCD)**, which follow different pathways.

**Ix:** Confirmation that preconditions are met, documented (*why:* the determination is invalid without them, and this is the step most open to error; *what:* known cause, confounders excluded, temperature and haemodynamics adequate). Drug levels and time since administration, with nerve stimulator assessment (*why:* residual sedation or paralysis invalidates the examination entirely; *what:* absence of neuromuscular blockade, adequate clearance interval). Core temperature and blood pressure (*why:* both must be above defined thresholds for testing to be valid; *what:* within required range). Arterial blood gas during the apnoea test (*why:* the test is only valid if CO₂ rises above the threshold with adequate oxygenation; *what:* documented PaCO₂ rise). Imaging of cerebral blood flow where clinical testing is not possible (*why:* provides the alternative pathway; *what:* absent intracranial flow). Neuroimaging establishing the cause (*why:* a known sufficient cause is a precondition; *what:* catastrophic injury).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Continue full physiological support until determination is complete. Ensure preconditions are met. Senior intensive care involvement — **this is not an intern-performed determination**, and the intern's role is supporting the family, ensuring documentation, and understanding the process.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Formal determination by two qualified practitioners. Clear communication with the family. Referral to DonateLife per local pathway. Coroner notification where the death is reportable. `UNVERIFIED — reportable death criteria under the Coroners Act 2003 (SA).`

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Family bereavement support. **Team debrief** — these cases are difficult for staff, particularly where the patient is young, and the debrief is not optional courtesy.

## 0.5 Professional Practice Concern

**D:** Situations where a clinician must raise, escalate or report a concern about care, conduct or a colleague — including their own.

> [!tip] Escalating a clinical concern — graded assertiveness
> Juniors frequently identify a problem and fail to escalate it effectively. A structured approach helps when the first attempt is not heard:
> **1. Probe** — "I'm not sure you're aware, the patient's respiratory rate is 34."
> **2. Alert** — "I'm concerned that this patient may be deteriorating."
> **3. Challenge** — "I'm uncomfortable proceeding without a senior review."
> **4. Emergency** — "Stop. I need you to come and see this patient now."
> **Use the words "I am concerned" and "I need"** — they are recognised escalation language in Australian health services. If you cannot get a response, **go around** — call the next person up, the rapid response team, or the on-call consultant. **Escalating and being wrong is a minor problem. Not escalating and being right is a catastrophe.** No reasonable senior will criticise a junior for calling.

> [!warning] Open disclosure after an adverse event
> Australian health services operate an **open disclosure** framework: an apology or expression of regret, a factual explanation of what happened, an account of the consequences, and what is being done to prevent recurrence. **An apology is not an admission of legal liability**, and legislation in most jurisdictions protects expressions of regret. Do not speculate about cause before the facts are known, do not blame colleagues, and involve senior staff and the patient safety unit early. Document. Submit an incident report — the reporting system exists to find system failures, not to punish individuals.

> [!danger] Mandatory notification to Ahpra
> Registered practitioners have a legal obligation to notify Ahpra about **notifiable conduct** by another practitioner. The categories are, broadly:
> **· practising while intoxicated by alcohol or drugs**
> **· sexual misconduct in connection with practice**
> **· placing the public at risk of substantial harm because of an impairment**
> **· placing the public at risk of harm by practising in a way that constitutes a significant departure from accepted professional standards**
> The threshold is deliberately high — it is **not** a mechanism for reporting ordinary error, a difficult personality, or a clinical disagreement.
> `UNVERIFIED — the precise wording of the notifiable conduct categories, the exemptions (which differ for practitioners providing health care to another practitioner, and which vary between states — Western Australia and Queensland have historically differed), and the reporting process must be verified against current Ahpra guidance.`

> [!info] Seeking help for your own health
> Doctors have poor rates of having their own GP and high rates of self-diagnosis and self-prescription. **Every doctor should have their own independent general practitioner.** Self-prescribing, and prescribing for family and close friends, is discouraged by the Medical Board other than in emergencies or minor conditions, and is **prohibited outright for drugs of dependence.**
> **Doctors' Health SA** provides confidential support for medical practitioners and students in South Australia, and reforms to mandatory notification were specifically intended to ensure that a practitioner seeking treatment for their own health is not thereby reported. `UNVERIFIED — confirm current mandatory notification exemptions for treating practitioners and the current Doctors' Health SA contact and scope.`

> [!warning] Boundaries, confidentiality and social media
> Confidentiality binds beyond the encounter, and its limits — mandatory reporting of child abuse, notifiable diseases, some driving and firearms provisions, court order, and serious risk of harm — are narrow and specific rather than general.
> **Do not look up records of people you are not treating.** Access is audited, and curiosity about a colleague, a friend or a public figure is a disciplinary matter.
> Social media: no identifiable patient information, ever, including in de-identified anecdotes that a patient could recognise. Advertising and testimonial restrictions apply to registered practitioners.

**Ix:** Not applicable in the diagnostic sense. The equivalent process is: establish the facts before acting, seek senior advice early, document contemporaneously, and use the formal pathways (incident reporting, patient safety unit, medical administration, medical defence organisation) rather than informal ones.

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Patient safety first — address the immediate clinical risk before addressing the professional issue. Escalate.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Formal reporting where the threshold is met. **Contact your medical defence organisation early** for anything involving a complaint, a coronial matter or a notification — this is what the membership is for, and doing it late is worse than doing it early.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
System review, education, and support for the staff involved — the **"second victim"** effect after a serious adverse event is real and under-recognised, and clinicians involved in one need active support rather than isolation.

## 0.6 Fitness Certification

**D:** Assessment and certification of a person's fitness for driving, work, sport, travel or other activity.

> [!danger] Driving — South Australia differs from most other states
> The national clinical standard is **Austroads "Assessing Fitness to Drive"**, which sets out condition-specific criteria and distinguishes **private** from **commercial** vehicle standards, the latter being substantially stricter.
> The doctor's role is to **assess against the standard, advise the patient clearly of their obligations and any driving restriction, and document that advice.** The patient has a legal obligation to notify the licensing authority of a relevant condition.
> **South Australia is notable in that legislation imposes a reporting obligation on medical practitioners** in relation to patients unfit to drive, which is a stronger position than the discretionary reporting that applies in most other states. `UNVERIFIED — this is a genuine jurisdictional difference and the precise nature, scope and extent of the South Australian obligation must be verified against the Motor Vehicles Act 1959 (SA) and current SA Government guidance before relying on it. Do not assume the position in another state applies, and do not assume this summary is complete.`

> [!tip] Conditions that commonly trigger a driving assessment
> **Seizure or epilepsy** — non-driving periods apply, differing for private and commercial licences and for provoked versus unprovoked seizures.
> **Syncope** — depends on cause and whether it is treated.
> **Cardiac** — myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, device implantation, and cardiac surgery each carry defined non-driving periods.
> **Diabetes** — the issue is **hypoglycaemia and, in particular, hypoglycaemia unawareness**, not diabetes itself.
> **Dementia and cognitive impairment** — often the most difficult conversation, and driving cessation causes real loss of independence that deserves acknowledgement and practical alternatives.
> **Vision** — acuity and visual field standards, with field loss frequently unrecognised by the patient.
> **Obstructive sleep apnoea** with excessive daytime sleepiness.
> **Substance use disorders.**
> `UNVERIFIED — every non-driving period and every threshold is omitted. Use the current Assessing Fitness to Drive publication directly; the periods differ by condition, by licence class, and are revised.`

> [!warning] The conversation matters
> Telling someone they cannot drive removes independence, employment and social connection. **Do it in person, explain the reasoning and the standard, give the timeframe, acknowledge what it costs them, and offer practical alternatives** — community transport, taxi subsidy schemes, family arrangements, occupational therapy driving assessment where the situation is borderline. A patient who feels ambushed will simply not disclose symptoms to the next doctor.

> [!info] Work capacity certification
> Australian work capacity certificates are framed around **what the person can do, not what they cannot** — the goal is safe, appropriate, and usually early return to some duties, because prolonged absence from work is itself associated with worse health outcomes. Specify capacity, restrictions, and a review date. Avoid open-ended total incapacity where any modified duty is possible.
> In South Australia, work injury certification operates through the **ReturnToWorkSA** scheme with its own certificate of capacity. `UNVERIFIED — the current certificate format, requirements and scheme rules require verification against ReturnToWorkSA.`

> [!warning] Certification is a professional act, not a favour
> Sickness certificates, work capacity certificates and fitness assessments are **legal documents.** Certify only what you have assessed and genuinely believe, backdate only where you can honestly attest to the earlier period, and do not certify under pressure from the patient, an employer or an insurer. Fraudulent or careless certification is a recognised source of Ahpra notifications.

**Ix:** Condition-specific assessment against the published standard (*why:* certification must be against the criteria that apply, not against clinical impression, and the standards are specific and often counterintuitive; *what:* whether the criteria are met). Objective testing where the standard requires it — visual fields and acuity, sleep study, cardiac assessment, cognitive assessment (*why:* the standard specifies objective evidence for several conditions and subjective report is inadequate; *what:* results against the criteria). **Occupational therapy on-road driving assessment** where fitness is borderline, particularly in cognitive impairment (*why:* it provides an objective functional assessment where clinical judgement alone is uncertain, and it carries more weight with both patient and family than an opinion; *what:* on-road performance). Collateral history from family (*why:* patients with cognitive impairment under-report near-misses, getting lost and minor collisions, and the family usually know; *what:* driving incidents, family concern). Documentation of the advice given (*why:* the record that advice was given is central to the practitioner's position if an incident later occurs; *what:* what was said, when, and the patient's response).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Advise the patient clearly of any restriction that applies now. Document it.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Assess against the standard, obtain the objective testing required, certify accurately, and comply with any applicable reporting obligation.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Review at the interval the standard specifies. For permanent cessation, address the consequences actively — transport alternatives, social isolation, and the depression that not uncommonly follows loss of a licence in an older person.
