## 0.1 Transient Loss of Consciousness — The Framework

**D:** Complete loss of consciousness that is transient, self-limiting, of rapid onset, and followed by full spontaneous recovery.

> [!tip] Three categories, and the first branch is the whole assessment
> **1. Syncope** — global cerebral hypoperfusion. Reflex, orthostatic, or cardiac.
> **2. Seizure** — abnormal cortical electrical activity.
> **3. Other** — psychogenic non-epileptic events, hypoglycaemia, intoxication, head trauma, and the "not really TLoC" group such as falls without loss of consciousness.

> [!danger] The collateral history is the investigation
> **What a witness saw is worth more than any test in this presentation.** The patient by definition did not observe their own event. Ring the family member, the ambulance officer, the bystander. Ask specifically: how long were they out, what colour were they, did they jerk and for how long, were their eyes open or closed, how long before they were talking sensibly again, and what were they doing immediately beforehand.
> Failing to obtain a collateral history is the commonest reason this presentation goes unresolved.

> [!info] Syncope versus seizure
> | | Favours syncope | Favours seizure |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Trigger** | Standing, pain, heat, emotion, venepuncture | Sleep deprivation, alcohol, flashing lights, or none |
> | **Prodrome** | Nausea, sweating, warmth, **greying or tunnelling of vision**, ringing ears | Aura — déjà vu, epigastric rising, olfactory or focal sensory |
> | **Duration of LOC** | **Seconds** | **Minutes** |
> | **Colour** | **Pale, clammy** | Flushed or cyanosed |
> | **Movements** | Brief, few, irregular jerks **after** collapse | **Rhythmic, sustained**, often preceding or with onset of LOC |
> | **Tongue** | Tip, or not bitten | **Lateral tongue bite — highly specific** |
> | **Recovery** | **Rapid orientation**, often within seconds | **Prolonged postictal confusion**, drowsiness, headache |
> | **Head turning** | No | Forced head or eye deviation |

> [!danger] Convulsive syncope — the trap
> **Brief myoclonic jerking occurs in a substantial proportion of ordinary syncopal episodes**, caused by transient cerebral hypoperfusion, and it is frequently reported by witnesses as "a fit". Crucially, **the jerking in syncope follows the collapse**, is brief and irregular, and is not accompanied by prolonged postictal confusion.
> Patients are commonly and incorrectly labelled epileptic on this basis, started on antiepileptic drugs and have their licence removed. **The presence of jerking does not make it a seizure.** Ask about the sequence and the recovery, not just the movements.

> [!warning] Incontinence does not discriminate
> Urinary incontinence occurs in both syncope and seizure and is not a useful discriminator, despite being taught as one. **Lateral tongue biting and prolonged postictal confusion are the features that genuinely separate them.**

**S/Smx:** As above, plus circumstances — posture at onset, activity, relation to exertion, meals, medication changes, and preceding palpitations or chest pain.

**Ix:** **12-lead ECG in every patient with TLoC** (*why:* it is cheap, immediate, and the only routine test that identifies the conditions that kill — long QT, Brugada, pre-excitation, conduction disease, prior infarction and hypertrophy; *what:* see the ECG hunt list in 0.2). Bedside glucose (*why:* hypoglycaemia causes both loss of consciousness and seizure and is instantly reversible; *what:* low glucose). **Lying and standing blood pressure**, measured properly (*why:* orthostatic hypotension is common, easily demonstrated and routinely not measured because it takes a few minutes; *what:* postural fall). FBC (*why:* anaemia and occult GI bleeding present as syncope; *what:* haemoglobin). UEC and electrolytes (*why:* volume depletion and electrolyte disturbance; *what:* derangement). Pregnancy test in a woman of reproductive age (*why:* **ruptured ectopic pregnancy presents as collapse** and is rapidly fatal; *what:* positive test). Troponin only where ischaemia is clinically suspected (*why:* routine troponin in syncope generates more confusion than diagnosis — cross-refer [[B1]] 0.5; *what:* elevation in context). Further testing directed by the category identified — see 0.2.

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E, glucose, ECG, lying and standing blood pressure, collateral history. Look for and treat injury sustained in the collapse.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Category-specific, per 0.2.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Driving advice, given and documented at the first presentation** — cross-refer [[A10]] 0.6. Non-driving periods differ for syncope and seizure, for treated and untreated causes, and for private and commercial licences.

## 0.2 Syncope

**D:** Transient loss of consciousness due to **global cerebral hypoperfusion**, with rapid onset, short duration and spontaneous complete recovery.

**A/P:** Cerebral perfusion depends on mean arterial pressure, which is cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance. A fall in either, sufficient to drop cerebral blood flow below the autoregulatory floor for a matter of seconds, causes loss of consciousness. **Lying flat restores perfusion, which is why syncope is self-terminating** — and why holding a fainting patient upright, or sitting them in a chair, prolongs the episode and is a common bystander and staff error.

> [!tip] The three categories
> **Reflex (neurally mediated)** — the commonest, and generally benign.
> · **Vasovagal**: a clear trigger (prolonged standing, heat, pain, fear, the sight of blood, venepuncture), a **prodrome** of nausea, sweating, warmth and visual greying, and rapid recovery.
> · **Situational**: cough, micturition, defecation, swallowing, laughing, or post-exercise.
> · **Carotid sinus hypersensitivity**: older patients, sometimes triggered by head turning, tight collars or shaving.
> **Orthostatic** — occurs on standing, within seconds to minutes.
> **Cardiac** — arrhythmic or structural. **The dangerous category.**

> [!danger] Red flags for cardiac syncope
> **· Syncope DURING exertion** — as opposed to immediately after, which is more often reflex. **Exertional syncope is aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy until proven otherwise**, and it warrants urgent assessment.
> **· Syncope while supine or sitting** — reflex syncope essentially requires an upright posture.
> **· No prodrome — sudden collapse without warning**, particularly with facial or dental injury, which indicates no protective reflex time.
> **· Palpitations immediately preceding.**
> **· Known structural heart disease, heart failure or prior myocardial infarction.**
> **· An abnormal ECG.**
> **· Family history of sudden cardiac death under 40, or of drowning or unexplained single-vehicle accident** — these are how channelopathy deaths are frequently recorded.
> Any of these moves the patient out of "probable vasovagal, discharge home".

> [!warning] The ECG hunt list in syncope
> Look deliberately for: **prolonged QT** · **short QT** · **Brugada pattern** (coved ST elevation in V1–V2) · **delta wave** of Wolff-Parkinson-White · **epsilon waves or T inversion in V1–V3** suggesting arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy · **deep Q waves and voltage criteria** suggesting hypertrophic cardiomyopathy · **bifascicular block or first degree AV block** suggesting intermittent complete heart block · prior infarction. `UNVERIFIED — QT correction formulae and cut-offs, and Brugada morphology criteria.`
> These are not subtle once you are looking for them, and they are missed when the ECG is glanced at for rate and rhythm only.

> [!info] Orthostatic hypotension
> Measured as a fall in blood pressure on standing from supine, assessed at intervals over several minutes — **many falls only appear after two or three minutes**, so an immediate single reading misses them. `UNVERIFIED — the diagnostic fall in systolic and diastolic pressure, and the timing of measurements, require verification.`
> **Drugs are the commonest cause** — antihypertensives, alpha-blockers for prostatic symptoms, diuretics, nitrates, tricyclics, antipsychotics, and opioids — and the medication list is the first place to look. Also: volume depletion, prolonged bed rest, and **autonomic failure** in Parkinson's disease, diabetes, multiple system atrophy and amyloidosis.

**S/Smx:** Prodrome or its absence, posture, trigger, duration, recovery speed, injury, and associated palpitations or chest pain. Examine for murmurs (aortic stenosis, and the dynamic murmur of HOCM), postural blood pressure change, and signs of anaemia or volume depletion.

**Ix:** As per 0.1, plus: **echocardiography** where structural disease is suspected, there is a murmur, or the ECG is abnormal (*why:* identifies aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and impaired ventricular function, the structural causes of exertional and life-threatening syncope; *what:* valve gradients, septal thickness with outflow obstruction, ejection fraction). **Ambulatory ECG monitoring**, with duration matched to symptom frequency (*why:* the diagnosis requires correlating symptoms with rhythm, and a 24-hour monitor in a patient with monthly episodes is close to useless — extended monitoring or an implantable loop recorder is required for infrequent events; *what:* symptom-rhythm correlation). Exercise testing where syncope was exertional (*why:* reproduces the circumstances and identifies exercise-induced arrhythmia and outflow obstruction; *what:* arrhythmia, blood pressure response). Tilt table testing in selected recurrent reflex syncope (*why:* confirms a reflex mechanism where the diagnosis is uncertain and reassurance is not sufficient; *what:* reproduced syncope with characteristic haemodynamics). Carotid sinus massage in older patients, **with cardiac monitoring and after excluding carotid bruit or recent stroke** (*why:* diagnoses carotid sinus hypersensitivity, which is pacemaker-treatable; *what:* pause or blood pressure fall). Risk stratification with a validated tool such as the Canadian Syncope Risk Score (*why:* structures the admit-versus-discharge decision more reliably than impression; *what:* risk category — `UNVERIFIED — components and thresholds`). **Genetic and family screening** where an inherited condition is suspected (*why:* the patient's relatives may be at risk of sudden death and the index presentation is the opportunity to find them; *what:* family cardiac assessment).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Lie the patient flat with legs elevated. Assess for injury. ECG, glucose, postural blood pressure. Admit and monitor where red flags are present.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
**Reflex syncope:** explanation and reassurance, trigger avoidance, adequate hydration and salt, **counterpressure manoeuvres** (leg crossing with muscle tensing, arm tensing, handgrip) taught explicitly — these genuinely abort episodes if started during the prodrome and are the single most useful intervention. Review and reduce contributing medications.
**Orthostatic:** rationalise the medication list first, then compression stockings, gradual position change, adequate fluid and salt, and pharmacological options if needed.
**Cardiac:** treat the arrhythmia or structural lesion. Pacemaker, ICD, valve intervention or ablation as indicated.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Driving advice. Occupational advice for those working at height, with machinery, or in safety-critical roles. Family screening for inherited conditions. Reassurance in reflex syncope is genuinely therapeutic — **patients frequently believe they have a serious heart problem, and explaining the mechanism reduces both anxiety and recurrence.**

## 0.3 Presyncope

**D:** The sensation of impending loss of consciousness — lightheadedness, greying vision, weakness — without actually losing it.

> [!danger] Presyncope has the same causes and the same significance as syncope
> **It is not a lesser problem.** Whether the patient lost consciousness or merely nearly did may reflect nothing more than whether they happened to sit down in time. **Investigate presyncope with the same red flags, the same ECG scrutiny and the same threshold as syncope**, including exertional presyncope and presyncope with palpitations.
> This is a common and consequential under-triage.

> [!warning] Distinguish presyncope from vertigo and from disequilibrium
> Patients use "dizzy" for at least four different things, and the word alone is uninformative. Ask them to describe it without using the word:
> **· Presyncope** — "about to faint", greying vision, going down.
> **· Vertigo** — an illusion of movement, spinning of self or surroundings. Points to vestibular pathology — cross-refer [[D5]].
> **· Disequilibrium** — unsteadiness on the feet with a clear head. Points to gait, proprioceptive or cerebellar problems.
> **· Non-specific lightheadedness** — often anxiety, hyperventilation, or medication.
> Getting this distinction right redirects the entire workup, and it costs one well-phrased question.

**S/Smx:** Lightheadedness, visual greying or tunnelling, ringing in the ears, nausea, sweating, weakness, and the sense of needing to sit or lie down.

**Ix:** As for syncope in 0.2 — the workup is the same. Particular attention to lying and standing blood pressure, the ECG, haemoglobin and the medication list.

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Lie flat, assess as for syncope.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific, as per 0.2.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
As per 0.2, including driving advice where the episodes are unpredictable.

## 0.4 Conscious Collapse and the Unwitnessed Fall

**D:** A fall to the ground without clear loss of consciousness, or a fall where whether consciousness was lost is unknown.

> [!danger] "Mechanical fall" is a diagnosis of exclusion, not a triage category
> An older person who falls has an underlying reason until proven otherwise, and labelling it mechanical at the front door closes the assessment prematurely. **The fall may be the presentation of syncope, arrhythmia, sepsis, myocardial infarction, stroke, hypoglycaemia, anaemia, dehydration, a new medication, or a subdural haematoma.**
> **If the fall was unwitnessed and the patient does not clearly recall hitting the ground, assume syncope and investigate accordingly.** Patients frequently have no memory of losing consciousness and will honestly report that they simply tripped.

> [!warning] The long lie
> An older person on the floor for hours accumulates **rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, hypothermia, dehydration, pressure injury and aspiration** — cross-refer [[A6]] 0.5 and [[A7]] 0.6. **Ask how long they were down**, and check a CK, potassium, renal function and temperature in anyone who was there for a prolonged period.
> The long lie is also a strong marker of social vulnerability, and it should trigger a conversation about living arrangements, alarms and support.

> [!tip] The multifactorial falls assessment
> Falls in older people are almost always multifactorial. Assess: **gait and balance** · **vision** · **cognition** · **medications**, particularly the number of them and any sedatives, antihypertensives, opioids and anticholinergics · **postural blood pressure** · **feet and footwear** · **continence**, since rushing to the toilet at night is a classic mechanism · **home hazards** · **vitamin D, calcium and bone health** · **alcohol** · and **fear of falling**, which itself reduces activity and worsens the risk.
> Each of these is modifiable, which is the point of listing them.

**S/Smx:** Circumstances of the fall, whether consciousness was lost, prodrome, time on the floor, injuries, and the presence of any preceding illness or medication change.

**Ix:** **CK, UEC and potassium** where the patient was down for a prolonged period (*why:* rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemia are common after a long lie and will be missed unless specifically sought; *what:* raised CK, rising creatinine, hyperkalaemia). Core temperature (*why:* hypothermia after a long lie occurs indoors and is easily missed; *what:* low temperature). ECG and postural blood pressure (*why:* the fall may be syncope; *what:* arrhythmia, conduction disease, postural drop). Bedside glucose (*why:* hypoglycaemia; *what:* low glucose). FBC, CRP, urinalysis and cultures (*why:* infection presents in the elderly as a fall with no other symptom; *what:* infective source). **CT head with a low threshold, particularly if anticoagulated** (*why:* subdural and intracranial haemorrhage after apparently minor head trauma in an anticoagulated older person, and delayed presentation is common; *what:* haemorrhage — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.8). Radiography of any painful area, with specific attention to the hip (*why:* **an impacted or undisplaced neck of femur fracture may allow weight-bearing and be missed on plain films**, so persistent pain warrants further imaging; *what:* fracture). Medication review (*why:* the commonest modifiable contributor; *what:* culprit drugs). Bone health assessment (*why:* the fall that caused a fragility fracture predicts the next one, and osteoporosis is under-treated after fractures; *what:* fracture risk, vitamin D).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess and treat injuries. Investigate for the cause. Rehydrate and correct electrolytes. Analgesia.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the identified cause. Orthopaedic management of fractures.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Multifactorial falls intervention** — exercise programs with a balance component have good evidence, alongside medication review, home assessment by occupational therapy, vision correction, and bone protection. Cross-refer [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]]. Consider a personal alarm where the patient lives alone and has had a long lie.

## 0.5 Hypotension

**D:** Blood pressure below the range required to perfuse organs adequately — a functional definition rather than a numerical one.

> [!danger] Hypotension is relative
> **A blood pressure that is normal on paper may be profoundly hypotensive for a patient whose usual pressure is much higher.** A chronically hypertensive patient perfusing at 180 systolic may be shocked at 110. Conversely, a small young woman may live comfortably at 90.
> **Compare against the patient's own baseline**, which is usually available in the record or from the GP, rather than against a population range. Failure to do this delays recognition of shock in exactly the patients who tolerate it worst.

**A/P:** Blood pressure is the product of cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. Hypotension therefore reflects a fall in preload, pump function, or vascular tone — which maps onto the shock phenotypes and their bedside signatures. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.1 for the full phenotype framework, which is not repeated here.

> [!tip] Causes worth separating
> **Hypovolaemic** — haemorrhage, dehydration, third-spacing, burns.
> **Cardiogenic** — infarction, arrhythmia, valvular failure, cardiomyopathy.
> **Obstructive** — tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive PE.
> **Distributive** — sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic, **adrenal insufficiency.**
> **Iatrogenic** — this deserves separate emphasis on the ward, since it is the commonest cause of an inpatient hypotension call: antihypertensives given to a patient who is now dry or septic, opioids and sedatives, spinal and epidural anaesthesia, diuretics continued through an acute illness, and rapid infusion of certain drugs.

> [!warning] Hypotension refractory to fluid and vasopressors — think of these
> **Adrenal insufficiency** — cortisol is required for vascular responsiveness to catecholamines, and its absence produces vasoplegia that does not respond to noradrenaline. Look for hyponatraemia with hyperkalaemia and hypoglycaemia, and ask about steroid use or recent cessation. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.6.
> **Tension pneumothorax and tamponade** — obstruction that no volume of fluid will overcome.
> **Severe acidosis** — impairs catecholamine responsiveness.
> **Anaphylaxis** — including biphasic and delayed presentations.
> **Occult haemorrhage** — retroperitoneal, intra-abdominal, or into a fractured limb.

**S/Smx:** Dizziness, weakness, confusion, oliguria, cool peripheries or warm vasodilated ones depending on the phenotype, and prolonged capillary refill. **Mental state and urine output are more informative than the number.**

**Ix:** Comparison with the patient's baseline blood pressure (*why:* determines whether this reading represents shock for this individual; *what:* usual pressure from the record or the GP). Serial lactate (*why:* the objective marker of whether the pressure is producing hypoperfusion, which is the question that matters; *what:* elevation and clearance). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* phenotypes the hypotension in under a minute — contractility, RV size, pericardial fluid, IVC calibre; *what:* the four-way answer). ECG (*why:* arrhythmia and infarction; *what:* rhythm, ischaemia). FBC (*why:* occult bleeding; *what:* haemoglobin trend). UEC with **sodium, potassium and glucose** (*why:* the hyponatraemia-hyperkalaemia-hypoglycaemia triad points at adrenal insufficiency; *what:* the pattern). Cortisol where adrenal crisis is possible, **taken before hydrocortisone but never delaying it** (*why:* the sample is unobtainable once treatment starts; *what:* inappropriately low cortisol). Septic screen (*why:* sepsis is the commonest distributive cause; *what:* source). **Full medication chart review including timing of the last doses** (*why:* iatrogenic hypotension is the commonest inpatient cause and is immediately reversible by not giving the next dose; *what:* antihypertensives, opioids, sedatives, diuretics). CXR (*why:* pneumothorax, pulmonary oedema; *what:* findings).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E, lie flat with legs elevated, oxygen, large-bore access, fluid challenge with **reassessment after each bolus** rather than a prescribed volume. Identify the phenotype. **Withhold the contributing drugs** — this is the fastest intervention available on a ward and is frequently overlooked while fluid is being run.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Phenotype-specific — cross-refer [[F0.3]]. Vasopressors and ICU where fluid-unresponsive. Hydrocortisone where adrenal insufficiency is plausible, given empirically rather than after confirmation.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Review the antihypertensive regimen** — an episode of hypotension in an older patient is a signal that the regimen may be too aggressive for their current state, and deprescribing is an active intervention rather than a failure. Cross-refer [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Polypharmacy and Deprescribing.
