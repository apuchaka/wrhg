## 0.1 Acute Chest Pain

**D:** New chest discomfort requiring exclusion of the conditions that kill quickly before consideration of the many that do not.

> [!danger] The six that must not be missed
> **Acute coronary syndrome · pulmonary embolism · aortic dissection · tension pneumothorax · oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave) · cardiac tamponade.**
> All six can present with chest pain and a benign-looking patient. The assessment is structured around excluding these first, and only then considering musculoskeletal, gastro-oesophageal and anxiety-related causes.

**A/P:** Visceral afferents from the heart, great vessels, oesophagus and mediastinum converge on the same spinal segments as somatic afferents from the chest wall and arm. **This shared innervation is why cardiac pain is poorly localised, radiates to the arm, jaw and epigastrium, and is genuinely indistinguishable from oesophageal pain by character alone** — the overlap is anatomical, not a failure of history-taking.

> [!warning] The classical descriptors discriminate less well than taught
> "Crushing, central, radiating to the left arm" raises the probability of ACS but its absence does not lower it usefully. Pain that is **sharp, positional, pleuritic or reproducible on palpation reduces the likelihood of ACS but does not exclude it** — a meaningful minority of confirmed infarcts have one of these features. Relief with GTN does not confirm cardiac pain (it relieves oesophageal spasm), and relief with antacid does not exclude it.
> **Do not use response to treatment as a diagnostic test in chest pain.**

> [!danger] Groups who present atypically
> **Women, patients with diabetes, the elderly, and patients with chronic kidney disease** more often present without chest pain at all — with dyspnoea, fatigue, nausea, syncope, epigastric discomfort or simply feeling unwell. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people develop ischaemic heart disease at substantially younger ages and with worse outcomes**, so age-based reassurance is less safe in this group and the threshold for investigation should be lower.

> [!tip] Features pointing to each of the six
> | Diagnosis | Suggestive features |
> |---|---|
> | **ACS** | Exertional, heavy, radiation to arm/jaw, diaphoresis, nausea, risk factors |
> | **PE** | Pleuritic, sudden, dyspnoea, syncope, hypoxia, calf pain, immobility or malignancy |
> | **Aortic dissection** | **Tearing, maximal at onset, interscapular**, pulse or BP differential, new aortic regurgitation, neurological deficit |
> | **Tension pneumothorax** | Sudden, dyspnoea, hypotension, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation (late) |
> | **Oesophageal rupture** | **After forceful vomiting**, severe pain, surgical emphysema, systemic toxicity |
> | **Tamponade** | Dyspnoea, hypotension, raised JVP, muffled sounds, pulsus paradoxus |

> [!danger] Aortic dissection — the diagnosis that gets anticoagulated by mistake
> Dissection presenting with ST elevation from coronary ostial involvement, or with chest pain and an abnormal ECG, has been treated as ACS — and **antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation and thrombolysis are catastrophic in dissection.**
> Think of it with: pain **maximal at onset** rather than building · tearing or ripping quality · interscapular or migrating pain · **blood pressure differential between arms** · pulse deficit · new aortic regurgitation murmur · focal neurological deficit · syncope · a widened mediastinum on chest radiograph.
> Management principle: **reduce shear stress — heart rate first with a beta-blocker, then blood pressure with a vasodilator.** Giving a vasodilator first causes reflex tachycardia and increases shear. Urgent CT aortogram and cardiothoracic referral. `UNVERIFIED — target heart rate and blood pressure, and agent choice and dosing, require verification against eTG.`

> [!warning] ECG points that change management
> **ST depression in V1–V3** may be a **posterior STEMI** — obtain posterior leads (V7–V9) rather than treating it as NSTEMI.
> **Inferior STEMI** — obtain **right-sided leads (V4R)** for right ventricular infarction. **RV infarct is preload-dependent: nitrates cause profound hypotension and are contraindicated**, and these patients need fluid rather than vasodilatation. This is a high-yield and clinically important trap.
> **New left bundle branch block** in the right clinical context is treated as a STEMI equivalent, though "new" is often unknowable.
> **A single normal ECG does not exclude ACS.** Repeat it — during pain, and serially.

**S/Smx:** Character, onset speed, radiation, duration, exacerbating and relieving factors, and associated dyspnoea, diaphoresis, nausea, syncope or palpitations. Examine for blood pressure in both arms, murmurs, JVP, breath sounds, tracheal position, calf swelling, surgical emphysema and chest wall tenderness.

**Ix:** **ECG within ten minutes of arrival**, and repeated serially and during pain (*why:* it identifies the STEMI that requires immediate reperfusion, and dynamic change across serial tracings is more informative than any single one; *what:* ST elevation or depression, T wave change, new bundle branch block, arrhythmia — `UNVERIFIED — the door-to-ECG time target and STEMI voltage criteria require verification`). **Serial high-sensitivity troponin** at intervals defined by the local pathway (*why:* the **change** between samples, not the absolute first value, distinguishes acute myocardial injury from chronic elevation — see 0.5; *what:* baseline value and delta). CXR (*why:* pneumothorax, widened mediastinum, consolidation, pulmonary oedema, and it is quick; *what:* mediastinal width, pneumothorax, free air under the diaphragm, effusion). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* regional wall motion abnormality, RV strain, pericardial effusion and aortic root dilatation — it addresses four of the six life threats at once; *what:* wall motion, RV size, effusion, valve). FBC, UEC, glucose, lipids (*why:* anaemia precipitates ischaemia, renal function affects both troponin interpretation and drug dosing; *what:* anaemia, creatinine). D-dimer or CTPA per pre-test probability (*why:* PE; *what:* filling defect). **CT aortogram where dissection is possible** (*why:* the diagnosis is time-critical and missed dissection treated as ACS is lethal; *what:* intimal flap, false lumen). Risk stratification with a validated accelerated diagnostic protocol (*why:* Australian emergency departments use structured pathways — HEART, EDACS or similar — to identify low-risk patients suitable for early discharge, and applying one is more defensible than gestalt; *what:* risk category — `UNVERIFIED — score components and thresholds, and which pathway your site uses`).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Monitored bed, oxygen **only if hypoxaemic** — routine oxygen in normoxic ACS is not beneficial and may be harmful. Aspirin. Analgesia. GTN, **excluding right ventricular infarction, severe aortic stenosis, and recent phosphodiesterase inhibitor use.** IV access, bloods, serial ECGs. **STEMI → immediate reperfusion**, primary PCI where achievable within the time target, otherwise fibrinolysis with transfer — a live consideration for rural South Australian presentations. `UNVERIFIED — all antiplatelet and anticoagulant agents and doses, and the PCI and fibrinolysis time targets, are omitted; obtain from your health network's ACS pathway.`

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Reperfusion and antithrombotic therapy for ACS. Anticoagulation for PE. Urgent surgery for type A dissection. Decompression for tension pneumothorax. Cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.4–0.5.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Secondary prevention after ACS — antiplatelet therapy, statin, ACE inhibitor or ARB, beta-blocker, cardiac rehabilitation, smoking cessation, and management of diabetes, blood pressure and lipids. **Cardiac rehabilitation referral is under-done and improves both mortality and quality of life.**

## 0.2 Chest Tightness and Chronic Chest Pain

**D:** Recurrent or persistent chest discomfort over weeks to months, the central question being whether it represents stable coronary disease.

> [!tip] Stable angina — the three defining features
> **1. Constricting discomfort** in the chest, neck, shoulders, jaw or arms.
> **2. Precipitated by physical exertion** (and often more easily in cold weather, after meals, or with emotion).
> **3. Relieved by rest or GTN within about five minutes.**
> All three = **typical angina.** Two = **atypical.** One or none = **non-anginal.** This simple classification drives pre-test probability and is worth using explicitly.
> Severity is graded by the **Canadian Cardiovascular Society class** according to the level of exertion that provokes it. `UNVERIFIED — class descriptors.`

> [!danger] Crescendo pattern means unstable angina, not stable angina
> Angina that is **new, occurring at rest, more frequent, more severe, or provoked by progressively less exertion** is an acute coronary syndrome and is managed as such — not booked for an outpatient stress test. The change in pattern is the diagnosis.

> [!warning] Non-cardiac causes of chronic chest pain are common and frequently coexist
> **Gastro-oesophageal reflux** — the commonest, often burning and postprandial, but genuinely indistinguishable from angina in some patients. **Oesophageal spasm** — can be severe and GTN-responsive, which is exactly why GTN response is not diagnostic. **Musculoskeletal** — costochondritis, Tietze syndrome, chest wall strain; reproducible on palpation, though this does not exclude coexisting cardiac disease. **Anxiety and panic disorder** — a diagnosis of exclusion in anyone with cardiac risk factors, and one that is applied too readily to young women. **Herpes zoster** before the rash appears. **Pulmonary hypertension** and **aortic stenosis**, both of which cause exertional chest tightness. **Malignancy** with chest wall or mediastinal involvement.

**A/P:** Fixed atherosclerotic stenosis limits coronary flow reserve → at rest, flow is adequate → on exertion, myocardial oxygen demand rises and the stenosis prevents a matching increase in supply → **demand ischaemia** → anginal pain, relieved when demand falls. This is the mechanism that distinguishes stable angina from ACS, where the problem is an acute supply interruption from plaque rupture and thrombus rather than a demand mismatch.

**S/Smx:** As above. Examine for anaemia, aortic stenosis, hypertension, xanthelasma, peripheral vascular disease and heart failure signs.

**Ix:** Resting ECG (*why:* baseline, and may show prior infarction, hypertrophy or arrhythmia — though a normal resting ECG is common in significant coronary disease and is not reassuring; *what:* Q waves, LVH, ischaemic change). **CT coronary angiography** (*why:* increasingly the first-line test in low-to-intermediate risk patients — it has excellent negative predictive value, and a normal CTCA effectively excludes obstructive coronary disease; *what:* stenosis, plaque burden, calcium score). Functional testing — exercise stress ECG, stress echocardiography or myocardial perfusion imaging (*why:* demonstrates inducible ischaemia and its extent, which informs revascularisation decisions where anatomy alone does not; *what:* ischaemic territory and burden). Invasive coronary angiography (*why:* definitive anatomy with the option of intervention in the same procedure, reserved for high-risk or symptomatically refractory patients; *what:* stenosis, suitability for PCI or bypass). Echocardiography (*why:* identifies aortic stenosis and impaired ventricular function, both of which cause exertional chest pain and change management entirely; *what:* valve gradients, ejection fraction). FBC, TFTs, lipids, HbA1c, UEC (*why:* anaemia and thyrotoxicosis precipitate angina, and the metabolic risk factors need identifying and treating regardless; *what:* haemoglobin, lipids, glycaemia). Trial of proton pump inhibitor where reflux is plausible (*why:* practical and often diagnostic, though a response does not exclude coexisting cardiac disease; *what:* symptomatic response).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Not usually required unless the pattern is unstable, in which case manage as 0.1.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Antianginal therapy — beta-blocker or calcium channel blocker first line, with long-acting nitrates and other agents added. **Secondary prevention regardless of whether revascularisation occurs**: antiplatelet, statin, and blood pressure and diabetes control. Revascularisation for symptoms refractory to medical therapy, or for prognostic indications based on anatomy. `UNVERIFIED — agents and doses from eTG Cardiovascular.`

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Cardiac rehabilitation, exercise, smoking cessation, weight and diet, and a written plan for what to do if the pattern changes — **patients should know that a change in their usual angina means calling an ambulance, not waiting for the clinic.**

## 0.3 Pleuritic Chest Pain

**D:** Sharp chest pain worsened by inspiration, coughing or movement, arising from the parietal pleura, pericardium or chest wall.

**A/P:** The **visceral pleura has no pain fibres**; the **parietal pleura is richly innervated** by intercostal nerves and, over the diaphragm, by the phrenic nerve. Inflammation, irritation or stretching of parietal pleura therefore produces sharp, well-localised, respirophasic pain — and **diaphragmatic pleural irritation refers to the shoulder tip via C3–C5**, which is why a subphrenic collection or splenic injury presents with shoulder pain.

> [!warning] Pleuritic pain does not exclude a cardiac cause
> **Pericarditis and, importantly, pulmonary embolism** both produce pleuritic pain. PE is the diagnosis most often missed in this presentation, precisely because "sharp and pleuritic" is taken as reassurance. Apply a structured PE risk assessment rather than dismissing pleuritic pain as musculoskeletal.

> [!tip] Differential
> **PE · pneumonia · pneumothorax · pericarditis · pleurisy from viral or connective tissue disease · rib fracture · costochondritis · herpes zoster before the rash · malignancy with pleural involvement · subphrenic pathology** referring upward.

> [!info] Acute pericarditis — the discriminators from STEMI
> | | Pericarditis | STEMI |
> |---|---|---|
> | Pain | **Positional — worse supine, better sitting forward**, pleuritic | Not positional, pressure-like |
> | ST elevation | **Widespread, concave ("saddle-shaped")**, not fitting a territory | **Regional**, convex, in a coronary territory |
> | Reciprocal change | Absent (except aVR and V1) | **Present** |
> | **PR segment** | **PR depression, with PR elevation in aVR** | Normal |
> | Rub | Present in a proportion | Absent |
> PR segment change is the most specific ECG feature of pericarditis and is routinely overlooked.
> Treatment is **NSAID plus colchicine**, the colchicine reducing recurrence substantially. `UNVERIFIED — doses and duration from eTG.`
> **Consider myocarditis** where troponin is raised, ventricular function is impaired, or arrhythmia occurs — that changes the disease from a nuisance to a potentially serious one, with implications including exercise restriction.

**S/Smx:** Sharp, localised, worse with deep breath, cough or movement, often with shallow guarded breathing. Look for a pleural or pericardial rub, focal chest signs, calf swelling, a vesicular rash in a dermatomal distribution, and chest wall tenderness.

**Ix:** ECG (*why:* distinguishes pericarditis from STEMI and may show right heart strain in PE; *what:* widespread concave ST elevation with PR depression, regional ST elevation, S1Q3T3 pattern). CXR (*why:* pneumothorax, consolidation, effusion, rib fracture; *what:* findings). Structured PE risk assessment with D-dimer or CTPA as indicated (*why:* PE is the missable diagnosis in this presentation and the assessment should be deliberate rather than intuitive; *what:* risk category, filling defect). Troponin (*why:* raised in myocarditis and in PE from RV strain, and its elevation in apparent pericarditis reclassifies the illness as myopericarditis; *what:* elevation). Echocardiography (*why:* pericardial effusion, tamponade physiology, ventricular function in myocarditis; *what:* effusion, wall motion). Inflammatory markers (*why:* support pericarditis and track response; *what:* raised CRP). Autoimmune screen where recurrent or with systemic features (*why:* connective tissue disease presents with serositis; *what:* ANA, ENA). Bedside lung ultrasound (*why:* rapid identification of pneumothorax, effusion and consolidation without moving the patient; *what:* absent lung sliding, effusion, B-lines).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Analgesia, oxygen if hypoxaemic, and exclusion of PE and pneumothorax before attributing pain to the chest wall.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. NSAID and colchicine for pericarditis. Anticoagulation for PE. Drainage for pneumothorax or empyema.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Follow-up echocardiography for pericarditis with effusion. **Exercise restriction in myocarditis** for a defined period, since exertion during active myocarditis is associated with arrhythmic death — this is important advice in young athletic patients and is frequently not given. `UNVERIFIED — duration of restriction.`

## 0.4 Coronary Vasospasm

**D:** Transient intense spasm of an epicardial coronary artery causing myocardial ischaemia, historically Prinzmetal or variant angina.

**A/P:** Hyperreactivity of coronary vascular smooth muscle, with endothelial dysfunction and autonomic influence → abrupt focal or diffuse spasm → **transient total or subtotal occlusion in an artery that may be angiographically normal** → transient ST elevation that resolves completely when the spasm relieves. Because the mechanism is smooth muscle constriction rather than thrombus, the treatment is vasodilatation rather than antithrombotic therapy.

> [!tip] The recognisable pattern
> **Pain at rest**, characteristically **at night or in the early hours of the morning**, in a patient who has preserved exercise tolerance — the opposite of the exertional pattern of fixed obstructive disease. **Transient ST elevation during pain that resolves entirely**, often with associated arrhythmia. Typically younger patients, **smoking being the dominant modifiable risk factor**, and more frequent in women than fixed atherosclerotic disease.

> [!warning] Triggers worth asking about
> **Smoking** · **cocaine and amphetamines** — a critical history in any young person with chest pain · triptans · **5-fluorouracil and capecitabine**, a well-recognised chemotherapy-induced coronary vasospasm that presents during infusion · cold exposure · hyperventilation · magnesium deficiency · alcohol withdrawal · **beta-blockers.**

> [!danger] Non-selective beta-blockers worsen vasospasm — this inverts standard ACS management
> Blocking β2-mediated coronary vasodilatation leaves **unopposed α-mediated vasoconstriction**, intensifying the spasm. **Calcium channel blockers are first-line**, with long-acting nitrates as add-on therapy.
> **The same principle applies to cocaine-associated chest pain**, where benzodiazepines and nitrates are the treatment and beta-blockers are traditionally avoided for the same unopposed-alpha reason. Cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.4.

> [!info] MINOCA and INOCA
> **Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries** and **ischaemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries** are recognised entities. A patient with a genuine troponin rise and a normal angiogram has **not** had a false alarm — vasospasm, microvascular dysfunction, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, myocarditis and embolism all belong in that differential, and the workup continues rather than stopping. **Spontaneous coronary artery dissection specifically affects younger women, including peripartum**, and is frequently misattributed to anxiety.

**S/Smx:** Rest and nocturnal angina, sometimes severe, with preserved exertional capacity. Syncope or palpitations if arrhythmia accompanies the spasm.

**Ix:** **ECG during pain** (*why:* the diagnosis rests on demonstrating transient ischaemic change that resolves, and an ECG taken after the pain has settled is normal — so capturing one during an episode is the single most valuable investigation; *what:* transient ST elevation resolving completely). Ambulatory ECG monitoring (*why:* captures nocturnal and asymptomatic episodes that are otherwise never documented; *what:* transient ST shift). Troponin (*why:* may be raised if spasm is prolonged, distinguishing infarction from transient ischaemia; *what:* elevation). Coronary angiography (*why:* excludes fixed obstructive disease, which coexists in some patients, and may demonstrate spasm; *what:* normal or non-obstructive arteries). Provocative testing in specialist centres (*why:* confirms the diagnosis where it remains uncertain, though it carries risk and is not routine; *what:* induced spasm). **Urine toxicology for cocaine and amphetamines** (*why:* it changes the diagnosis, the treatment and the long-term advice, and the history is unreliable; *what:* stimulant use). Magnesium and electrolytes (*why:* deficiency contributes; *what:* low magnesium).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Nitrates, which typically relieve the spasm rapidly. Calcium channel blocker. **Avoid beta-blockers.** Benzodiazepines where stimulant-related. Monitor for arrhythmia.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Long-term calcium channel blocker, with long-acting nitrates added if needed. Cardiology involvement.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Smoking cessation is the single most effective intervention** and should be framed as such. Stimulant cessation with drug and alcohol support. Avoidance of precipitating drugs, documented as a contraindication where chemotherapy-related.

## 0.5 Troponin and Cardiac Biomarkers

**D:** Cardiac troponin I and T are structural proteins released on myocyte injury, and the primary biomarkers of myocardial damage.

> [!danger] Troponin indicates myocardial injury — it does not indicate the cause of that injury
> **A raised troponin is not a diagnosis of myocardial infarction.** Infarction requires a **rise and/or fall** in troponin **plus clinical evidence of ischaemia** — symptoms, ECG changes, imaging evidence, or angiographic findings. Elevation without that context is **myocardial injury**, which has an entirely different differential and management.
> This distinction is the most consequential point in this section, and misunderstanding it leads to patients being anticoagulated, catheterised and labelled with coronary disease they do not have.

> [!tip] Type 1 versus Type 2 myocardial infarction — the distinction that changes treatment
> **Type 1** — **atherosclerotic plaque rupture with thrombus.** The classical ACS. Treatment is antithrombotic therapy and revascularisation.
> **Type 2** — **supply-demand mismatch without plaque rupture.** Caused by sepsis, tachyarrhythmia, severe anaemia, hypotension, hypoxia, severe hypertension. **Treatment is the underlying cause** — antiplatelets and catheterisation do not help and the bleeding risk is real.
> A septic patient with a raised troponin usually has type 2 injury. Treating them as ACS is a common error with real harm.

> [!warning] Non-ACS causes of a raised troponin
> **Pulmonary embolism** (RV strain) · **heart failure** · **myocarditis** · **chronic kidney disease** — where troponin is frequently chronically elevated and **the patient's own baseline is what matters, not the population reference range** · sepsis · tachyarrhythmia · cardiac contusion · **stroke and subarachnoid haemorrhage** · takotsubo cardiomyopathy · cardioversion and ablation · strenuous endurance exercise · infiltrative cardiomyopathy.

> [!info] High-sensitivity assays and the delta
> High-sensitivity troponin detects far lower concentrations and therefore identifies far more patients with detectable levels — most of whom do not have ACS. **The serial change (the "delta") between the initial and repeat sample is what distinguishes acute injury from chronic elevation.** A high but flat troponin over serial samples suggests chronic injury; a rising or falling pattern suggests an acute event.
> Assays have **sex-specific 99th percentile thresholds**, since normal troponin concentrations differ between men and women, and using a single threshold under-diagnoses in women.
> `UNVERIFIED — the 99th percentile values, sex-specific cut-offs, sampling intervals and delta thresholds are all assay-specific and site-specific. Obtain them from your own laboratory and chest pain pathway; troponin I and troponin T values are not interchangeable and neither are values between manufacturers.`

> [!danger] Don't order a troponin without a reason
> A troponin sent "for completeness" in a patient with no plausible ischaemic presentation generates a result that must then be explained, frequently leading to admission, further testing, anticoagulation and an incorrect label. **Order it when the answer will change what you do.**

> [!tip] The other biomarkers
> **BNP / NT-proBNP** — released on ventricular wall stretch. Most useful for its **negative predictive value**: a normal level makes heart failure unlikely in an undifferentiated breathless patient. Raised in renal impairment, AF, PE, sepsis and age; **lowered in obesity**, which is a genuine pitfall.
> **D-dimer** — a fibrin degradation product. Useful only in **low pre-test probability** to exclude PE or DVT; raised in infection, malignancy, pregnancy, surgery, trauma and old age, so a positive result in a high-risk patient adds nothing. Age-adjusted thresholds improve specificity in the elderly.
> **CK-MB** — largely superseded by troponin; retains a limited role in detecting reinfarction because it clears faster.
> **Myoglobin** — obsolete for this purpose.

**Ix:** Serial troponin per the local pathway with attention to the delta (*why:* the change over time is the diagnostic information, and a single value at presentation frequently cannot distinguish acute from chronic; *what:* baseline and change). Comparison with any **previous troponin values** in the record (*why:* a patient with chronic kidney disease may have a documented stable baseline, which immediately reframes an "abnormal" result; *what:* prior levels). ECG in parallel (*why:* troponin without ECG correlation cannot classify the event; *what:* ischaemic change). Echocardiography (*why:* regional wall motion abnormality supports ischaemic injury, global impairment suggests myocarditis, and RV strain suggests PE; *what:* wall motion pattern). UEC and eGFR (*why:* renal impairment raises baseline troponin and alters interpretation; *what:* creatinine, eGFR). Assessment for a type 2 precipitant — sepsis screen, haemoglobin, rhythm, blood pressure, oxygenation (*why:* identifying and treating the precipitant is the management of type 2 injury; *what:* infection, anaemia, arrhythmia, hypotension, hypoxia).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Interpret in clinical context. Classify: STEMI, NSTEMI, type 2 injury, or non-ischaemic myocardial injury. Treat accordingly — **and treat the precipitant rather than the number in type 2.**

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
As per the classification. Cardiology involvement for type 1 events and for unexplained significant elevation.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Communicate the classification clearly in the discharge summary.** A patient discharged with "troponin rise" and no explanation acquires a coronary artery disease label that follows them for life, affects insurance and future care, and may lead to unnecessary long-term antiplatelet therapy. State explicitly whether this was a type 1 infarct, a type 2 injury, or a non-ischaemic elevation, and what the follow-up plan is.
