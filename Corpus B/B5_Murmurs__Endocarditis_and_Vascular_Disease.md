## 0.1 Heart Murmurs — Assessment

**D:** An audible sound generated by turbulent blood flow across a valve, a septal defect, or a high-flow state.

> [!danger] All diastolic murmurs are pathological. All continuous murmurs are pathological.
> There is no such thing as an innocent diastolic murmur. A diastolic murmur warrants echocardiography regardless of how well the patient appears.
> **Innocent systolic murmurs** are soft, early or mid-systolic, without radiation or thrill, with normal pulses, normal heart sounds and no symptoms — and they are common, particularly in children, pregnancy, anaemia, fever and thyrotoxicosis, all of which are high-output states.

> [!tip] What to characterise
> **Timing** — systolic, diastolic, or continuous, and where within the cycle.
> **Site of maximal intensity** and **radiation** — aortic stenosis to the carotids, mitral regurgitation to the axilla.
> **Character** — ejection (crescendo-decrescendo), pansystolic (uniform), blowing, rumbling, harsh.
> **Intensity** — graded 1 to 6, with **grade 4 and above defined by a palpable thrill**, which is the practically useful boundary. `UNVERIFIED — Levine grading descriptors.`
> **Associated findings** — pulse character, pulse pressure, apex beat, heart sounds, added sounds, and signs of heart failure. **The peripheral findings frequently establish the diagnosis more reliably than the murmur itself.**

> [!info] Dynamic manoeuvres
> **Inspiration increases venous return to the right heart, so right-sided murmurs get louder** — the mnemonic **RILE** (Right on Inspiration, Left on Expiration).
> **Valsalva and standing reduce preload, so most murmurs get quieter — with two exceptions that get LOUDER: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and mitral valve prolapse.** Reduced ventricular volume worsens outflow obstruction in HOCM and increases prolapse in MVP. This pair of exceptions is examined repeatedly.
> **Squatting and handgrip increase afterload and preload**, increasing mitral regurgitation, ventricular septal defect and aortic regurgitation murmurs, and reducing the HOCM murmur.

> [!tip] The common lesions
> | Lesion | Murmur | Key associated findings |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Aortic stenosis** | Ejection systolic, right sternal edge, **radiates to carotids** | **Slow-rising pulse, narrow pulse pressure**, heaving apex, soft S2. Triad: **angina, syncope, dyspnoea** |
> | **Mitral regurgitation** | **Pansystolic**, apex, **radiates to axilla** | Displaced apex, soft S1, S3 |
> | **Aortic regurgitation** | **Early diastolic**, left sternal edge, **sitting forward in expiration** | **Collapsing pulse, wide pulse pressure**, displaced apex |
> | **Mitral stenosis** | **Mid-diastolic rumble** at apex, in left lateral position | **Loud S1, opening snap**, malar flush, AF |
> | **HOCM** | Ejection systolic, **louder on Valsalva** | Jerky pulse, family history of sudden death |
> | **VSD** | Harsh pansystolic, left sternal edge, thrill | Smaller defects are louder |

> [!warning] Murmurs in children
> Innocent murmurs are very common. **Red flags requiring assessment: any diastolic murmur · a harsh or loud murmur or a thrill · cyanosis · failure to thrive or poor feeding · absent or delayed femoral pulses (coarctation) · abnormal second heart sound · family history of congenital heart disease.**
> **Feel the femoral pulses in every child with a murmur** — coarctation is missed for years by not doing so.

**S/Smx:** Frequently asymptomatic. Otherwise dyspnoea, exertional intolerance, chest pain, syncope, palpitations, oedema, or the systemic features of endocarditis.

**Ix:** **Transthoracic echocardiography** (*why:* the definitive investigation — it identifies the lesion, quantifies severity, assesses ventricular consequences and determines whether intervention is needed, and auscultation alone cannot grade severity; *what:* valve morphology, gradients, regurgitant severity, chamber size, ejection fraction). ECG (*why:* left ventricular hypertrophy in aortic stenosis and HOCM, atrial fibrillation and P mitrale in mitral stenosis, and conduction disease suggesting an aortic root abscess in endocarditis; *what:* hypertrophy, AF, new AV block). CXR (*why:* chamber enlargement, pulmonary congestion, valve calcification; *what:* cardiomegaly, oedema, calcification). FBC (*why:* anaemia causes a flow murmur and worsens any valve lesion; *what:* haemoglobin). TFTs (*why:* thyrotoxicosis is a high-output state producing murmurs and AF; *what:* suppressed TSH). **Blood cultures before antibiotics in anyone with a murmur and fever** (*why:* endocarditis is the diagnosis that must not be missed and cultures taken after antibiotics may never grow; *what:* organism — see 0.2).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Only if the patient is acutely unwell — see 0.2.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Echocardiography and cardiology referral for any pathological murmur. Intervention decisions by severity, symptoms and ventricular consequences.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Surveillance echocardiography at intervals determined by lesion severity. **Endocarditis prophylaxis** for defined high-risk lesions and procedures — the indications are much narrower than historically taught. `UNVERIFIED — current prophylaxis indications and regimens from eTG Antibiotic; these have been substantially restricted.`

## 0.2 New and Changing Murmur

**D:** A murmur that is genuinely new, or that has changed in character or intensity — which in an unwell patient carries specific and urgent implications.

> [!danger] A new murmur in a febrile patient is infective endocarditis until proven otherwise
> **Take three sets of blood cultures from separate sites before antibiotics, and arrange echocardiography.** The combination of fever and a new murmur is one of the highest-yield clinical pairings in medicine.
> Risk groups: **prosthetic valves · previous endocarditis · congenital heart disease · injecting drug use** (characteristically right-sided, tricuspid, with septic pulmonary emboli and a relatively normal cardiac examination) · **rheumatic heart disease**, which in Australia disproportionately affects **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people**, among whom rates are among the highest in the world — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.10 · intravascular devices and recent instrumentation.
> Diagnosis uses the **modified Duke criteria**, combining microbiological, echocardiographic and clinical elements. `UNVERIFIED — the criteria, their weighting, and all antibiotic regimens and durations from eTG Antibiotic.`
> **Peripheral stigmata** — splinter haemorrhages, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, Roth spots — are classically taught, genuinely uncommon in modern practice, and their absence means nothing.
> **A new AV block in endocarditis suggests an aortic root abscess** extending into the conduction system, and it is a surgical emergency. Watch the ECG.

> [!danger] Mechanical complications of myocardial infarction — days 3 to 7
> A new murmur in a patient several days after an infarct is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise.
> **Papillary muscle rupture** → acute severe mitral regurgitation with flash pulmonary oedema and cardiogenic shock. **The murmur may be soft or absent**, because the regurgitant pressure gradient equalises rapidly across a low-compliance left atrium — so a quiet murmur is not reassurance.
> **Ventricular septal rupture** → harsh pansystolic murmur with a thrill, and shock.
> **Free wall rupture** → tamponade and pulseless electrical activity.
> All three require urgent echocardiography and immediate cardiothoracic involvement. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.3–0.4.

> [!warning] Other causes of a genuinely new murmur
> **Aortic dissection involving the root** → acute aortic regurgitation with chest pain — cross-refer [[B1]] 0.1. **Prosthetic valve thrombosis or dehiscence** → a new or altered murmur with muffled prosthetic sounds. **A high-output state** — anaemia, fever, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy — producing a flow murmur that resolves with the cause. **Papillary muscle dysfunction** from ischaemia without frank rupture.

**S/Smx:** Fever, night sweats, weight loss, malaise and embolic phenomena in endocarditis; acute dyspnoea and shock in mechanical complications; chest pain in dissection.

**Ix:** **Three sets of blood cultures from separate venepuncture sites before antibiotics** (*why:* endocarditis is a continuous bacteraemia so separate sets increase yield and support the Duke criteria, and yield falls sharply once antibiotics start — in a stable patient it is worth waiting; *what:* organism and sensitivities). **Transthoracic echocardiography, proceeding to transoesophageal** (*why:* TTE has limited sensitivity for vegetations, particularly on prosthetic valves and for aortic root abscess, and a normal TTE does not exclude endocarditis — TOE is required where suspicion persists; *what:* vegetation, abscess, valve destruction, regurgitation, mechanical complication). Serial ECG (*why:* **new conduction disease signals aortic root abscess**; *what:* new AV block, lengthening PR). FBC, CRP, UEC, urinalysis (*why:* anaemia of chronic infection, inflammatory markers for monitoring, renal impairment and haematuria from immune complex glomerulonephritis or emboli; *what:* anaemia, raised CRP, haematuria). Imaging for embolic complications — CT brain, abdomen, and CT chest for septic pulmonary emboli in right-sided disease (*why:* embolic events change management and may need intervention; *what:* infarcts, abscesses, mycotic aneurysms). Troponin and urgent echocardiography where the murmur is post-infarct (*why:* mechanical complications are time-critical and surgical; *what:* wall motion, regurgitant jet, septal defect).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Cultures before antibiotics. Empirical therapy once cultures are taken in a septic patient. Urgent echocardiography. **Cardiothoracic involvement immediately for post-infarct mechanical complications**, and cardiology plus infectious diseases for endocarditis.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Prolonged targeted intravenous antibiotics for endocarditis, with surgery for heart failure, abscess, large vegetations with embolic risk, prosthetic valve involvement or uncontrolled infection. Urgent surgical repair for post-infarct rupture.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Dental review and oral health optimisation after endocarditis, which is under-arranged. Patient education about future prophylaxis and about seeking assessment for unexplained fever. **Secondary prophylaxis and follow-up in rheumatic heart disease**, with register-based recall — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.10. Drug and alcohol support where injecting drug use is the risk factor.

## 0.3 Peripartum Cardiomyopathy

**D:** Heart failure with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction developing towards the end of pregnancy or in the months following delivery, without another identifiable cause.

**R:**
*Unmodifiable:* Multiparity, multiple gestation, older maternal age, African ancestry, family history.
*Modifiable:* Pre-eclampsia and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, obesity, prolonged tocolytic use, anaemia, malnutrition.

**A/P:** The mechanism is incompletely understood but involves **oxidative stress generating an antiangiogenic fragment of prolactin**, which damages the myocardial microvasculature, in a background of the profound haemodynamic load of pregnancy — increased plasma volume, increased cardiac output and reduced systemic vascular resistance. This prolactin hypothesis is the basis for investigational bromocriptine therapy.

> [!danger] The diagnosis is missed because the symptoms are the symptoms of pregnancy
> **Breathlessness, fatigue, ankle swelling and reduced exercise tolerance are normal in late pregnancy and the puerperium** — which is exactly why a failing heart is attributed to being pregnant. Women have died from this having been reassured repeatedly.
> **Features that should prompt an echocardiogram rather than reassurance: orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea · dyspnoea at rest · a resting tachycardia that does not settle · a new murmur or third heart sound · raised JVP · hypoxia · symptoms that are worsening rather than plateauing · and any of these in a woman with pre-eclampsia.**
> The threshold for echocardiography in a breathless peripartum woman should be low.

> [!warning] The differential in a breathless peripartum woman
> **Pulmonary embolism** — pregnancy and the puerperium are prothrombotic. **Amniotic fluid embolism.** **Pre-eclampsia with pulmonary oedema.** **Pre-existing or undiagnosed valvular disease unmasked by pregnancy** — particularly mitral stenosis, where the increased cardiac output and heart rate are poorly tolerated. **Anaemia.** **Thyrotoxicosis.** All are serious and all present the same way.

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND, fatigue, palpitations, cough, oedema, and in severe cases pulmonary oedema, arrhythmia or cardiogenic shock. Examination: tachycardia, raised JVP, third heart sound, crackles, displaced apex, murmur of functional mitral regurgitation.

**Ix:** **Echocardiography** (*why:* the diagnostic test — it demonstrates the reduced ejection fraction and excludes valvular and other structural causes, and there is no substitute; *what:* reduced LVEF, chamber dilatation, functional regurgitation, thrombus). **BNP or NT-proBNP** (*why:* a normal level makes heart failure unlikely, which is useful given how non-specific the symptoms are, though levels rise modestly in normal pregnancy; *what:* elevation). ECG (*why:* arrhythmia, ischaemia, and non-specific changes; *what:* tachycardia, arrhythmia, ST changes). CXR with abdominal shielding (*why:* pulmonary oedema and cardiomegaly, and the radiation dose is small and should not be withheld where indicated; *what:* oedema, cardiomegaly). FBC, UEC, LFT, TFTs (*why:* anaemia, renal impairment, thyroid disease, and the haemolysis and transaminitis of HELLP; *what:* derangement). Blood pressure and urine protein (*why:* pre-eclampsia coexists frequently and changes management; *what:* hypertension, proteinuria). Troponin (*why:* excludes ischaemic cause including spontaneous coronary artery dissection, which occurs peripartum; *what:* elevation). Investigation for PE where suspected (*why:* the principal differential; *what:* filling defect).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen, sit upright, and manage acute pulmonary oedema — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.3. **Involve obstetrics, cardiology, anaesthetics and neonatology together**; this is a multidisciplinary condition and delivery timing is a joint decision.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Standard heart failure therapy **adjusted for pregnancy and lactation** — **ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated antenatally** and alternatives are used, with the regimen changing after delivery and again depending on breastfeeding. **Anticoagulation is considered where the ejection fraction is very low**, given the elevated thromboembolic risk in an already prothrombotic state. Bromocriptine has been used investigationally on the prolactin hypothesis. `UNVERIFIED — every agent, its safety in pregnancy and lactation, the ejection fraction threshold for anticoagulation, and the status of bromocriptine require verification against SOMANZ and current cardio-obstetric guidance.`

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Serial echocardiography** — a substantial proportion recover ventricular function, and the degree of recovery determines both prognosis and advice about future pregnancy.
**Counselling about subsequent pregnancy is essential and is frequently not given.** Recurrence risk is significant, and it is higher in women whose ejection fraction has not normalised. This conversation should happen before the woman is pregnant again, with contraception discussed in the interim. Cross-refer [[O6]] Contraception.

## 0.4 Post-Catheterisation Vascular Complications

**D:** Local vascular injury following arterial access, most often femoral, for coronary or peripheral intervention.

**A/P:** Arterial puncture creates a defect that normally seals. Failure to seal produces a **pseudoaneurysm** — a contained pulsatile haematoma communicating with the arterial lumen through a neck, contained only by surrounding tissue rather than by a true vessel wall. An accompanying venous injury produces an **arteriovenous fistula.** A puncture made **above the inguinal ligament** bleeds into the retroperitoneum, where there is no tamponading tissue and no visible swelling.

> [!danger] Retroperitoneal haematoma — the one with no visible signs
> **A high femoral puncture bleeds into the retroperitoneum, and there may be NO groin swelling, NO bruise and NO local tenderness.**
> The presentation is **back, flank or abdominal pain, hypotension, tachycardia and a falling haemoglobin** in a patient hours after a procedure. It is frequently attributed to sedation, vasovagal reaction or analgesia effect while the patient exsanguinates.
> **Any hypotension after arterial access is retroperitoneal haemorrhage until excluded**, and the investigation is a CT abdomen. Cross-refer [[A9]] 0.1.

> [!tip] Pseudoaneurysm
> **A pulsatile groin mass with a systolic bruit or palpable thrill, and pain**, developing over hours to days after the procedure. Confirmed by **duplex ultrasound**, which shows the characteristic to-and-fro flow in the neck and swirling "yin-yang" flow in the sac.
> Management depends on size: small ones may thrombose spontaneously with observation; larger ones are treated with **ultrasound-guided thrombin injection** or, less commonly now, compression; surgery is reserved for failure, infection, rapid expansion, distal ischaemia or skin compromise. `UNVERIFIED — size thresholds and the technique details.`

> [!warning] The other access complications
> **Arteriovenous fistula** — a **continuous** bruit, since flow occurs in both systole and diastole. **Haematoma** — common, usually managed conservatively, but monitor for expansion and for compartment or nerve compression. **Arterial occlusion or dissection** — a cold, pulseless, painful limb, which is a vascular emergency (see 0.6). **Infection.** **Femoral nerve compression** by haematoma, causing thigh weakness and sensory loss.
> **Radial access has substantially lower rates of all of these**, which is why it has become the default for coronary intervention.

**S/Smx:** Groin pain, swelling, pulsatile mass, bruit, bruising, and — for retroperitoneal bleeding — back or flank pain with hypotension and no local signs.

**Ix:** **Duplex ultrasound of the groin** (*why:* the diagnostic test for pseudoaneurysm and AV fistula, distinguishing them from simple haematoma, and it guides thrombin injection; *what:* to-and-fro neck flow, yin-yang sac, fistulous connection). **CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast where hypotension or unexplained haemoglobin fall** (*why:* retroperitoneal haematoma is invisible clinically and this is the only way to find it; *what:* retroperitoneal collection, active extravasation). **Serial FBC** (*why:* a falling haemoglobin is often the only objective sign; *what:* declining haemoglobin). Coagulation profile and review of antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy (*why:* these patients are almost always on dual antiplatelet therapy plus a periprocedural anticoagulant, which both causes and complicates the bleeding; *what:* INR, agents given). Distal neurovascular examination (*why:* limb ischaemia and femoral nerve compression are limb- and function-threatening; *what:* pulses, capillary refill, sensation, quadriceps power). Group and hold or crossmatch (*why:* transfusion may be required; *what:* units available).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Direct pressure. Resuscitate. **Stop or reverse anticoagulation where bleeding is significant** — cross-refer [[A9]] 0.4. **Contact the interventional team that performed the procedure**, who know the access site, the sheath size and the closure device used.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Thrombin injection or surgical repair for pseudoaneurysm. Interventional radiology or surgery for ongoing retroperitoneal bleeding. Vascular surgery for occlusion.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Document the complication and the access site. Consider radial access for future procedures. Resume antiplatelet therapy after a deliberate risk-benefit decision — **a patient with a recent coronary stent who has antiplatelet therapy stopped is at risk of stent thrombosis**, so the decision needs cardiology input rather than being made unilaterally.

## 0.5 Claudication and Peripheral Arterial Disease

**D:** Reproducible ischaemic muscle pain brought on by walking and relieved by rest, caused by arterial stenosis limiting exercise-induced increases in flow.

**A/P:** Atherosclerotic stenosis permits adequate resting flow but prevents the increase required by exercising muscle → anaerobic metabolism and metabolite accumulation in the muscle group **distal to** the lesion → cramping pain → relieved when demand falls. **The site of pain therefore indicates the level of disease**, which makes the history genuinely localising.

> [!tip] The site tells you the level
> **Buttock and hip** → aortoiliac disease. With **erectile dysfunction and absent femoral pulses this is Leriche syndrome.**
> **Thigh** → common femoral or aortoiliac.
> **Calf** — the commonest — → superficial femoral or popliteal.
> **Foot** → tibial or peroneal, characteristic of diabetes.

> [!warning] Vascular versus neurogenic claudication
> Spinal canal stenosis produces "pseudoclaudication" that is frequently mistaken for vascular disease.
> | | Vascular | Neurogenic (spinal stenosis) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Onset distance | **Consistent and reproducible** | Variable day to day |
> | Relief | **Standing still is enough** | **Requires sitting or flexing forward** |
> | Walking uphill | Worse | Often **better** (spine flexed) |
> | Cycling | Worse | Often tolerated well (spine flexed) |
> | Pulses | Reduced or absent | Normal |
> | Sensory symptoms | Uncommon | Common — numbness, tingling |
> The "shopping trolley sign" — relief when leaning forward on a trolley — points strongly to spinal stenosis. Cross-refer [[L4]] Back Pain.

> [!danger] PAD is a marker of systemic atherosclerosis, and the leg is not the main threat
> **A patient with claudication has a substantially elevated risk of myocardial infarction and stroke**, and their cardiovascular mortality far exceeds their risk of limb loss. **The most important interventions are therefore systemic**: smoking cessation, antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statin, blood pressure and diabetes control.
> Treating the leg while ignoring the coronary and cerebral circulations is treating the least dangerous manifestation.

> [!info] Exercise therapy is first-line and works
> **Supervised exercise programs improve walking distance comparably to revascularisation** for intermittent claudication, without procedural risk. They are consistently under-referred in favour of imaging and intervention. The instruction is to walk to near-maximal claudication pain, rest, and repeat, regularly.

**S/Smx:** Cramping pain in a muscle group on walking, relieved by rest within minutes, reproducible at a consistent distance. Examination: absent or reduced pulses, bruits, cool and hairless skin, thin shiny skin, delayed capillary refill, and dependent rubor with elevation pallor.

**Ix:** **Ankle-brachial index** (*why:* the simple bedside test that confirms and quantifies arterial disease, and it can be done with a hand-held Doppler in clinic; *what:* reduced index — `UNVERIFIED — the diagnostic and severity thresholds`). **Toe-brachial index or toe pressures where the ABI is falsely elevated** (*why:* **medial arterial calcification in diabetes and chronic kidney disease makes the ankle vessels incompressible, producing a normal or high ABI in a patient with severe disease** — this is a critical pitfall and diabetics are exactly the population most affected; *what:* reduced toe pressure). Exercise ABI (*why:* unmasks disease where the resting index is normal but symptoms are typical; *what:* fall in index after exercise). Duplex ultrasound (*why:* localises and grades the stenosis without contrast; *what:* stenosis site and severity). CT or MR angiography (*why:* defines anatomy for planning revascularisation; *what:* lesion location, length, run-off vessels). Fasting lipids, HbA1c, UEC, ECG (*why:* PAD mandates full cardiovascular risk assessment and management, which is where the mortality benefit lies; *what:* risk factors). Lumbar spine imaging where neurogenic claudication is suspected (*why:* the alternative diagnosis; *what:* canal stenosis).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Not usually required unless rest pain or acute ischaemia — see 0.6.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
**Smoking cessation, supervised exercise therapy, antiplatelet, statin, and control of blood pressure and diabetes.** Revascularisation — angioplasty, stenting or bypass — for lifestyle-limiting symptoms failing conservative management, or for limb-threatening ischaemia.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Ongoing cardiovascular risk management, foot care education, and annual assessment. **Foot care advice is essential in diabetes** — well-fitting footwear, daily inspection, podiatry, and prompt presentation with any wound.

## 0.6 Rest Pain, Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischaemia and Acute Limb Ischaemia

**D:** **Rest pain** is ischaemic pain at rest, indicating perfusion inadequate for basal metabolic requirements. With tissue loss or gangrene it constitutes **chronic limb-threatening ischaemia. Acute limb ischaemia** is a sudden reduction in limb perfusion threatening viability.

> [!tip] Rest pain has a characteristic and diagnostic history
> **Pain in the forefoot and toes, worse at night and when lying flat, relieved by hanging the leg out of bed or sleeping in a chair.**
> The mechanism is straightforward: lying flat removes the gravitational assistance to perfusion of a critically ischaemic foot, and hanging the leg down restores it. A patient describing exactly this has critical ischaemia and needs **urgent** vascular referral, not an outpatient appointment in six weeks.

> [!danger] Acute limb ischaemia — the six Ps, and which two matter most
> **Pain · Pallor · Pulselessness · Perishing cold · Paraesthesia · Paralysis.**
> **Paraesthesia and paralysis are the critical two.** The first four indicate ischaemia; **sensory and motor loss indicate that the nerves and muscle are dying and the limb is immediately threatened.** A patient with a cold pulseless but neurologically intact limb has time; one with sensory loss or weakness does not.
> **This is time-critical — muscle tolerates ischaemia for only a few hours.** Immediate vascular surgical involvement, and immediate anticoagulation while arranging it. `UNVERIFIED — the ischaemic time window, the Rutherford classification categories, and heparin dosing require verification.`

> [!info] Embolic versus thrombotic acute ischaemia
> | | Embolic | Thrombotic (on existing disease) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Onset | **Sudden, dramatic** | More gradual, may be less severe |
> | Prior claudication | **Absent** | Usually present |
> | Contralateral pulses | **Normal** | Also abnormal |
> | Source | **AF**, mural thrombus post-MI, aneurysm, valve | Existing atherosclerosis |
> | Treatment | Embolectomy | Bypass, thrombolysis, angioplasty |
> **A cold pulseless leg in a patient with atrial fibrillation and no history of claudication is embolic** — and this is where the history and the contralateral pulse examination do most of the diagnostic work.

> [!warning] Reperfusion injury
> Restoring flow to an ischaemic limb releases potassium, myoglobin and acid into the circulation, and causes compartment syndrome from muscle oedema — **exactly the crush syndrome physiology in [[A7]] 0.6.** Anticipate hyperkalaemia, arrhythmia, acute kidney injury and the need for fasciotomy. This is why revascularisation of a long-ischaemic limb is a decision made with the whole patient in view.

> [!danger] The diabetic foot is different
> **Neuropathy means the ulcer and the ischaemia may be painless**, so the classic history of rest pain is absent and the patient presents late with established tissue loss and infection. The triad is **neuropathy, ischaemia and infection**, and any diabetic foot ulcer warrants assessment of all three plus imaging for osteomyelitis. Palpable pulses do not exclude significant disease because of the calcification problem in 0.5.

**S/Smx:** Rest pain as described, non-healing ulceration, gangrene, dependent rubor, elevation pallor, absent pulses, and in acute ischaemia the six Ps.

**Ix:** **Urgent vascular surgical assessment — clinical, and it precedes imaging in acute ischaemia** (*why:* the limb is lost by delay, and a threatened limb with sensory or motor loss goes to theatre rather than to the CT scanner; *what:* viability category). ABI and toe pressures (*why:* quantifies perfusion, with the diabetic calcification caveat; *what:* reduced values). CT or MR angiography (*why:* defines anatomy for revascularisation planning, where time permits; *what:* occlusion level, run-off). ECG (*why:* **atrial fibrillation as an embolic source**, and it changes both the diagnosis and the long-term management; *what:* AF). Echocardiography (*why:* mural thrombus, valvular vegetation, or a cardiac source of embolus; *what:* thrombus, vegetation). **CK, UEC and potassium** (*why:* rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemia before and especially after reperfusion; *what:* raised CK, potassium). FBC, coagulation, group and hold (*why:* preoperative preparation and thrombophilia consideration in a young patient without atherosclerosis; *what:* baseline). Imaging for osteomyelitis in diabetic foot ulceration (*why:* determines antibiotic duration and the need for surgery, and probe-to-bone is a useful bedside test; *what:* bone involvement on MRI).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
**Acute limb ischaemia: immediate vascular surgical referral, anticoagulation, analgesia, nil by mouth, keep the limb dependent and warm but do not apply direct heat**, and resuscitate. **Do not wait for imaging in a limb with sensory or motor loss.**
**Rest pain without acute ischaemia:** urgent — not routine — vascular referral, analgesia, and cardiovascular risk management.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Embolectomy, thrombolysis, angioplasty or bypass. Amputation where the limb is unsalvageable. Fasciotomy for reperfusion compartment syndrome. **Treat the embolic source** — anticoagulation for AF.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Long-term anticoagulation where a cardiac embolic source is found. Full cardiovascular secondary prevention. **Amputation rehabilitation, prosthetics, and psychological support** where limb loss occurs. Diabetic foot surveillance with podiatry, since the contralateral limb is at high risk and the strongest predictor of amputation is a previous one.
