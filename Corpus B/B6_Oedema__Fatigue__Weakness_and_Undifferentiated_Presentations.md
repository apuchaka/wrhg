## 0.1 Oedema — Mechanism and Framework

**D:** Palpable swelling from expansion of the interstitial fluid volume.

**A/P:** Fluid movement across the capillary wall is governed by the **Starling forces**. Oedema arises when one of four things happens:
**1. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure** — venous obstruction, heart failure, sodium and water retention.
**2. Reduced plasma oncotic pressure** — hypoalbuminaemia from nephrotic syndrome, liver disease, malnutrition or protein-losing enteropathy.
**3. Increased capillary permeability** — inflammation, sepsis, burns, angioedema.
**4. Impaired lymphatic drainage** — lymphoedema from surgery, radiotherapy, malignancy, infection or congenital causes.
Identifying which of the four is operating usually identifies the diagnosis.

> [!tip] The three questions that sort almost every oedema
> **1. Bilateral or unilateral?** Unilateral points to a local cause — venous, lymphatic, infective or traumatic. Bilateral points to a systemic one.
> **2. Pitting or non-pitting?** **Pitting** indicates mobile interstitial fluid — cardiac, renal, hepatic, venous, drug-related. **Non-pitting** suggests **lymphoedema** (protein-rich, fibrotic) or **myxoedema** (mucopolysaccharide deposition in hypothyroidism).
> **3. What is the JVP doing?** **Raised JVP with oedema** → cardiac, pericardial, pulmonary hypertension, or volume overload. **Normal or low JVP with oedema** → hypoalbuminaemia, venous insufficiency, lymphatic obstruction, or drug-induced. This single observation splits the systemic causes efficiently and is free.

> [!warning] Look at the sacrum in a bedbound patient
> Oedema is **dependent**, so it accumulates wherever gravity takes it. In an ambulant patient that is the ankles; **in a bedbound patient it is the sacrum and the posterior thighs**, and examining only the legs will underestimate or entirely miss it. Roll the patient.

**S/Smx:** Swelling, tightness, heaviness, skin changes, and the associated features of the underlying cause. Ask about the timing — worse at the end of the day suggests venous or dependent; worse on waking with periorbital involvement suggests low oncotic pressure.

**Ix:** UEC with eGFR and **urine albumin-creatinine ratio or protein-creatinine ratio** (*why:* nephrotic-range proteinuria is a specific and easily identified cause that requires a nephrology pathway, and it is missed if urine is not tested; *what:* heavy proteinuria, renal impairment). **Serum albumin** (*why:* determines whether reduced oncotic pressure is contributing; *what:* hypoalbuminaemia). LFT (*why:* hepatic synthetic failure; *what:* low albumin, deranged synthetic function). **BNP or NT-proBNP and echocardiography** where cardiac cause is possible (*why:* separates cardiac from non-cardiac oedema, which the examination alone often cannot; *what:* elevation, ventricular function). TFTs (*why:* hypothyroid myxoedema causes non-pitting oedema; *what:* raised TSH). FBC (*why:* anaemia contributes to heart failure and to fatigue; *what:* haemoglobin). **Full medication review** (*why:* drug-induced oedema is common, entirely reversible, and repeatedly investigated as though it were organ failure — see 0.2; *what:* culprit agent).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Only where there is airway involvement (angioedema), acute limb ischaemia, or decompensated heart failure.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific, as below.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Compression, elevation, skin care and treatment of the underlying condition.

## 0.2 Bilateral and Generalised Oedema

**D:** Symmetrical oedema, usually dependent, from a systemic cause.

> [!danger] Check the medication list before investigating organ failure
> **Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers — amlodipine, felodipine, nifedipine — cause dose-dependent ankle oedema in a substantial proportion of patients.** The mechanism is preferential arteriolar dilatation raising capillary hydrostatic pressure, **not fluid retention** — which is why **diuretics do not help and simply cause volume depletion.** The treatment is dose reduction or a change of agent.
> This is one of the most commonly missed causes of bilateral leg swelling, and patients are frequently worked up for heart and renal failure while continuing the drug that is causing it.
> Other culprits: **NSAIDs · corticosteroids · pioglitazone · gabapentin and pregabalin · oestrogens and testosterone · minoxidil.**

> [!tip] The main systemic causes and their giveaways
> **Heart failure** — raised JVP, orthopnoea, PND, crackles, third heart sound, raised BNP.
> **Nephrotic syndrome** — **periorbital oedema on waking**, frothy urine, heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia, hyperlipidaemia. Investigate promptly; it carries thrombotic and infective risk.
> **Chronic liver disease** — ascites disproportionate to leg oedema, stigmata of chronic liver disease, low albumin.
> **Chronic venous insufficiency** — the commonest cause of chronic bilateral leg oedema. **Haemosiderin staining, lipodermatosclerosis ("inverted champagne bottle" leg), varicosities, and ulceration at the medial malleolus.** Worse at the end of the day, better on elevation overnight.
> **Dependency and immobility** — a genuine cause in people who sit all day, and it is under-recognised.
> **Pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale** — raised JVP with clear lungs, loud P2.
> **Constrictive pericarditis** — raised JVP with Kussmaul's sign, and it is easy to miss.
> **Hypothyroidism** — non-pitting.
> **Obesity and obstructive sleep apnoea** — frequently combined with several of the above.

> [!warning] Not all bilateral leg swelling with redness is cellulitis
> **Bilateral simultaneous cellulitis is rare.** Bilateral red, warm, swollen lower legs are far more often **venous stasis dermatitis or lipodermatosclerosis**, which are inflammatory but not infective and do not respond to antibiotics. Misdiagnosis leads to repeated antibiotic courses, admissions and resistance while the venous disease goes untreated.
> **If both legs look "cellulitic", question the diagnosis.**

**S/Smx:** Symmetrical pitting swelling, worse dependently, with the associated features above.

**Ix:** As per 0.1. Additionally, in suspected nephrotic syndrome: **lipids and screening for secondary causes** (*why:* hyperlipidaemia is part of the syndrome and diabetes, amyloid, myeloma, lupus and infections are treatable underlying causes; *what:* lipid profile, glucose, autoimmune and paraprotein screen). Venous duplex where venous insufficiency is suspected and intervention is contemplated (*why:* identifies reflux and obstruction amenable to treatment; *what:* reflux, obstruction). Sleep study where OSA is likely (*why:* untreated OSA drives pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Rarely required.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the cause. **Stop or reduce the offending drug.** Diuretics where there is genuine volume overload — **and not where the cause is a calcium channel blocker, lymphoedema or venous insufficiency**, where they do not work and cause harm.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Graduated compression stockings for venous insufficiency, after excluding significant arterial disease** — applying compression to an ischaemic limb causes necrosis, so **check the ABI or the pulses first.** Elevation, exercise, skin care and emollients. Weight management.

## 0.3 Unilateral Limb Swelling

**D:** Asymmetrical swelling of one limb, which shifts the differential toward a local cause.

> [!danger] Deep vein thrombosis until proven otherwise
> **Unilateral leg swelling is DVT until excluded**, and the classical features — calf tenderness, warmth, erythema, dilated superficial veins, a measured calf circumference difference — are individually unreliable, which is why structured risk assessment exists.
> Use a validated risk score, then D-dimer in low-probability patients and **compression ultrasound** in the rest. **A negative D-dimer in a high-probability patient does not exclude DVT** and should not be used that way. `UNVERIFIED — Wells score components and thresholds, age-adjusted D-dimer, and the scanning protocol including whether a repeat scan is required.`

> [!warning] Phlegmasia cerulea dolens
> Massive iliofemoral thrombosis causing a **grossly swollen, painful, cyanotic limb with compromised arterial inflow.** It threatens the limb and carries a risk of venous gangrene. It is a vascular emergency requiring urgent thrombolysis or thrombectomy, not routine anticoagulation alone.

> [!tip] The rest of the differential
> **Cellulitis** — fever, spreading erythema with a definable edge, portal of entry. **It can coexist with DVT**, and each is a risk factor for the other.
> **Ruptured Baker's cyst** — "pseudothrombophlebitis", with sudden calf pain and swelling in a patient with knee arthritis, sometimes with a crescent bruise below the malleolus.
> **Compartment syndrome** — pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch. Cross-refer [[A7_Burns__Chemical_Injury__Wounds_and_Crush_Injury]] 0.6.
> **Lymphoedema** — non-pitting, chronic, with skin thickening and **a positive Stemmer sign** (inability to pinch a fold of skin at the base of the second toe). Post-surgical, post-radiotherapy, post-infective, or malignant.
> **Post-thrombotic syndrome** — chronic swelling, pain and skin change after a previous DVT.
> **Pelvic or abdominal malignancy** obstructing venous or lymphatic return — consider particularly in unilateral swelling without another explanation.
> **May-Thurner syndrome** — left iliac vein compression by the right iliac artery, causing left-sided DVT in young patients.
> **Trauma, haematoma, and muscle tear.**

**S/Smx:** Swelling with a measurable circumference difference, pain, warmth, erythema, dilated superficial veins, and the specific features above.

**Ix:** Structured DVT risk assessment (*why:* determines whether D-dimer or imaging is the correct next test, and applying it is more reliable than clinical impression; *what:* probability category). **D-dimer in low-probability patients only** (*why:* useful for its negative predictive value in that group and uninterpretable in high-probability patients, the elderly, pregnancy, malignancy and post-operative states; *what:* negative result excluding DVT in low-risk patients). **Compression ultrasound** (*why:* the diagnostic test; *what:* non-compressible vein, thrombus). CT venography or MR venography for suspected iliac or pelvic thrombus (*why:* ultrasound is unreliable above the inguinal ligament and pelvic thrombus is missed; *what:* iliac thrombus, extrinsic compression). FBC, UEC, coagulation profile (*why:* baseline before anticoagulation and renal function for DOAC dosing; *what:* platelets, renal function). Assessment for occult malignancy in **unprovoked** thrombosis (*why:* unprovoked VTE has a meaningful yield for underlying cancer, and age-appropriate screening plus a careful history and examination is the accepted approach rather than indiscriminate whole-body imaging; *what:* symptoms, examination, up-to-date screening). Thrombophilia testing only in selected cases and **not during the acute event or while on anticoagulation** (*why:* results are unreliable acutely and rarely change management; *what:* deferred).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Anticoagulation for confirmed or strongly suspected DVT while awaiting imaging where the delay is significant. Urgent vascular involvement for phlegmasia. Antibiotics for cellulitis. Analgesia and elevation.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Anticoagulation for a duration determined by whether the event was provoked or unprovoked. Compression for post-thrombotic syndrome. Complex decongestive therapy for lymphoedema.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Cross-refer `TODO:link — J3 Bleeding & thrombosis (unbuilt)` Bleeding & Thrombosis. **Lymphoedema requires lifelong skin care, compression and prompt treatment of cellulitis**, and referral to a lymphoedema service — it is a chronic condition that is frequently left unmanaged after the initial cause is addressed.

## 0.4 Eyelid and Facial Swelling

**D:** Swelling of the periorbital tissues or face, which has a differential distinct from limb oedema because of the loose tissue planes and the anatomy behind them.

> [!danger] Orbital versus preseptal cellulitis — the distinction that saves sight
> **Preseptal (periorbital)** cellulitis is anterior to the orbital septum: eyelid swelling and erythema, **with normal eye movements, normal visual acuity, no proptosis and no pain on eye movement.** Usually managed with oral or intravenous antibiotics.
> **Orbital (post-septal)** cellulitis involves the orbit itself: **proptosis · pain on eye movement · restricted or painful ophthalmoplegia · reduced visual acuity · relative afferent pupillary defect · chemosis · systemic toxicity.**
> **Orbital cellulitis is a sight- and life-threatening emergency** — it causes optic nerve compression, abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis and intracranial spread. It requires **urgent CT orbits and sinuses, intravenous antibiotics, and ophthalmology and ENT involvement**, and it usually arises from ethmoid sinusitis.
> **Test eye movements and visual acuity in every swollen eyelid.** That examination is the entire distinction, and it takes a minute.

> [!warning] Angioedema
> Non-pitting, asymmetric swelling of lips, tongue, face or larynx. **Histamine-mediated** forms accompany urticaria and respond to adrenaline and antihistamines. **Bradykinin-mediated** forms — ACE inhibitor-induced and hereditary angioedema — have **no urticaria** and do not respond well to the anaphylaxis drugs. Cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.9 and [[A2_Airway_Compromise__Stridor_and_Tracheostomy_Emergencies]] 0.2. **Assess the airway first in any facial or tongue swelling.**

> [!tip] Superior vena cava obstruction
> **Facial, neck and bilateral upper limb swelling with plethora, distended non-pulsatile neck and chest wall veins, worse on lying flat or bending forward.** **Pemberton's sign** — facial plethora and distress on raising both arms above the head — supports it.
> Usually caused by malignancy, most often lung cancer or lymphoma, and increasingly by intravascular devices and pacing leads. It requires urgent imaging and oncological or interventional management. **A patient with new facial swelling and dilated chest wall veins needs a CT chest, not a course of antihistamines.**

> [!tip] The rest of the differential
> **Nephrotic syndrome** — **periorbital oedema worst on waking**, because overnight recumbency removes gravitational drainage from the loose periorbital tissue. Check the urine.
> **Hypothyroid myxoedema** — non-pitting, with coarse features and other hypothyroid signs.
> **Cushing syndrome** — moon facies, with the other cushingoid features.
> **Dermatomyositis** — **heliotrope periorbital rash with oedema**, Gottron papules and proximal weakness. Worth knowing because it is a paraneoplastic marker.
> **Dental and sinus infection**, and **contact dermatitis** from cosmetics or eye drops.

**S/Smx:** Swelling, erythema, pain, visual change, and the systemic features of the underlying cause.

**Ix:** **Visual acuity, eye movements and pupillary responses** (*why:* the examination that separates preseptal from orbital cellulitis, which determines whether this is an outpatient or an emergency; *what:* reduced acuity, painful or restricted movement, relative afferent pupillary defect). CT orbits and sinuses with contrast where orbital involvement is suspected (*why:* identifies orbital and subperiosteal abscess requiring drainage; *what:* post-septal inflammation, abscess, sinus disease). Urinalysis with protein quantification and albumin (*why:* nephrotic syndrome is a common and easily confirmed cause of periorbital oedema; *what:* heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia). CT chest where SVC obstruction is suspected (*why:* identifies the mass or thrombus and stages the malignancy; *what:* mediastinal mass, SVC compression or thrombus). TFTs (*why:* myxoedema; *what:* raised TSH). C1 esterase inhibitor level and function where recurrent angioedema without urticaria (*why:* hereditary angioedema requires entirely different treatment; *what:* low level or function). CK and autoimmune screen where dermatomyositis is suspected (*why:* confirms myositis and prompts malignancy screening; *what:* raised CK, myositis antibodies).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
**Airway assessment in facial or tongue swelling.** Urgent ophthalmology and intravenous antibiotics for orbital cellulitis. Adrenaline for anaphylaxis.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Drainage of orbital abscess. Oncological management and stenting for SVC obstruction.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Cessation and permanent documentation of an ACE inhibitor causing angioedema. Nephrology follow-up in nephrotic syndrome.

## 0.5 Fatigue, Lethargy and Malaise

**D:** A subjective sense of exhaustion, low energy or being unwell, disproportionate to activity and not relieved by rest.

> [!warning] Two opposite errors, and both are common
> **Over-investigation** — an indiscriminate battery of tests in a patient with an obvious psychosocial cause generates false positives, anxiety and further testing.
> **Dismissal** — attributing fatigue to stress or depression without a basic assessment misses anaemia, hypothyroidism, diabetes, malignancy, coeliac disease and sleep apnoea, all of which are common and treatable.
> The middle path is a **focused history, a proper examination, and a defined baseline panel**, with further testing directed by findings.

> [!danger] Red flags
> **Unintentional weight loss · fever or night sweats · lymphadenopathy · a short history in an older person · focal symptoms or signs · bone pain · new breathlessness · bruising or bleeding · a change from a previously well baseline over weeks rather than years.**

> [!tip] Categories worth working through
> **Haematological** — anaemia of any cause, and **iron deficiency without anaemia also causes fatigue**, so check ferritin rather than haemoglobin alone.
> **Endocrine** — hypothyroidism, diabetes, adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia.
> **Infective** — chronic viral infection including hepatitis and HIV, tuberculosis, infective endocarditis, and post-viral states.
> **Inflammatory** — connective tissue disease, inflammatory bowel disease, polymyalgia rheumatica.
> **Malignancy** — particularly haematological.
> **Cardiorespiratory** — heart failure, chronic lung disease.
> **Sleep** — **obstructive sleep apnoea is very commonly the answer and very commonly not asked about.** Ask about snoring, witnessed apnoeas, and daytime sleepiness as distinct from fatigue.
> **Drugs** — beta-blockers, antihistamines, opioids, antidepressants, statins, and alcohol.
> **Psychiatric** — **depression is the single commonest treatable cause**, and it must be asked about directly rather than inferred from a normal blood panel. Cross-refer `TODO:link — N4 Mood (unbuilt)`.
> **Coeliac disease** — frequently presents as fatigue with minimal gastrointestinal symptoms.

> [!info] Chronic fatigue and post-viral states
> Persistent debilitating fatigue with post-exertional worsening, unrefreshing sleep and cognitive difficulty, following an infection or arising insidiously, is a recognised clinical entity variously labelled ME/CFS or post-viral fatigue.
> **It is a real condition and dismissing it damages both the patient and the therapeutic relationship.** It is also a diagnosis made positively, after reasonable exclusion of alternatives, rather than a label for unexplained symptoms.
> **Management guidance has shifted substantially**, moving away from graded exercise as a universal prescription and toward **activity management and pacing that respects post-exertional symptom exacerbation**, alongside symptom-directed treatment and management of comorbid sleep and mood problems. `UNVERIFIED — the current recommended approach, and the status of graded exercise therapy, require verification against current guidance, as this area has changed and remains contested.`

**S/Smx:** Duration, pattern through the day, relation to exertion, sleep quality, mood, appetite, weight change, and the red flags above.

**Ix:** A **defined baseline panel** rather than an open-ended one — **FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP and ESR, TSH, HbA1c or fasting glucose, ferritin, calcium, and coeliac serology** (*why:* this set covers the great majority of organic causes at reasonable cost, and each abnormal result has a clear next step; *what:* anaemia, renal or hepatic disease, inflammation, thyroid dysfunction, diabetes, iron deficiency, hypercalcaemia, coeliac disease). Vitamin D and B12 where risk factors exist (*why:* deficiency is common in specific groups and correctable; *what:* deficiency). CK where muscle symptoms (*why:* myositis and statin myopathy; *what:* elevation). **Direct enquiry about mood, sleep and alcohol** (*why:* these are the commonest causes and are found by asking, not by testing; *what:* depression, OSA, alcohol use). Sleep study where OSA features are present (*why:* highly treatable and commonly missed; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index). HIV and hepatitis serology where risk factors (*why:* treatable chronic infection; *what:* positive serology). Chest radiograph and further imaging only where red flags direct it (*why:* untargeted imaging in fatigue has low yield and high incidentaloma rate; *what:* directed findings).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Rarely required.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the identified cause. Where none is found, **say so honestly rather than continuing to test**, and offer a structured follow-up plan with a review interval — this is more reassuring than open-ended investigation.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Sleep hygiene, graded and paced activity where appropriate, treatment of mood disorder, and continuity of care with one clinician, which matters more in this presentation than in almost any other.

## 0.6 Generalised Weakness

**D:** Reduced muscle power, which must first be distinguished from fatigue.

> [!danger] "Weak" means at least three different things
> **Ask the patient what they cannot do.**
> **· True weakness** — reduced power. "I can't lift my arm above my head", "I can't get out of a chair without using my hands." This is a neurological or muscular problem and requires localisation.
> **· Fatigue or asthenia** — power is normal but effort feels excessive. This is 0.5.
> **· Functional limitation** — pain, joint restriction or breathlessness limiting activity, with normal power.
> Conflating these sends the workup in entirely the wrong direction.

> [!tip] Localise true weakness
> **Upper motor neurone** — increased tone, brisk reflexes, upgoing plantars, pyramidal distribution.
> **Lower motor neurone** — wasting, fasciculation, reduced tone and reflexes.
> **Neuromuscular junction** — **fatigable** weakness, ptosis, diplopia, bulbar symptoms; worse through the day.
> **Muscle** — **symmetrical proximal** weakness, normal sensation, preserved reflexes until late. Difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, or raising the arms to comb hair.
> Cross-refer [[D4_Weakness__Neuropathy_and_Radiculopathy]] for the full neurological approach.

> [!warning] Acute or rapidly progressive weakness is an emergency
> **Guillain–Barré syndrome and myasthenic crisis** both present as ascending or fatigable weakness that can progress to respiratory failure over hours to days. **Monitor forced vital capacity, not oxygen saturation** — cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.5. Do not send a patient with new progressive weakness home to be seen in clinic.

> [!tip] The treatable systemic causes of proximal weakness
> **Electrolytes** — hypokalaemia, hyperkalaemia, hypercalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypophosphataemia.
> **Endocrine** — thyroid disease in both directions, **Cushing syndrome and exogenous corticosteroids (steroid myopathy)**, adrenal insufficiency, **osteomalacia from vitamin D deficiency**, hyperparathyroidism.
> **Drugs** — **statins**, colchicine, corticosteroids, and alcohol.
> **Inflammatory myopathy** — polymyositis and dermatomyositis, with a raised CK and, in dermatomyositis, the characteristic rash and a paraneoplastic association.
> **Deconditioning** — genuine, common after illness or hospitalisation, and improved by rehabilitation rather than by more tests.

**S/Smx:** Distribution and pattern of weakness, its time course, fatigability, associated sensory symptoms, bulbar and respiratory symptoms, and systemic features.

**Ix:** UEC with **potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphate** (*why:* electrolyte disturbance is a common, rapidly reversible cause of profound weakness; *what:* derangement). **CK** (*why:* separates myopathic from neuropathic causes and identifies statin myopathy and inflammatory myositis; *what:* elevation). TFTs (*why:* both hypo- and hyperthyroidism cause proximal myopathy; *what:* abnormal TSH). Vitamin D, calcium, phosphate and ALP (*why:* osteomalacia causes proximal weakness with bone pain and is fully treatable; *what:* low vitamin D, raised ALP). Morning cortisol or short synacthen where adrenal insufficiency is possible (*why:* treatable and easily missed; *what:* low cortisol). FBC, ESR and CRP (*why:* inflammatory and malignant causes; *what:* raised markers). **Serial forced vital capacity where weakness is acute or progressive** (*why:* the parameter that detects impending respiratory failure in neuromuscular disease, which gases and oximetry will not; *what:* declining FVC). Acetylcholine receptor antibodies, nerve conduction studies and EMG where indicated (*why:* confirm myasthenia and localise the lesion; *what:* antibodies, decrement, denervation). Malignancy screening in confirmed dermatomyositis (*why:* the paraneoplastic association is strong enough to warrant an age-appropriate search; *what:* underlying cancer).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Correct electrolytes. **Assess respiratory function in acute or progressive weakness and escalate.** Exclude cord compression where there is a sensory level, bladder or bowel disturbance, or back pain — cross-refer [[D3_Stroke_and_Focal_Neurological_Deficit]] and `TODO:link — L4 Back & neck pain (unbuilt)`.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Neurology involvement for suspected neuromuscular disease.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Physiotherapy and rehabilitation, which is the definitive treatment for deconditioning and an important adjunct in everything else. Medication review.

## 0.7 Generalised Pain

**D:** Widespread musculoskeletal pain affecting multiple regions, without a single localising lesion.

> [!info] Polymyalgia rheumatica — the one not to miss
> **Age over 50 — essentially never younger** · **bilateral shoulder and pelvic girdle pain and stiffness** · **prolonged morning stiffness** · raised ESR and CRP · systemic features of fatigue, weight loss and low-grade fever · and a **dramatic response to low-dose corticosteroids**, often within days, which is itself close to diagnostic.
> **Ask specifically about giant cell arteritis symptoms in every PMR patient** — new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, and visual disturbance. **GCA causes irreversible blindness and requires immediate high-dose steroids on suspicion, before biopsy.** The two conditions overlap substantially and the patient must be told what symptoms to report urgently. Cross-refer [[F0-5_Acute_Respiratory__Headache_and_Head_Injury]] 0.6.

> [!tip] Fibromyalgia is a positive diagnosis, not a residual label
> Widespread pain for months, with **fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, and cognitive difficulty ("fibro fog")**, often with a history of a triggering physical or psychological stressor. Examination shows widespread tenderness without synovitis, and inflammatory markers are normal.
> **It is diagnosed by its features, not by the absence of anything else**, and telling a patient "all your tests are normal" without a diagnosis is unhelpful and often harmful. Explaining central sensitisation — that the pain is real and reflects amplified pain processing rather than tissue damage — is a genuinely therapeutic intervention.
> **Management is exercise, sleep, psychological therapy and selected medications; opioids are ineffective and harmful.** `UNVERIFIED — diagnostic criteria and recommended pharmacotherapy.`

> [!danger] Red flags in widespread pain
> **Focal bone pain, particularly at night · unexplained weight loss · age over 50 with new onset · raised inflammatory markers · anaemia · hypercalcaemia · renal impairment · a history of malignancy · fever.**
> **The combination of bone pain, anaemia, renal impairment and hypercalcaemia is myeloma until proven otherwise** — the CRAB features — and it is a diagnosis routinely delayed by attributing back and rib pain to degenerative disease. Cross-refer `TODO:link — J4 Paraproteins & lymphoproliferative (unbuilt)`.
> **Metastatic bone disease** likewise presents as generalised or focal bone pain, and progressive pain unrelieved by rest with a known primary requires imaging.

> [!tip] The rest of the differential
> **Inflammatory arthritis** with polyarticular involvement · **hypothyroidism** · **vitamin D deficiency and osteomalacia** — bone pain with proximal weakness · **statin-associated muscle symptoms** · **depression**, which both causes and amplifies pain · **hypermobility spectrum disorders** in younger patients · **medication withdrawal**, including opioid-induced hyperalgesia in chronic opioid users.

**S/Smx:** Distribution, duration, diurnal pattern, morning stiffness and its duration, associated systemic features, sleep quality and mood.

**Ix:** **FBC, ESR and CRP** (*why:* separates inflammatory from non-inflammatory causes, which is the primary branch, and PMR and GCA both show markedly raised inflammatory markers; *what:* raised markers, anaemia). **Calcium, phosphate, ALP and vitamin D** (*why:* hypercalcaemia points to myeloma or malignancy, and low vitamin D with raised ALP suggests osteomalacia; *what:* the pattern). UEC (*why:* renal impairment as part of the CRAB features and as a cause of bone disease; *what:* creatinine). **Serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains where myeloma is possible** (*why:* the specific test, and it is not part of a routine panel so it must be asked for; *what:* paraprotein, abnormal light chain ratio). TFTs (*why:* hypothyroidism; *what:* raised TSH). CK (*why:* myositis and statin myopathy; *what:* elevation). Autoimmune serology where inflammatory features are present (*why:* connective tissue disease; *what:* ANA, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP — interpreted with the caveat that these have poor specificity when ordered without clinical indication). Imaging directed at focal pain (*why:* identifies metastasis, fracture and infection; *what:* lytic or sclerotic lesions, fracture).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
**High-dose corticosteroids immediately on suspicion of giant cell arteritis**, before temporal artery biopsy. Analgesia otherwise.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Corticosteroids with a slow taper for PMR, with steroid-sparing agents where relapse occurs.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Bone protection for anyone on prolonged corticosteroids** — calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate consideration, and monitoring for glucose intolerance. Multidisciplinary management in fibromyalgia with an emphasis on function rather than pain elimination.

## 0.8 Undifferentiated Lump

**D:** A palpable mass, of any site, requiring characterisation and a decision about investigation.

> [!tip] What to characterise
> **Site · size · shape · surface · consistency** (soft, firm, rubbery, hard) **· mobility** — mobile, tethered, or fixed to deep structures **· fluctuance · pulsatility and expansility** (an expansile mass is an aneurysm until proven otherwise) **· transillumination · overlying skin change · tenderness · temperature · relation to surrounding structures · duration and rate of growth.**
> **Rate of growth and fixity carry more diagnostic weight than any other features.**

> [!danger] Soft tissue sarcoma — do not biopsy before referral
> Features suggesting sarcoma: **larger than approximately 5 cm · deep to the deep fascia · firm or hard · fixed · increasing in size · painless · or recurrence after a previous "lipoma" excision.**
> **These lesions should be referred to a specialist sarcoma service for imaging and biopsy planning BEFORE any biopsy or excision is attempted.**
> An unplanned excision or a poorly placed biopsy tract contaminates tissue planes, converts a limb-sparing operation into a more extensive one, and worsens outcomes. **"I'll just take it out and send it" is the specific error this guidance exists to prevent.** `UNVERIFIED — the size threshold and referral criteria; obtain your state's sarcoma referral pathway.`

> [!warning] Lymphadenopathy
> **Localised** reactive nodes near a site of infection are common and benign. Concerning features: **size above roughly 1–2 cm persisting beyond a few weeks · hard or rubbery consistency · fixed or matted · painless · progressive · generalised distribution · accompanied by B symptoms — fever, night sweats, unexplained weight loss.**
> **Rubbery, painless, mobile nodes suggest lymphoma. Hard, fixed nodes suggest metastatic carcinoma.**
> **A supraclavicular node is pathological until proven otherwise** — the left one (Virchow's node) classically drains intra-abdominal malignancy. Never dismiss it.
> `UNVERIFIED — size thresholds and the duration beyond which investigation is indicated.`

> [!tip] Common benign lesions and their giveaways
> **Lipoma** — soft, lobulated, mobile, slow-growing, subcutaneous. **Epidermoid or sebaceous cyst** — attached to skin with a **central punctum**. **Ganglion** — near a joint or tendon sheath, transilluminates. **Abscess** — tender, hot, fluctuant, with overlying erythema. **Hernia** — reducible, with a cough impulse, at a hernial orifice. **Aneurysm** — **expansile** pulsation, not merely transmitted.

**S/Smx:** As above, with systemic symptoms — weight loss, fever, night sweats — and a full examination of regional nodes and the likely primary sites.

**Ix:** **Ultrasound as first-line for most superficial lumps** (*why:* distinguishes solid from cystic, assesses vascularity and depth relative to fascia, and is quick, cheap and radiation-free; *what:* cystic versus solid, depth, vascularity, nodal architecture). **MRI for any lesion suspected of being a sarcoma, arranged through the sarcoma service** (*why:* defines extent and relationship to neurovascular structures for surgical planning, and must precede biopsy; *what:* size, depth, compartment involvement). CT chest, abdomen and pelvis where malignancy is suspected (*why:* identifies a primary and stages disease; *what:* primary lesion, metastases). **Core biopsy or excision biopsy of a lymph node rather than fine needle aspiration where lymphoma is suspected** (*why:* lymphoma diagnosis requires assessment of nodal architecture, which cytology from an FNA cannot provide, so an FNA frequently produces a non-diagnostic result and delays diagnosis; *what:* histological subtype). FBC, LDH, film and ESR (*why:* haematological malignancy screening; *what:* cytopenias, blasts, raised LDH).
