## 0.1 The Deteriorating Patient — Recognition

**D:** Physiological decline in a ward inpatient that precedes arrest or ICU transfer, usually detectable in observations hours beforehand.

**R/Causes:** Post-operative day 1–3, recent ICU step-down, age, frailty, sepsis, opioid or sedative administration, new oxygen requirement, missed or incomplete observation rounds, out-of-hours and weekend admission, language barrier or delirium limiting symptom reporting.

**A/P:** Physiological insult → compensation (tachycardia, tachypnoea, vasoconstriction) maintains normal BP and conscious state → compensatory reserve exhausted → hypotension and altered mentation appear late → tissue hypoperfusion → organ failure → arrest. The clinical point: **blood pressure falls last.** Respiratory rate rises first and is the single most predictive and most commonly unrecorded observation.

**S/Smx:** Rising respiratory rate, new or increasing oxygen requirement, tachycardia, narrowing pulse pressure, falling urine output, new confusion or agitation, "just not right" from nursing staff or family. The last of these carries genuine predictive weight and is a formal escalation criterion in most Australian track-and-trigger systems.

> [!danger] The three that get missed
> **Rising respiratory rate with a normal saturation** — compensation, not reassurance. **New confusion in an older patient** — assume hypoxia, hypoglycaemia or sepsis before assuming delirium. **A single grossly abnormal observation** — one red-zone parameter triggers escalation on its own; you do not need a total score.

> [!info] Track-and-trigger
> Australian hospitals use a colour-zoned observation chart with graded escalation (nursing review → registrar → rapid response/MET). Zone boundaries and the MET call criteria differ between health networks. `UNVERIFIED — do not memorise generic numbers; obtain the observation chart and MET criteria for your own health network, as these are what you will be examined and audited against.`

**Ix:** Full set of vital signs including respiratory rate counted for a full minute and conscious state (*why:* the escalation decision is made on observations, not on tests, and an estimated respiratory rate is the commonest source of a missed deterioration; *what:* rising RR, widening trend across the chart rather than a single reading). Bedside glucose (*why:* hypoglycaemia is instantly reversible and mimics almost every neurological deterioration; *what:* low value demanding immediate treatment). VBG or ABG with lactate (*why:* separates a compensating patient from one already in anaerobic metabolism, and gives CO₂ which pulse oximetry cannot; *what:* rising lactate, or a rising CO₂ signalling ventilatory failure in a patient who looks merely drowsy). FBC, UEC, CRP (*why:* baseline organ function and infective marker to direct the search for a cause; *what:* new renal impairment, leucocytosis or leucopenia). Blood cultures before antibiotics if infection is plausible (*why:* yield falls sharply once antibiotics are given; *what:* organism and sensitivities). ECG (*why:* arrhythmia and ischaemia are silent causes of deterioration in the elderly and diabetic; *what:* new arrhythmia, ischaemic change). Erect CXR (*why:* pneumonia, pulmonary oedema and pneumothorax are the highest-frequency ward causes of a new oxygen requirement; *what:* consolidation, effusion, upper-lobe diversion, absent lung markings).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E assessment at the bedside before any investigation. Airway patency, oxygen titrated to target saturation, IV access, fluids or vasopressor decision per [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.2 if sepsis is suspected, bedside glucose, and escalation according to local criteria. Escalate *while* assessing, not after — the call is not a failure of your own assessment. Document the trigger, the time, who was called, and the response.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Directed at the identified cause. The parallel task is a resuscitation-status and goals-of-care conversation if none is documented: deterioration is the point at which an undocumented ceiling of care causes harm in both directions. See [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] Capacity Concern and Treatment Refusal.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Post-event review, incident documentation, and where relevant an ICU outreach follow-up. Structured handover using ISBAR at every transition of care.

## 0.2 SIRS, Sepsis and Septic Shock — Definitions

**D:** Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Septic shock is the subset with circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormality carrying substantially higher mortality.

**R:**
*Unmodifiable:* Extremes of age, neonatal period, pregnancy and the postpartum period, asplenia, genetic immunodeficiency.
*Modifiable:* Indwelling devices (IDC, central line, prosthetic joint), immunosuppressive therapy, chemotherapy-induced neutropenia, poorly controlled diabetes, malnutrition, incomplete vaccination, injecting drug use, recent surgery or instrumentation.

**A/P:** Pathogen-associated molecular patterns recognised by innate immune receptors → massive cytokine release → endothelial glycocalyx injury and widespread vasodilatation → capillary leak with intravascular volume loss into the interstitium → simultaneous microvascular thrombosis and impaired oxygen extraction → tissue hypoxia despite adequate or supranormal cardiac output → mitochondrial dysfunction → multi-organ failure.

**S/Smx:** Fever or hypothermia, rigors, tachycardia, tachypnoea, confusion, oliguria, mottled peripheries and prolonged capillary refill. Warm vasodilated peripheries early, cold and shut-down late. **Hypothermia, a normal white cell count, and absence of fever do not exclude sepsis and are markers of worse outcome, particularly in the elderly, the neonate, and the immunosuppressed.**

> [!info] Why SIRS was retired as a sepsis definition
> SIRS criteria (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, white cell count) describe a generic inflammatory response and fire in pancreatitis, trauma, burns and post-operative states with no infection at all. They were both non-specific and insufficiently sensitive — a meaningful minority of patients with infection and organ failure never met two SIRS criteria. Sepsis-3 replaced them with organ dysfunction assessed by SOFA. SIRS remains a valid description of systemic inflammation; it is no longer the definition of sepsis. `UNVERIFIED — confirm the specific SIRS threshold values and the SOFA/qSOFA component cut-offs against a current source before quoting numbers in an exam.`

> [!warning] qSOFA is a prognostic flag, not a screening tool
> Its components are altered mentation, respiratory rate and systolic blood pressure. It identifies infected patients at higher risk of poor outcome. It is insensitive as a bedside screen, and a negative qSOFA must not be used to stand down concern about sepsis. `UNVERIFIED — confirm component thresholds.`

**Ix:** Serum lactate (*why:* the single most useful marker of tissue hypoperfusion and the trigger for escalating resuscitation; *what:* elevation, and more importantly failure to clear on repeat). Blood cultures, two sets from separate sites before antibiotics (*why:* organism identification permits de-escalation from empirical therapy, and yield collapses after the first dose; *what:* growth with sensitivities). FBC, UEC, LFT, coagulation profile (*why:* these populate the organ-dysfunction assessment and reveal the coagulopathy and renal and hepatic failure that define sepsis rather than simple infection; *what:* thrombocytopenia, rising creatinine, rising bilirubin, deranged INR). CRP (*why:* supports but never establishes the diagnosis and is too slow to guide the first hours; *what:* elevation with a rising trend). Site-directed cultures — urine, sputum, wound swab, CSF, aspirated collection (*why:* the source must be found because source control determines survival more than antibiotic choice; *what:* localising growth). Imaging directed at the suspected source, commonly CXR, urinary tract ultrasound or CT abdomen (*why:* identifies a collection or obstruction requiring drainage; *what:* consolidation, hydronephrosis, abscess, free gas).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Resuscitation and empirical antibiotics run in parallel, not in sequence. The components are oxygen, blood cultures, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics, IV fluid resuscitation, lactate measurement, and urine output monitoring. Antibiotic choice is source- and local-resistance-dependent and must come from eTG Antibiotic and your hospital's antimicrobial guideline — `UNVERIFIED — no empirical regimen, dose, or fluid volume is stated here; obtain both from eTG Antibiotic and local policy. The timing target for antibiotic administration and the initial fluid bolus volume in Australian practice both require verification against the current Sepsis Clinical Care Standard.`

Fluid resuscitation is given in boluses with reassessment after each. In cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, end-stage renal failure and severe malnutrition, the volume that helps a young septic patient will drown a frail one — reassess after every bolus rather than prescribing a total.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
**Source control is the definitive treatment.** Drain the collection, remove the infected line, relieve the obstructed ureter, debride the necrotic tissue. Antibiotics without source control fail. Escalate to ICU for vasopressor support if hypotension persists after fluid resuscitation — see [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.3 for phenotype-specific management and cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]].

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
De-escalate antibiotics on culture results and reassess duration daily. Post-sepsis syndrome — persistent fatigue, cognitive impairment, functional decline — is common and under-recognised; flag it for the GP in the discharge summary.

## 0.3 Sepsis Phenotypes by Source

**D:** Recognisable clinical patterns of sepsis defined by the site of origin, each with a characteristic presentation, investigation set and source-control requirement.

**A/P:** Source determines organism → organism and site determine empirical antibiotic → site determines whether drainage, removal or relief of obstruction is required → time to source control determines mortality.

> [!tip] Source-based comparison
> | Source | Points toward it | Source control step |
> |---|---|---|
> | Urinary / urosepsis | Dysuria, loin pain, IDC in situ, elderly with confusion alone | Relieve obstruction, change or remove catheter |
> | Biliary (cholangitis) | RUQ pain, jaundice, rigors; the classic triad | ERCP or percutaneous drainage |
> | Respiratory | Cough, focal chest signs, new oxygen requirement | Drain empyema if present |
> | Skin / soft tissue | Erythema, pain out of proportion, crepitus | Urgent surgical debridement if necrotising |
> | Intra-abdominal | Peritonism, recent surgery, free gas | Laparotomy or radiological drain |
> | Line-related | Rigors on flushing, no other source, erythematous exit site | Remove the line |
> | Meningococcal | Non-blanching rash, meningism, rapid deterioration in a young person | None surgical; antibiotics are time-critical |
> | Neutropenic | Recent chemotherapy, fever, often no localising signs at all | None; treat as emergency regardless of appearance |

> [!danger] Two that kill fast and look deceptively well early
> **Necrotising soft tissue infection** — pain disproportionate to visible skin change, rapid progression, systemic toxicity out of keeping with the rash. The skin is the last thing to look bad. Surgical, not medical. **Febrile neutropenia** — no localising signs and an unimpressive examination is the typical presentation, not a reassuring one.

**S/Smx:** As per the table above. The recurring trap is the elderly patient with no fever, no localising symptom and only new confusion or a fall — urinary and respiratory sepsis both present this way.

**Ix:** Source-directed cultures before antibiotics (*why:* the empirical regimen differs by source and de-escalation depends on isolate; *what:* organism and sensitivities). Urinalysis and urine culture (*why:* commonest occult source in the elderly; *what:* pyuria, nitrites, growth — noting that asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in the catheterised elderly and does not by itself establish the source). LFT and biliary ultrasound where RUQ pain or jaundice (*why:* cholangitis requires drainage, not just antibiotics; *what:* duct dilatation, stone, thickened gallbladder wall). CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast where an intra-abdominal source is suspected (*why:* identifies the collection that must be drained; *what:* abscess, free gas, ischaemic bowel). Skin marking of the erythema border with time noted (*why:* rate of spread distinguishes cellulitis from a necrotising process and is free; *what:* advancing margin over minutes to hours).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
As per 0.2.1, plus early surgical or interventional radiology referral where the phenotype implies drainage. Cross-refer [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.2 and the existing framework entries for Adult Sepsis, Paediatric Sepsis, Meningococcal Sepsis, Urosepsis and Biliary Sepsis rather than restating them.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Source control as per the table. Where the source is genuinely unclear and the patient is deteriorating, this is an argument for earlier cross-sectional imaging, not for waiting.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Device review and replacement planning, immunisation for asplenic and immunocompromised patients, and GP follow-up for recurrent urinary sepsis.

## 0.4 Cardiac Arrest

**D:** Cessation of effective cardiac output with unresponsiveness and absent or abnormal breathing.

**R/Causes:** In-hospital arrest is usually the end of an unrecognised deterioration rather than a sudden event — hypoxia and hypovolaemia dominate. Out-of-hospital arrest is more often primary arrhythmic from ischaemic heart disease.

**A/P:** Loss of cardiac output → cerebral perfusion ceases within seconds → unconsciousness → agonal gasping (a brainstem reflex, frequently misread as breathing) → global ischaemia → irreversible neuronal injury. Chest compression generates a fraction of normal cardiac output; every interruption drops coronary perfusion pressure, which then takes several compressions to rebuild. This is why interruptions are minimised.

**S/Smx:** Unresponsive, not breathing normally. **Agonal gasps are not breathing.** Pulse checks are unreliable and should not delay compressions.

> [!tip] Reversible causes — 4 Hs and 4 Ts
> Hypoxia · Hypovolaemia · Hypo/hyperkalaemia and metabolic · Hypothermia · Tension pneumothorax · Tamponade · Toxins · Thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary). Work through these explicitly during rhythm checks rather than relying on recall under pressure.

> [!warning] Shockable vs non-shockable
> VF and pulseless VT are shockable. Asystole and PEA are not. The commonest in-hospital rhythm is PEA, where the entire management value lies in finding the reversible cause — defibrillation has nothing to offer and searching for the H's and T's is the treatment.

**Ix:** Rhythm assessment at the defibrillator (*why:* determines the entire algorithm branch; *what:* shockable versus non-shockable). Bedside glucose (*why:* reversible and takes seconds; *what:* hypoglycaemia). VBG or ABG with potassium (*why:* hyper- and hypokalaemia are treatable arrest causes and potassium comes back in minutes on a gas; *what:* severe derangement). Bedside echocardiography during the rhythm check pause where a skilled operator is present (*why:* distinguishes true PEA from pseudo-PEA and identifies tamponade, right heart strain and hypovolaemia; *what:* pericardial fluid, dilated RV, empty underfilled LV — noting it must not extend the pause). Waveform capnography once the airway is secured (*why:* confirms tube placement, gives real-time compression quality feedback, and a sudden rise suggests return of spontaneous circulation; *what:* trace present and rising).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Call for help and the arrest trolley. Start chest compressions immediately. Attach the defibrillator as soon as it arrives and follow the ANZCOR algorithm for the identified rhythm, minimising interruptions and rotating the compressor regularly to limit fatigue. `UNVERIFIED — compression rate, compression depth, compression-to-ventilation ratio, defibrillation energy levels, and the dose and interval for adrenaline and amiodarone are all deliberately omitted here. Obtain them directly from ANZCOR Guideline 11 (adult ALS) and Guideline 12/13 (paediatric and newborn). The paediatric adrenaline timing error already found in this corpus came from exactly this category of figure.` Treat reversible causes in parallel.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Post-ROSC care: targeted oxygenation and ventilation (avoid both hyperoxia and hypocapnia), 12-lead ECG with early consideration of coronary angiography, haemodynamic support, temperature management, and ICU transfer. `UNVERIFIED — oxygen saturation targets, CO₂ targets and temperature management parameters require verification against current ANZCOR post-resuscitation guidance.`

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Neurological prognostication is deferred and is not an intern task. Family communication, debrief for the team, and referral for implantable defibrillator assessment in survivors of arrhythmic arrest.

## 0.5 Post-Procedural Deterioration

**D:** New physiological instability in the hours following surgery, endoscopy, or an interventional procedure.

**R/Causes:** Anaesthetic and analgesic agents, procedure-specific complications, pre-existing cardiorespiratory disease, prolonged operative time, large fluid shifts, unrecognised pre-operative sepsis.

**A/P:** The differential organises by time from procedure. Immediate (minutes to hours) → bleeding, anaesthetic effect, residual neuromuscular blockade, airway obstruction, anaphylaxis. Early (hours to 48 h) → haemorrhage, hypovolaemia, atelectasis, myocardial ischaemia, aspiration. Later (day 2 onward) → sepsis from anastomotic leak or surgical site infection, pulmonary embolism, delirium.

**S/Smx:** Tachycardia and hypotension suggest bleeding until proven otherwise, particularly with abdominal distension, pain out of keeping with expectation, or falling haemoglobin. New hypoxia suggests atelectasis, aspiration, pulmonary oedema or PE. Pain, agitation and confusion are non-specific and are frequently attributed to the procedure when they are the first sign of haemorrhage or sepsis.

> [!danger] The default assumption
> **Tachycardia with hypotension in the first 24 hours post-operatively is bleeding until excluded.** Do not attribute it to pain, anxiety or anaesthetic effect without examining the wound, the drains and the abdomen, and checking a haemoglobin.

> [!warning] Anastomotic leak
> Presents around days 3–7 with tachycardia, low-grade fever, ileus, and a patient who is "not progressing" rather than one who is obviously peritonitic. New atrial fibrillation post-operatively is a recognised early flag. Escalate to the surgical team early; the diagnosis is missed by waiting for classical peritonism.

**Ix:** FBC with comparison to the pre-operative value (*why:* establishes whether haemoglobin has fallen and by how much, which prose descriptions of the wound will not tell you; *what:* falling Hb, and a platelet count for coagulopathy). UEC and lactate (*why:* lactate rises in both haemorrhagic and septic deterioration and is the earliest objective marker; *what:* elevation, failure to clear). Coagulation profile and group and hold or crossmatch (*why:* transfusion may be needed and the delay is in the crossmatch, not the decision; *what:* correctable coagulopathy, units available). ECG and troponin (*why:* peri-operative myocardial infarction is frequently silent and presents as unexplained hypotension or dyspnoea; *what:* ischaemic change, troponin rise). Erect CXR (*why:* atelectasis, aspiration, effusion, pneumothorax after central line insertion; *what:* consolidation, collapse, absent lung markings). CT with contrast directed at the operative site where leak or collection is suspected (*why:* the only reliable way to identify a leak before peritonism develops; *what:* free gas, extraluminal contrast, collection). Wound and drain inspection with output volume and character documented (*why:* costs nothing and frequently gives the diagnosis; *what:* frank blood, enteric content, expanding haematoma).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E, oxygen, IV access, fluid resuscitation, and **call the surgical team that performed the procedure.** They know the specific complications of that operation and whether return to theatre is indicated. Do not work the patient up in isolation before informing them.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Return to theatre for bleeding or leak, drainage of collection, or medical management of the identified cause. Cross-refer [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.2 where the picture is septic.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
VTE prophylaxis review, early mobilisation, and clear documentation of the complication for the discharge summary and the operating surgeon.

## 0.6 Failure to Wake Post-Sedation

**D:** Persistently reduced conscious state after procedural sedation or anaesthesia beyond the expected recovery interval for the agents used.

**R/Causes:** Opioid or benzodiazepine effect, particularly with renal or hepatic impairment or in the elderly; residual neuromuscular blockade; hypoglycaemia; hypercapnia; hypoxia; hypothermia; stroke or intracranial event; hyponatraemia; seizure with prolonged post-ictal state; underlying dementia unmasked.

**A/P:** Sedative agent with prolonged clearance in the elderly or organ-impaired patient → continued CNS depression → hypoventilation → CO₂ retention → CO₂ narcosis deepens the depression → a self-reinforcing loop that looks like simple over-sedation but is a ventilatory failure requiring active management.

**S/Smx:** Unrousable or minimally rousable, slow shallow respiration, small pupils in opioid effect. The discriminators worth holding: **pinpoint pupils with a low respiratory rate** points to opioid; **normal pupils with a rising CO₂** points to hypercapnic ventilatory failure; **any lateralising sign** points away from drug effect entirely and demands urgent imaging.

> [!danger] Do not accept "still sedated" as a diagnosis
> A focal neurological deficit, an asymmetric pupil, or a conscious state that is deteriorating rather than improving is a neurological emergency, not a pharmacological one. Hypoglycaemia is checked in every single case because it is instantly reversible and costs seconds.

> [!warning] Reversal agents
> Naloxone and flumazenil have real hazards. Naloxone precipitates acute pain and withdrawal and has a shorter duration than most opioids, so re-sedation follows. Flumazenil can precipitate seizures in benzodiazepine-dependent patients and in mixed overdose, particularly with tricyclics, and is generally avoided in the undifferentiated patient. `UNVERIFIED — doses, titration increments and duration of action for both agents require verification against eTG and your local sedation policy before use.`

**Ix:** Bedside glucose (*why:* immediately reversible, mimics everything, takes seconds; *what:* hypoglycaemia). ABG or VBG (*why:* the pivotal test — distinguishes CO₂ narcosis from pure drug effect, and pulse oximetry cannot detect hypercapnia in a patient on supplemental oxygen; *what:* raised pCO₂ with respiratory acidosis). UEC including sodium (*why:* hyponatraemia and renal impairment both cause and prolong reduced consciousness; *what:* low sodium, impaired clearance). Non-contrast CT brain where any focal sign, asymmetric pupil, or unexplained persistence (*why:* excludes haemorrhage and large infarct, which are not survivable to miss; *what:* bleed, established infarct, mass effect). Train-of-four monitoring if available and neuromuscular blockade was used (*why:* residual paralysis presents as an apparently unresponsive patient who is in fact awake and unable to move; *what:* incomplete reversal). Medication chart and anaesthetic record review (*why:* the answer is usually written down — cumulative doses, timing, agents given in theatre and recovery; *what:* total opioid and benzodiazepine load, and the interval since the last dose).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E with attention to airway patency and ventilation. Oxygen, positioning, and consider airway adjunct or bag-mask support if ventilation is inadequate. Bedside glucose in every case. Consider reversal only where the agent is known and the clinical picture fits — with the caveats above. Escalate to the anaesthetic or ICU team.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the identified cause. Where hypercapnia is the driver, ventilatory support rather than reversal is the answer. Where imaging shows an intracranial event, this becomes a stroke or neurosurgical pathway — see [[04_Neurology]] and [[A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest]] 0.1.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Document the event and the agents involved. Flag reduced sedative requirement for future procedures, particularly in the elderly and the renally impaired, and update the anaesthetic alert on the record.
