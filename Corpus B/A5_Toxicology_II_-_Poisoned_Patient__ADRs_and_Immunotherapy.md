## 0.1 The Poisoned Patient — Risk Assessment and General Approach

**D:** The structured approach to any suspected poisoning, deliberate or accidental, occupational or environmental.

**A/P:** Outcome is determined by the interaction of agent, dose, time since exposure and patient factors — not by the drug name alone. **A "large" overdose of a benign agent may need nothing, while a small ingestion of a high-toxicity agent in a child is an emergency.** Risk assessment, not identification, drives every subsequent decision.

> [!tip] The sequence
> **1. Resuscitate** — A–E, glucose, monitoring. Resuscitation precedes identification.
> **2. Risk assess** — agent, dose, formulation (immediate versus slow-release), time since ingestion, co-ingestants, patient weight and comorbidity, and the clinical course so far. This determines everything else.
> **3. Decontaminate** — where indicated and within the useful window.
> **4. Enhance elimination** — urinary alkalinisation, dialysis, repeat-dose charcoal for selected agents.
> **5. Antidote** — where one exists and is indicated.
> **6. Supportive care** — which is what actually saves most poisoned patients.
> **7. Disposition and psychiatric assessment.**

> [!danger] Screening tests in every deliberate self-poisoning
> **Paracetamol level** — common, co-ingested, silent for a day, lethal if missed. Take it regardless of the stated history.
> **ECG** — QRS and QT reveal sodium and potassium channel blockade independent of what the patient says they took.
> **Bedside glucose.**
> Add a **salicylate level** where the acid-base picture is mixed or unexplained.
> The history in deliberate self-poisoning is unreliable in both directions — under- and over-reporting are both common — and these three tests do not depend on it.

> [!warning] Call the Poisons Information Centre — 13 11 26
> This is the expected step in Australian practice, not a fallback. They have access to current toxicological resources, national antivenom and antidote stock locations, and a toxicologist. In an exam, "contact the Poisons Information Centre" is a correct and expected component of the answer.

> [!info] Decontamination and elimination — the principles, not the numbers
> **Activated charcoal** adsorbs many but not all toxins — it does not bind alcohols, metals including iron and lithium, or corrosives. It carries aspiration risk and requires an intact or protected airway. `UNVERIFIED — the time window in which charcoal retains benefit, dosing, and contraindications require verification against eTG Toxicology.`
> **Whole bowel irrigation** is considered for slow-release preparations, body packers and agents charcoal does not bind.
> **Urinary alkalinisation** enhances elimination of weak acids, notably salicylate — cross-refer [[F0-2_Acid-Base__DKA_and_Fluid_States]] 0.8.
> **Haemodialysis** is effective for small, water-soluble, minimally protein-bound toxins with a low volume of distribution — toxic alcohols, salicylate, lithium, metformin. `UNVERIFIED — indications and thresholds require verification and are agent-specific.`
> **Intravenous lipid emulsion** has a role in local anaesthetic systemic toxicity and is used in some refractory lipophilic drug toxicities. `UNVERIFIED — indications and regimen.`

> [!warning] Occupational and chemical exposure
> **Decontaminate before or while treating, with appropriate PPE** — secondary contamination of the treating team is a real and preventable event. Obtain the **Safety Data Sheet** for the substance, which is legally required to be available in Australian workplaces and gives composition and first aid guidance. Consider notification to SafeWork SA and, for some agents, the public health unit. Cross-refer `TODO:link — GER3 Preventive & occupational health (unbuilt; was P1 in build queue v1)` Occupational Exposure and [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.3 for organophosphates.

**S/Smx:** Determined by agent. Approach it by toxidrome — cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.1 for the comparison table.

**Ix:** Paracetamol level, ECG and bedside glucose in every deliberate ingestion, as above (*why:* the history is unreliable and these three detect the treatable and lethal misses; *what:* level against time, QRS and QT, glucose). VBG with electrolytes, anion gap and lactate (*why:* identifies the metabolic acidosis and osmolar-gap pattern that points to specific agents; *what:* HAGMA, lactate, potassium). UEC, LFT, coagulation profile (*why:* baseline organ function, and hepatic and renal impairment alter both toxicity and elimination; *what:* derangement). Serum osmolality where toxic alcohol is plausible (*why:* the gap is raised before the anion gap rises; *what:* osmolar gap — cross-refer [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.8). Specific drug levels where they change management — paracetamol, salicylate, lithium, digoxin, iron, carbamazepine, phenytoin, theophylline (*why:* these are the agents where a level alters the decision, whereas a broad "drug screen" rarely does; *what:* level). Urine drug screen only in specific circumstances (*why:* it detects classes rather than quantities, has long detection windows for some agents and misses many, and **rarely changes acute management** — it is over-ordered; *what:* qualitative result, interpreted cautiously). Pregnancy test in women of reproductive age (*why:* alters both fetal risk assessment and some antidote decisions; *what:* positive test).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E, glucose, monitoring, IV access. Call 13 11 26. Screening bloods and ECG. Decontamination where indicated.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Agent-specific antidote, enhanced elimination, and supportive care. Observation period determined by agent and formulation — **slow-release preparations require far longer observation than the patient's current appearance suggests.**

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Mental health assessment before discharge in every deliberate self-poisoning, regardless of how medically trivial the ingestion proved to be.** Means restriction discussion with the patient and family where appropriate, drug and alcohol referral, GP follow-up, and medication supply review. ## 0.2 Tricyclic Antidepressant Overdose

**D:** Poisoning by tricyclic antidepressants, producing a combined sodium channel blockade, anticholinergic and alpha-blocking syndrome with rapid potential for deterioration.

**R/Causes:** Amitriptyline, nortriptyline, doxepin, clomipramine, imipramine. Still prescribed for neuropathic pain, migraine prophylaxis, insomnia and enuresis as well as depression — **so the tablets are frequently in households where the indication was not psychiatric**, which is worth remembering when assessing availability.

**A/P:** Three simultaneous mechanisms.
**Fast sodium channel blockade** in the myocardium → slowed phase 0 depolarisation → **QRS widening, right axis shift, arrhythmia** and reduced contractility. Acidosis worsens this, since more drug is in the ionised active form.
**Anticholinergic** — the toxidrome in [[F0-1_Toxidromes__Allergic_Emergencies_and_Envenomation]] 0.2: dry hot skin, mydriasis, ileus, retention, delirium.
**Alpha-1 blockade** → vasodilatation → hypotension, which compounds the myocardial depression.
Additionally, GABA-A antagonism → **seizures**, which cause acidosis, which worsens the sodium channel blockade — a self-reinforcing loop.

> [!danger] TCA overdose deteriorates fast
> A patient can look well and be arresting within an hour. **Anticholinergic features are the distraction; the ECG is the prognostic test.** Any patient with a possible TCA ingestion needs continuous cardiac monitoring and serial ECGs, not a single one at triage.

> [!tip] The ECG findings
> **QRS widening** — the key finding, and its degree correlates with the risk of seizures and ventricular arrhythmia. **A terminal R wave in aVR** with a rightward terminal QRS axis is characteristic of sodium channel blockade. QT prolongation also occurs. `UNVERIFIED — the specific QRS duration thresholds associated with seizure and arrhythmia risk, and the threshold at which bicarbonate is indicated, require verification against eTG Toxicology and the Poisons Information Centre.`

> [!warning] Three things not to do
> **Do not give flumazenil** if a benzodiazepine co-ingestion is suspected — removing benzodiazepine protection in a TCA overdose can precipitate seizures.
> **Do not give class Ia or Ic antiarrhythmics** (procainamide, flecainide) — they are themselves sodium channel blockers and will worsen the toxicity.
> **Do not allow the patient to become acidotic**, including by under-ventilating an intubated patient — acidosis increases the active drug fraction.

**S/Smx:** Anticholinergic features, drowsiness progressing to coma, seizures, hypotension, tachycardia, and arrhythmia.

**Ix:** Serial ECG with continuous monitoring (*why:* QRS width is the prognostic marker and it evolves over hours, so a single normal ECG at presentation does not exclude significant toxicity; *what:* QRS duration, terminal R in aVR, QT, arrhythmia). VBG with pH (*why:* acidosis worsens sodium channel blockade and the pH is a therapeutic target in its own right; *what:* pH, bicarbonate). UEC with potassium (*why:* bicarbonate therapy drives potassium down and hypokalaemia is arrhythmogenic; *what:* potassium, serially). Paracetamol level (*why:* co-ingestion; *what:* level against time). Bedside glucose (*why:* reversible cause of the altered conscious state; *what:* glucose). CK where prolonged seizures or immobility (*why:* rhabdomyolysis; *what:* elevation). TCA levels are **not** useful acutely (*why:* they correlate poorly with clinical severity and turn around too slowly; *what:* not indicated for acute management).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Continuous cardiac monitoring, IV access, airway protection where conscious state is reduced. **Sodium bicarbonate for QRS widening, arrhythmia or hypotension** — it works by both increasing extracellular sodium and alkalinising, reducing the active ionised drug fraction. Benzodiazepines for seizures. Fluid and vasopressor for hypotension. Charcoal in early presentations with a protected airway. Call 13 11 26. `UNVERIFIED — bicarbonate dosing, target pH, QRS threshold and vasopressor choice are all omitted.`

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
ICU admission for significant toxicity. Hyperventilation to maintain alkalosis in the intubated patient. Lipid emulsion and extracorporeal support in refractory cases are toxicologist decisions.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Mental health assessment. **Review of ongoing TCA prescribing** — where an antidepressant is still needed, the lethality of TCAs in overdose is a legitimate consideration in agent choice and quantity dispensed.

## 0.3 Benzodiazepine Overdose

**D:** Poisoning by benzodiazepines, usually benign in isolation and dangerous in combination.

**A/P:** Benzodiazepines potentiate GABA-A, increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening → CNS depression. **They do not directly open the channel**, which is why they have a ceiling effect and a wide therapeutic margin in isolation — unlike barbiturates, which do, and which lack that ceiling.

> [!warning] The danger is the combination, not the benzodiazepine
> Isolated benzodiazepine overdose in a healthy adult usually requires only observation and airway monitoring. **The deaths occur with co-ingested opioids, alcohol or other sedatives**, where the respiratory depression is synergistic. Co-prescription of opioids and benzodiazepines is a recognised and avoidable contributor to overdose death, and combined prescribing is now actively discouraged.

> [!danger] Flumazenil is rarely the answer
> It **precipitates seizures** in benzodiazepine-dependent patients and in mixed overdose — particularly with tricyclics, where the benzodiazepine may be the only thing preventing convulsion. It has a shorter duration of action than most benzodiazepines, so re-sedation follows. It is generally **avoided in the undifferentiated overdose patient**, and its main legitimate role is reversal of iatrogenic procedural sedation in a patient known not to be dependent. `UNVERIFIED — indications, dosing and contraindications from eTG and the Poisons Information Centre.`

> [!danger] Benzodiazepine withdrawal is more dangerous than the overdose
> Unlike opioid withdrawal, which is deeply unpleasant but rarely lethal, **benzodiazepine withdrawal causes seizures and delirium and can kill.** Do not abruptly cease benzodiazepines in a dependent inpatient. This is a common inpatient harm when a regular home benzodiazepine is simply omitted from the admission chart.

**S/Smx:** Drowsiness, slurred speech, ataxia, nystagmus, reduced conscious state, respiratory depression when combined with other agents. Pupils are typically normal, which helps distinguish it from opioid toxicity.

**Ix:** Bedside glucose (*why:* mimics and is instantly reversible; *what:* hypoglycaemia). VBG (*why:* identifies the hypercapnia that indicates significant respiratory depression, usually signalling a co-ingestant; *what:* raised pCO₂). Paracetamol level and ECG (*why:* standard deliberate-ingestion screen, and the ECG identifies a co-ingested sodium channel blocker; *what:* level, QRS and QT). Medication chart and community dispensing history (*why:* establishes dependence, which determines both flumazenil safety and the need for a withdrawal plan; *what:* regular benzodiazepine, dose, duration). Pupil examination (*why:* pinpoint pupils redirect toward opioid co-ingestion; *what:* pupil size). CT brain where conscious state does not fit the exposure (*why:* an intracranial event must not be attributed to sedation; *what:* haemorrhage, infarct).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Airway positioning and monitoring, oxygen, supportive care. Ventilatory support if inadequate. Observation until conscious state recovers. **Continue regular benzodiazepines in a dependent patient** rather than omitting them.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Supportive care. Flumazenil only in the narrow circumstances above.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Mental health assessment. Structured, slow tapering plan for dependence, in partnership with the GP — abrupt cessation is unsafe. Check ScriptCheckSA when prescribing. Cross-refer [[F0-4_Resuscitation_Algorithms_and_Emergency_Procedures]] 0.9.

## 0.4 Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

**D:** Poisoning by carbon monoxide, a colourless odourless gas produced by incomplete combustion.

**R/Causes:** Faulty or unflued gas heaters and water heaters · house fires · petrol generators or engines run in enclosed spaces · vehicle exhaust · charcoal barbecues indoors · **shisha and hookah use** · blocked flues. **Peaks in winter**, and clusters in households.

**A/P:** Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin with an affinity far exceeding oxygen → carboxyhaemoglobin cannot carry oxygen → **and it shifts the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve to the left**, so the remaining oxygen is released to tissues less readily. Both effects reduce delivery. Carbon monoxide additionally binds myoglobin and mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase → direct cellular respiratory impairment and cardiac depression → which is why the tissue injury exceeds what the carboxyhaemoglobin level alone would predict.

> [!danger] Standard pulse oximetry reads normal or high
> Carboxyhaemoglobin absorbs light similarly to oxyhaemoglobin, so **the oximeter cannot distinguish them.** A saturation of 99% in a carbon-monoxide-poisoned patient is meaningless. Diagnosis requires **co-oximetry or a specific carboxyhaemoglobin measurement**, on a venous or arterial sample. Cross-refer [[A3_Respiratory_Failure__Bronchospasm_and_Hypoxia]] 0.6.

> [!warning] The symptoms are entirely non-specific — the history makes the diagnosis
> Headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue, confusion. Commonly misdiagnosed as viral illness, migraine or gastroenteritis. **The discriminating features are contextual:**
> · **Multiple people in the same household affected**, including pets
> · **Symptoms improve when away from home and return on going back**
> · Winter, with a gas heater in use
> · Symptoms in a group in an enclosed space
> **"Cherry-red" skin is rare, late, and usually a post-mortem finding** — do not rely on it or wait for it.

> [!warning] Pregnancy and children
> **Fetal haemoglobin binds carbon monoxide with higher affinity and clears it more slowly**, so the fetus is at greater risk than the maternal level suggests. The threshold for treatment and for specialist involvement is lower in pregnancy. Children have higher minute ventilation relative to size and accumulate faster.

> [!info] Delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae
> Cognitive impairment, personality change, parkinsonism and memory deficits can develop days to weeks after apparent recovery. **Warn the patient and arrange follow-up** — the absence of symptoms at discharge does not exclude them.

**S/Smx:** Headache (most common), nausea, vomiting, dizziness, confusion, weakness, chest pain, dyspnoea, syncope, seizures, coma. Cardiac ischaemia in those with coronary disease.

**Ix:** Carboxyhaemoglobin level by co-oximetry, venous or arterial (*why:* the diagnostic test, and it is the one that standard oximetry and a routine gas will not give you unless specifically requested; *what:* elevated COHb, interpreted against smoking status and time since removal from exposure, since it falls once on oxygen). VBG or ABG with lactate (*why:* metabolic acidosis and lactate reflect tissue hypoxia severity, and a markedly raised lactate should also prompt thought of concurrent cyanide poisoning in fire victims; *what:* acidosis, lactate). ECG and troponin (*why:* myocardial ischaemia occurs and predicts worse outcome, including in young patients; *what:* ischaemic change, troponin rise). Bedside glucose (*why:* reversible mimic; *what:* glucose). Pregnancy test (*why:* changes the treatment threshold; *what:* positive). CT brain where conscious state is reduced or focal signs present (*why:* excludes alternative pathology and severe cases show basal ganglia changes; *what:* globus pallidus changes, alternative pathology). Cyanide consideration in enclosed-space fire victims (*why:* combustion of synthetic materials releases cyanide and it co-occurs with CO, presenting with profound acidosis out of proportion; *what:* clinical suspicion, high lactate).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
**Remove from the source. High-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask immediately** — this substantially shortens the carboxyhaemoglobin half-life compared with room air, and is the single most important intervention. Continue oxygen regardless of the pulse oximeter reading. Monitoring, IV access, ECG. Call 13 11 26.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
**Hyperbaric oxygen** is considered for severe poisoning — loss of consciousness, neurological signs, cardiac ischaemia, pregnancy, or a very high level — though the evidence for its benefit over normobaric oxygen remains debated and access in South Australia requires discussion with the hyperbaric service. `UNVERIFIED — indications, carboxyhaemoglobin thresholds, oxygen half-life figures, and the location and referral pathway for hyperbaric facilities require verification with the Poisons Information Centre.`

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Source identification and rectification before the patient returns home** — this is the part that prevents the next case, and it may require the gas supplier or an appliance inspection. Warn about delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae and arrange follow-up. Consider whether other household members need assessment.

## 0.5 Adverse Drug Reaction

**D:** A harmful or unintended response to a medicine at doses used for prophylaxis, diagnosis or therapy.

> [!tip] Classification — Type A versus Type B is the useful first split
> **Type A ("Augmented")** — an exaggeration of the drug's known pharmacology. **Dose-related, predictable, common**, and managed by dose reduction. Bleeding on warfarin, bradycardia on a beta-blocker, hypoglycaemia on insulin, constipation on opioids.
> **Type B ("Bizarre")** — idiosyncratic, **not dose-related, unpredictable**, often immunological. Uncommon but disproportionately severe. Anaphylaxis, SJS/TEN, drug-induced liver injury, agranulocytosis. Managed by **stopping the drug and never rechallenging.**
> Extended categories: **C** chronic (osteoporosis on long-term steroids), **D** delayed (malignancy, teratogenesis), **E** end-of-use withdrawal (benzodiazepine withdrawal, steroid crisis, beta-blocker rebound), **F** failure of therapy.

> [!danger] Distinguish true allergy from intolerance — the penicillin problem
> A large proportion of patients labelled "penicillin allergic" are not. Most report gastrointestinal upset, a non-specific childhood rash, or an event they cannot describe. **The label carries real harm** — it drives use of broader-spectrum, less effective and more toxic alternatives, with worse outcomes for surgical prophylaxis and serious infection, and it increases resistant organism acquisition.
> **Take an allergy history properly:** what exactly happened, how soon after the dose, what treatment was needed, and have they tolerated any related antibiotic since. Features suggesting true IgE-mediated allergy are onset within an hour, urticaria, angioedema, wheeze, hypotension. Features suggesting it was not are isolated nausea, a delayed non-urticarial rash in a child who was also viraemic, or an event decades ago with no detail.
> **Correcting an inaccurate allergy label is a genuinely valuable clinical act.** Refer to immunology for formal assessment and de-labelling where it matters. `UNVERIFIED — confirm current Australian de-labelling pathways and referral criteria.`

> [!warning] Reporting
> Adverse events are reported to the **TGA**, which maintains the Database of Adverse Event Notifications. Newly registered medicines carry a **Black Triangle** indicating they are subject to additional monitoring, and reporting for these is particularly encouraged. `UNVERIFIED — confirm current TGA reporting mechanisms and what must be reported.`

**R:**
*Unmodifiable:* Age at both extremes, genetic polymorphisms in drug metabolism (CYP2D6, HLA types), female sex for some reactions, renal and hepatic impairment.
*Modifiable:* **Polypharmacy** — risk rises steeply with the number of medicines · prescribing errors · failure to adjust for renal function · drug interactions · inadequate monitoring · unclear medication reconciliation at transitions of care.

**A/P:** Type A reactions follow directly from the drug's mechanism acting more strongly than intended, usually because of accumulation (renal or hepatic impairment), an interaction (enzyme inhibition), or a dosing error. Type B reactions typically involve immune recognition — IgE-mediated, T-cell mediated, or through drug binding to HLA molecules — or an idiosyncratic metabolic susceptibility.

**S/Smx:** Any organ system. The clue is temporal — a new symptom following a new drug, or following a dose increase, or following the addition of an interacting agent.

**Ix:** **Full medication reconciliation including over-the-counter, complementary and recently ceased medicines** (*why:* the answer is usually in the list, and complementary medicines interact more than patients expect — St John's wort being a notable enzyme inducer; *what:* temporal relationship between drug and symptom). UEC and eGFR (*why:* renal impairment is the commonest reason a Type A reaction develops in a patient who tolerated the drug previously; *what:* declining eGFR requiring dose adjustment). LFT (*why:* drug-induced liver injury is a leading cause of acute liver failure and is frequently drug-related before it is anything else; *what:* hepatocellular or cholestatic pattern). FBC (*why:* agranulocytosis, aplasia and eosinophilia; *what:* neutropenia, eosinophilia). Drug levels for narrow therapeutic index agents — digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, gentamicin, vancomycin (*why:* confirms accumulation as the mechanism; *what:* supratherapeutic level). Interaction check against a reference (*why:* enzyme inhibition and induction explain a large proportion of Type A reactions and are systematically under-checked; *what:* interacting pair). Rechallenge is **not** an investigation for Type B reactions (*why:* it risks a more severe reaction; *what:* contraindicated).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Stop or reduce the suspected agent. Treat the reaction. For severe Type B reactions — anaphylaxis, SCARs, agranulocytosis — cease immediately and permanently.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Substitute with an agent of a different class where continued treatment is needed. Immunology referral for assessment where the label matters clinically.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Document the reaction accurately in the record and the discharge summary — what the drug was, what happened, and how soon.** A documented "allergy: penicillin" with no detail is nearly useless and propagates for decades. Medication alert, patient education, and deprescribing review. Cross-refer [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Polypharmacy and Deprescribing.

## 0.6 Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions

**D:** The subset of Type B reactions with skin involvement and systemic severity — Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS, and acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis.

**A/P:** Drug or its metabolite binds HLA molecules and is presented to T cells → cytotoxic T-cell mediated keratinocyte apoptosis (SJS/TEN) or a delayed multi-organ hypersensitivity with eosinophil recruitment and viral reactivation (DRESS). **The HLA association explains why some reactions cluster in specific ancestral populations** and why pre-emptive genotyping is used for certain drugs.

> [!tip] The three, and how to tell them apart
> | | Latency | Skin | Systemic | Key marker |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **SJS / TEN** | ~4 days–4 weeks | Painful skin, dusky macules, blisters, **positive Nikolsky sign**, epidermal detachment | Fever, mucositis | **Mucosal involvement at two or more sites** — eyes, mouth, genitals |
> | **DRESS** | **2–8 weeks — long latency** | Widespread morbilliform rash, **facial oedema** | Fever, lymphadenopathy, hepatitis, nephritis, myocarditis | **Eosinophilia**, deranged LFTs |
> | **AGEP** | Days, often < 48 h | Numerous small sterile **pustules** on erythema | Fever | Neutrophilia, usually self-limiting |
> SJS and TEN are the same disease separated by the percentage of body surface with epidermal detachment. `UNVERIFIED — the body surface area thresholds separating SJS, SJS/TEN overlap and TEN require verification.`

> [!danger] Common culprits worth knowing
> **Allopurinol · anticonvulsants — carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, phenobarbitone · sulfonamides including trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole · NSAIDs · beta-lactams · nevirapine.** The long latency of DRESS is the reason it is missed — the drug started six weeks ago is not the one people suspect.
> **HLA-B\*5701 and abacavir**, **HLA-B\*5801 and allopurinol**, and **HLA-B\*1502 and carbamazepine** in some Asian populations are established associations, with pre-emptive testing recommended in specific circumstances. `UNVERIFIED — confirm which HLA tests are recommended and funded in Australian practice and for which populations.`

> [!warning] Mucosal involvement changes everything
> A morbilliform drug rash is common and usually benign. **Painful skin, blistering, mucosal ulceration, facial oedema, fever or systemic upset moves it into the severe category** and requires immediate drug cessation and specialist involvement. These are the features to ask about and look for in any drug rash.

**S/Smx:** As per the table. Prodromal fever and malaise often precede the rash. TEN patients are systemically unwell with extensive skin loss and behave physiologically like burns patients.

**Ix:** FBC with differential (*why:* eosinophilia supports DRESS, neutrophilia supports AGEP, and both are simple and immediately available; *what:* eosinophil count, neutrophils). LFT and UEC (*why:* DRESS causes hepatitis and nephritis and organ involvement determines severity and steroid need; *what:* transaminitis, rising creatinine). Skin biopsy (*why:* distinguishes the entities histologically where clinical features overlap; *what:* full-thickness epidermal necrosis in TEN, spongiosis and eosinophils in DRESS). Blood cultures and infection screen (*why:* sepsis is the principal cause of death in TEN, and infection is also the main differential for a febrile rash; *what:* organism). Full drug timeline including drugs started in the preceding two months (*why:* DRESS latency is long and the culprit is routinely overlooked because it was started weeks earlier; *what:* temporal correlation). Viral serology and PCR for HHV-6, EBV and CMV in DRESS (*why:* reactivation is characteristic and supports the diagnosis; *what:* reactivation). Ophthalmology assessment in SJS/TEN (*why:* ocular involvement causes permanent scarring and vision loss if not treated early; *what:* conjunctival involvement).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
**Cease the culprit drug and all non-essential medications immediately.** Fluid resuscitation, analgesia, temperature control, and meticulous skin and wound care. Involve dermatology urgently, and **manage extensive TEN in a burns unit** — the physiology is that of a major burn.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Supportive care is the mainstay in TEN. Systemic corticosteroids are used in DRESS, often with a prolonged taper because relapse on rapid withdrawal is common. Ophthalmology, urology and gynaecology input for mucosal involvement.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Absolute lifelong avoidance of the culprit drug and its class**, documented prominently with a medical alert. Family counselling where an HLA association exists, since relatives may share the allele. Long-term sequelae — ocular scarring, strictures, chronic skin changes — need follow-up. Cross-refer [[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]].

## 0.7 Immunotherapy Toxicity

**D:** Immune-related adverse events caused by immune checkpoint inhibitors, in which removal of immune self-tolerance produces autoimmune inflammation in any organ.

**R/Causes:** Anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab, nivolumab), anti-PD-L1 (atezolizumab, durvalumab) and anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) agents, used across an expanding range of malignancies. **Combination therapy carries substantially higher toxicity than single agents.** Pre-existing autoimmune disease increases risk.

**A/P:** Checkpoint pathways normally restrain T-cell activation to preserve self-tolerance. Blocking them unleashes anti-tumour immunity — and, by the same mechanism, **autoimmunity against normal tissue.** The toxicity is therefore not a drug side effect in the conventional sense but an intended mechanism acting in the wrong place, which is why the treatment is immunosuppression rather than antidote or supportive care alone.

> [!danger] The single most important clinical point
> **A patient on checkpoint inhibitor therapy presenting with almost any new symptom has an immune-related adverse event until proven otherwise — and the treatment is corticosteroids, not antibiotics.**
> Diarrhoea is colitis, not gastroenteritis. Breathlessness is pneumonitis, not infection. Fatigue is hypophysitis or adrenal insufficiency, not deconditioning. Infection must still be excluded, but the reflex to treat as infection and discharge is the recognised error, and it causes deaths.
> **Ask every oncology patient what they are on**, flag it, and **contact their oncology team** — most Australian cancer centres run a 24-hour line for exactly this.

> [!warning] Timing does not exclude it
> Immune-related adverse events occur at any point — within weeks of the first dose, months in, or **after treatment has stopped**, sometimes by many months. "They finished immunotherapy last year" does not exclude the diagnosis.

> [!tip] By organ, with the ones that must not be missed
> **Colitis** — diarrhoea, abdominal pain; risk of perforation. Common with anti-CTLA-4.
> **Hepatitis** — usually asymptomatic transaminitis found on bloods.
> **Pneumonitis** — dry cough, dyspnoea, ground-glass change on CT; potentially fatal.
> **Thyroiditis** — thyrotoxic phase followed by hypothyroidism, often permanent.
> **Hypophysitis and adrenal insufficiency** — fatigue, headache, hypotension, hyponatraemia. **May present as adrenal crisis** — cross-refer [[F0-3_Shock_Phenotypes_and_Sepsis_Syndromes]] 0.6.
> **Myocarditis** — rare but with **high mortality**; chest pain, dyspnoea, arrhythmia, raised troponin. Any cardiac symptom in a patient on immunotherapy warrants troponin and ECG.
> **Dermatological** — rash and pruritus commonly, but SCARs occur — see 0.6.
> **Neurological** — myasthenia-like syndrome, Guillain–Barré-like presentations, encephalitis.

**S/Smx:** Organ-specific as above. Frequently non-specific early — fatigue, low-grade fever, malaise — which is precisely why the exposure history is the key to the diagnosis.

**Ix:** Full blood count, UEC, LFT, TFTs, glucose and cortisol (*why:* this panel screens most of the common endocrine, hepatic and renal irAEs simultaneously, and several are asymptomatic when first detectable; *what:* transaminitis, deranged TFTs, hyponatraemia, low cortisol, new hyperglycaemia from immune-mediated diabetes). Troponin and ECG for any cardiac symptom (*why:* myocarditis has high mortality and is treatable if recognised early; *what:* troponin rise, conduction abnormality). CT chest where respiratory symptoms (*why:* pneumonitis is not distinguishable clinically from infection and CXR is insensitive; *what:* ground-glass opacity, organising pneumonia pattern). Stool culture, *C. difficile* toxin and PCR panel (*why:* infection must be excluded before immunosuppressing a patient with diarrhoea, and the two can coexist; *what:* pathogen). Flexible sigmoidoscopy with biopsy in significant colitis (*why:* confirms immune colitis and grades severity, guiding steroid and escalation decisions; *what:* colitis, ulceration). ACTH and paired cortisol (*why:* distinguishes primary adrenal insufficiency from hypophysitis, which have different management; *what:* ACTH level). Baseline and serial monitoring bloods per the oncology protocol (*why:* many irAEs are detected on routine bloods before symptoms; *what:* trend).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess severity by grade. **Withhold the immunotherapy. Exclude infection, then start corticosteroids** for anything beyond mild. **Contact the treating oncology service.** For suspected myocarditis or adrenal crisis, treat as an emergency. `UNVERIFIED — corticosteroid doses by toxicity grade, the grading criteria themselves, and escalation thresholds require verification against the treating centre's protocol and current oncology guidance.`

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Corticosteroids with a slow taper — **rapid tapering causes relapse** and courses are often measured in weeks. Escalation to infliximab, mycophenolate or other agents in steroid-refractory cases, directed by oncology. Decision on rechallenge is a specialist one and depends on the organ involved and severity.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Endocrinopathies are frequently permanent** and require lifelong replacement — thyroid hormone, hydrocortisone, insulin. Patients on long-term steroids need PJP prophylaxis, bone protection and glycaemic monitoring. Ensure the patient carries documentation of their immunotherapy exposure, because the next clinician to see them may not think of it.
