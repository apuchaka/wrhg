## 0.1 Foreign Bodies — General Principles

**D:** Retained material at any body site, presenting acutely, incidentally, or through its complications.

> [!danger] The button battery rule — the single most important thing in this file
> **A button or lithium coin cell lodged in the oesophagus, nose or ear is a surgical emergency and removal is measured in hours, not days.**
> Mechanism: current flows through moist mucosa, **hydrolysing tissue water and generating hydroxide at the negative pole** → liquefactive necrosis, exactly as in an alkali burn. **Oesophageal injury begins within about two hours**, and progresses to perforation, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, mediastinitis and fatal aorto-oesophageal fistula. Injury continues after removal.
> **This is not dependent on the battery being "dead"** — a discharged battery still generates current in tissue.
> **Any child with a possible button battery ingestion goes straight to imaging and straight to endoscopy.** Do not observe, do not wait for symptoms, do not wait until morning. `UNVERIFIED — the removal time window, the role of honey or sucralfate as a pre-hospital temporising measure, and post-removal surveillance imaging all require verification against current RCH and Poisons Information Centre guidance.`
> Australian children have died from this. It is a recurring public health campaign topic and a very plausible exam item.

> [!tip] The one-attempt principle
> **The first removal attempt has by far the highest success rate.** Each subsequent attempt causes oedema, bleeding and deeper impaction, converting a straightforward outpatient procedure into one requiring general anaesthesia. Before attempting removal, ask honestly: do I have adequate lighting, the right instrument, adequate analgesia or anaesthesia, and a cooperative or properly immobilised patient? **If any answer is no, refer rather than attempt.** A failed attempt is worse than no attempt.

> [!warning] Refer without attempting when
> Button battery or multiple magnets at any site · a child who will not cooperate and cannot be safely immobilised · an object impacted medially in the ear canal beyond the isthmus · suspected penetrating ocular injury · signs of perforation at any site · an object you cannot see clearly · previous failed attempts elsewhere.

> [!danger] Safeguarding
> **A rectal or vaginal foreign body in a prepubertal child raises the possibility of sexual abuse and must be escalated**, not simply removed and discharged. Recurrent foreign body insertion in children also warrants consideration of neglect, developmental concerns or abuse. In adults, consider intellectual disability, psychiatric illness, deliberate self-harm and body packing. Cross-refer [[P3]] Safeguarding & Forensic.

> [!info] Foreign body sensation with nothing found
> Persisting sensation after a foreign body has passed or been removed is common and usually reflects **mucosal abrasion**, which takes days to settle — particularly in the throat after a fish bone and on the cornea after an abrasion. However, sensation is not a reliable guide to absence: **a persistent, well-localised sensation in the throat, or persisting ocular symptoms, warrants specialist visualisation** rather than reassurance alone, because a retained object and an abrasion feel the same to the patient.

**Ix:** Site-specific, as below. Two general points: plain radiography detects radio-opaque objects — **metal, glass, most batteries and coins** — but **misses wood, plastic, most fish bones, thorns and aluminium**; and **the characteristic "double ring" or halo on a radiograph distinguishes a button battery from a coin**, which is the observation that changes the entire management pathway and must be actively looked for.

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess for airway compromise, perforation and battery or magnet involvement first. These three determine urgency before anything about the site does.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Site-specific removal, as below.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Prevention advice, safeguarding consideration, and follow-up for complications.

## 0.2 Aural Foreign Body

**D:** Object retained in the external auditory canal.

**R/Causes:** Children — beads, small toys, food, paper, seeds. Adults — cotton bud tips, hearing aid components, earplugs, insects.

> [!warning] Choose the technique for the object
> **Irrigation** works well for small, smooth, non-organic objects — **but is contraindicated** where there is a known or suspected tympanic membrane perforation, where the object is **organic or vegetable matter** (seeds, peas, beans — which **swell on contact with water** and become far harder to remove), and for **button batteries**, where water accelerates the current and the tissue injury.
> **Forceps** for objects with a graspable edge — paper, cotton wool, insects.
> **A hook or curette passed beyond the object** for smooth round objects that forceps will simply push deeper.
> **Suction** for light objects.

> [!tip] Insects
> An insect in the ear canal is intensely distressing because of the noise and movement against the tympanic membrane. **Kill it first** — instilling lidocaine, mineral oil or olive oil immobilises it — then remove. Attempting removal of a moving insect is unpleasant for everyone and usually unsuccessful. `UNVERIFIED — agent choice and whether to instil anything if perforation is suspected requires verification.`

> [!danger] Button battery in the ear
> Causes canal and tympanic membrane necrosis. **Urgent ENT removal, and do not irrigate.**

**S/Smx:** Often asymptomatic and found incidentally. Otherwise: pain, hearing loss, sensation of fullness, discharge, bleeding, or a witnessed insertion. Foul discharge suggests a long-retained object.

**Ix:** Otoscopy (*why:* identifies the object, its nature and position relative to the isthmus, and assesses the tympanic membrane before any irrigation is considered; *what:* object type, position, TM status, canal trauma). Assessment of hearing (*why:* baseline, and significant loss suggests medial impaction or TM involvement; *what:* conductive loss). Post-removal otoscopy (*why:* canal laceration and TM perforation occur both from the object and from the removal attempt, and must be documented; *what:* laceration, perforation, retained fragments — **always re-examine and check the other ear and the nose, since children frequently insert objects in more than one orifice**).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess the object. One well-prepared attempt with the appropriate technique. Adequate immobilisation in a child — a struggling child is the commonest reason removal fails and the canal is injured.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
ENT referral for failed removal, medial impaction, uncooperative child (often requiring removal under general anaesthesia), or complications.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Follow-up for perforation. Parental advice about small objects. **Discourage cotton bud use**, which is a common source of both foreign bodies and canal trauma.

## 0.3 Nasal Foreign Body

**D:** Object retained in the nasal cavity, most commonly in preschool children.

> [!danger] Unilateral foul purulent nasal discharge in a child is a nasal foreign body until proven otherwise
> This is the classic presentation, and the object has usually been there for days to weeks with the insertion unwitnessed or forgotten. **Recurrent unilateral "sinusitis" in a small child should prompt nasal examination, not another antibiotic.**

> [!danger] Button batteries and paired magnets in the nose
> **A button battery in the nose causes septal perforation within hours** through the same alkali mechanism. **Two magnets in opposite nostrils** attract across the septum, causing pressure necrosis and perforation. Both are emergencies requiring immediate removal, and neither will be identified unless you look and unless you think of it.

> [!tip] The "parent's kiss" (mother's kiss) technique
> The parent occludes the unaffected nostril with a finger, makes a seal over the child's mouth, and gives a short sharp puff. The positive pressure expels the object. It is simple, atraumatic, well tolerated, avoids instrumentation, and has a good success rate for anteriorly placed objects — **and it is under-used.** It can be repeated. Explain it clearly to the parent first.

> [!warning] Do not push the object posteriorly
> An object dislodged backwards may be aspirated. Keep the child upright, avoid blind instrumentation, and use techniques that work forwards — positive pressure, a hook passed beyond and drawn forward, or forceps on a graspable edge.

**R/Causes:** Beads, food, paper, foam, small toy parts, button batteries, magnets. In adults, occasionally retained nasal packing or a displaced dental or surgical item.

**S/Smx:** Unilateral discharge, often foul and purulent or blood-stained · unilateral obstruction · epistaxis · foul breath · pain. Frequently the insertion is unreported.

**Ix:** Anterior rhinoscopy with adequate light, after topical decongestant and anaesthetic where tolerated (*why:* decongestion shrinks the mucosa and improves both visualisation and the chance of successful removal at the first attempt; *what:* object type, position, mucosal state). Examination of **both nostrils, both ears and the oropharynx** (*why:* multiple simultaneous foreign bodies in young children are common and easily missed once the first is found; *what:* additional objects). Plain radiography where a battery or magnet is possible and the object is not directly visible (*why:* identifies the radio-opaque battery with its double ring, changing the pathway to emergency removal; *what:* double-ring appearance, paired magnets). Post-removal examination (*why:* fragments, mucosal injury and septal damage; *what:* residual object, perforation).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
Keep the child calm and upright. Topical decongestant and anaesthetic. **Try the parent's kiss first for anterior objects.** Then instrumentation if appropriate. Immediate ENT for battery or magnets.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
ENT removal under general anaesthesia where attempts fail or the child cannot cooperate.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Follow-up for septal perforation after battery or magnet injury. Prevention advice — **button battery storage and disposal in particular**, since loose batteries in the home are the source.

## 0.4 Corneal and Ocular Foreign Body

**D:** Foreign material on the ocular surface, embedded in the cornea, beneath the eyelid, or penetrating the globe.

> [!danger] The mechanism determines everything — suspect a penetrating injury
> **Grinding, hammering metal on metal, drilling, or use of a high-speed tool without eye protection** can drive a fragment through the cornea or sclera into the globe. **The entry wound may be tiny and the eye may look almost normal**, and the patient may report minimal pain.
> **If a high-velocity mechanism is described: do not press on the eye, do not attempt removal, apply a rigid shield (not a pad), keep the patient nil by mouth, give antiemetics to prevent Valsalva, and refer to ophthalmology immediately.**
> **Order CT orbits — not MRI**, because a ferromagnetic intraocular foreign body will move in the scanner and cause catastrophic injury.
> Look for a **positive Seidel test** — fluorescein diluting in a stream of aqueous from the wound — a distorted or peaked pupil, shallow anterior chamber, or hyphaema.

> [!tip] Vertical linear corneal abrasions mean a subtarsal foreign body
> Multiple fine vertical scratches on the cornea are made by an object under the upper lid abrading the cornea with every blink. **Evert the upper eyelid** — the object is sitting on the tarsal plate and will not be found otherwise. Failing to evert the lid is a classic and easily avoided miss.

> [!danger] Never give a patient topical anaesthetic to take home
> Topical anaesthetic is used to permit examination. **Repeated use impairs corneal epithelial healing, abolishes the protective blink reflex, and causes corneal melting and ulceration.** Patients ask for it because it works instantly, and it must be declined with an explanation.

**A/P:** Metallic fragments embedded in the cornea oxidise within hours to days, producing a **rust ring** which itself impairs healing and requires removal. Retained organic material carries a high infection risk. Any full-thickness breach risks endophthalmitis, which is sight-destroying.

**S/Smx:** Pain, foreign body sensation, watering, photophobia, blurred vision, blepharospasm, injection. Reduced acuity, hyphaema or an irregular pupil suggest a more serious injury.

**Ix:** **Visual acuity in both eyes before anything else** (*why:* baseline, medicolegally essential, and a reduction redirects the assessment toward a serious injury; *what:* acuity — noting the single exception is chemical injury, where irrigation precedes acuity, cross-refer [[A7]] 0.4). Slit lamp examination with fluorescein (*why:* localises the object, demonstrates abrasion, reveals a rust ring, and shows a Seidel test if the globe is breached; *what:* object, epithelial defect, Seidel positivity). **Upper lid eversion** (*why:* subtarsal foreign bodies are otherwise missed and are a common cause of persistent symptoms; *what:* object on tarsal plate). CT orbits where a penetrating injury is possible (*why:* detects intraocular and intraorbital foreign bodies; *what:* radio-opaque fragment — **MRI is contraindicated with possible ferromagnetic material**). Intraocular pressure, **avoided if globe perforation is suspected** (*why:* pressure on a perforated globe extrudes contents; *what:* deferred until perforation excluded). Tetanus status (*why:* penetrating ocular injury is tetanus-prone; *what:* immunisation history).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Acuity, then topical anaesthetic, then examination with fluorescein and lid eversion. Removal of a superficial object with a moistened cotton bud or, for embedded objects, a needle or burr **by someone trained to do it under slit lamp magnification.** For suspected penetrating injury: shield, nil by mouth, antiemetic, urgent ophthalmology.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Rust ring removal, topical antibiotic prophylaxis, and ophthalmology review. Surgical removal of intraocular foreign bodies.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Review within 24–48 hours** for any corneal foreign body removal. Eye protection advice and, where the injury was occupational, workplace review and SafeWork SA notification. Cross-refer [[E1]] and [[P1]].

## 0.5 Oropharyngeal Foreign Body

**D:** Object lodged in the mouth, oropharynx or hypopharynx — most commonly a fish bone.

**A/P:** Sharp objects lodge at sites of mucosal irregularity — the **tonsils, base of tongue, valleculae and piriform fossae.** The patient can usually localise the sensation, and localisation to one side above the level of the thyroid cartilage suggests an oropharyngeal site accessible to direct examination; a lower, midline, or poorly localised sensation suggests an oesophageal site requiring endoscopic assessment.

> [!warning] Symptoms often persist after the object has gone
> A fish bone that has scratched the mucosa on the way down leaves a sensation identical to a retained bone, and it can take **several days** to settle. This does not mean symptoms should be dismissed — **persistent, well-localised sensation, pain on swallowing, drooling, fever or neck swelling warrant ENT visualisation**, because retropharyngeal abscess, perforation and mediastinitis are the complications of a missed retained object.

> [!danger] Red flags
> Drooling and inability to swallow saliva · stridor or respiratory distress · neck swelling, crepitus or tenderness · fever · pain on neck movement · haematemesis. Any of these suggests perforation or deep space infection and requires urgent ENT and imaging.

**S/Smx:** Sharp pain on swallowing, localised foreign body sensation, odynophagia, drooling, and occasionally a visible object.

**Ix:** Direct inspection of the oropharynx with good light and a tongue depressor, including the tonsils and base of tongue (*why:* a substantial proportion of fish bones are visible and removable at the bedside with forceps; *what:* visible bone or object). **Flexible nasendoscopy** (*why:* the definitive examination of the base of tongue, valleculae and piriform fossae, which cannot be seen on direct inspection, and it is an outpatient procedure; *what:* object at a site not otherwise visible). Lateral soft tissue neck radiograph (*why:* may show a radio-opaque object, prevertebral soft tissue swelling from perforation, or surgical emphysema — though **fish bone radio-opacity varies enormously by species**, so a negative film is close to uninformative; *what:* object, prevertebral swelling, air). CT neck where perforation, abscess or a persistent unlocated object is suspected (*why:* far more sensitive and identifies the deep space complications; *what:* object, collection, extraluminal gas). Cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.10 for the deep neck space infection differential.

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Inspect and remove if visible and accessible. Analgesia. Nil by mouth if perforation is suspected. ENT referral where not visible and symptoms persist, or where red flags are present.

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Endoscopic removal. Management of perforation or abscess.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Advice that abrasion sensation may persist for days, with explicit return criteria for fever, worsening pain or neck swelling.

## 0.6 Swallowed Foreign Body

**D:** Ingested object within the gastrointestinal tract, ranging from benign and self-resolving to a time-critical emergency.

**A/P:** Objects lodge at points of physiological narrowing: the **cricopharyngeus** (commonest, particularly in children), the **level of the aortic arch and left main bronchus**, and the **lower oesophageal sphincter**. Once past the pylorus, most blunt objects traverse the remaining tract without difficulty. The duodenal C-loop and ileocaecal valve are the subsequent points of hold-up.

> [!danger] The four categories that are emergencies
> **1. Button battery in the oesophagus** — as per 0.1. Emergency endoscopic removal.
> **2. Two or more magnets, or one magnet plus another metallic object** — they attract across bowel loops, trapping the intervening walls, causing **pressure necrosis, perforation and fistula.** This is a surgical emergency and cannot be observed. Small high-powered "neodymium" magnet sets are the usual source and are a recognised paediatric hazard.
> **3. Sharp or pointed objects in the oesophagus or stomach** — needles, open safety pins, bones, razor blades, toothpicks. Perforation risk is significant and endoscopic removal is generally indicated rather than observation.
> **4. Any object causing oesophageal obstruction** — inability to swallow saliva, drooling, complete dysphagia. Requires urgent endoscopy.
> `UNVERIFIED — the specific time windows for removal in each category, the object size and length thresholds beyond which passage is unlikely, and the recommended observation intervals require verification against RCH and current gastroenterology guidance.`

> [!tip] The radiograph finding that decides the pathway
> **A button battery shows a "double ring" or halo sign on the frontal view and a step-off on the lateral view; a coin shows a single uniform disc.** Distinguishing these two on a child's chest radiograph is the observation that separates urgent endoscopy from observation. Actively look for it — the two are otherwise superficially identical.

> [!warning] Food bolus obstruction in an adult
> Almost always indicates an **underlying oesophageal abnormality** — stricture, ring, achalasia, or **eosinophilic oesophagitis**, which is increasingly recognised in younger adults and often presents exactly this way. Relieving the obstruction is not the end of the management; the oesophagus needs assessment afterwards. Cross-refer [[C6]] Dyspepsia.

**S/Smx:** Often asymptomatic. Otherwise: drooling, dysphagia, odynophagia, retrosternal pain, vomiting, refusal to feed in an infant, respiratory symptoms from tracheal compression, and — with perforation — fever, chest or abdominal pain and surgical emphysema.

**Ix:** Plain radiography from neck to pelvis in children (*why:* localises radio-opaque objects and, critically, distinguishes battery from coin; *what:* object type, level, double-ring sign, free gas). Chest and abdominal radiography in adults where indicated (*why:* as above; *what:* object, free gas). **Careful history about magnets and batteries specifically** (*why:* parents may not volunteer them, and the object's identity changes the pathway from observation to emergency; *what:* number and type of objects). CT where perforation is suspected or a radiolucent object needs localisation (*why:* detects extraluminal gas, collections and radiolucent objects; *what:* perforation, object). Endoscopy, which is both diagnostic and therapeutic (*why:* removes the object under direct vision and assesses the mucosal injury and any underlying pathology; *what:* object, mucosal damage, stricture or eosinophilic changes). **Barium studies are generally avoided** (*why:* barium coats the object and the mucosa, impairs subsequent endoscopy, and carries aspiration risk; *what:* not indicated).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess airway and for obstruction. Identify the object. **Battery, magnets, sharp object, or oesophageal obstruction → urgent endoscopy.** Otherwise, if the object is blunt, has passed the pylorus, and the patient is asymptomatic, conservative management with advice is appropriate. Nil by mouth pending a decision.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Endoscopic removal, or surgery for perforation, magnet-related injury or failure to progress.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Oesophageal assessment after adult food bolus obstruction. Post-battery injury surveillance for stricture and fistula, which develop after the acute episode. **Prevention advice about button battery storage and high-powered magnet sets** — both are the subject of Australian product safety regulation.

## 0.7 Rectal Foreign Body

**D:** Object retained in the rectum or distal colon.

> [!warning] The consultation matters as much as the procedure
> Presentation is frequently **delayed by embarrassment**, and the history is often incomplete or altered. **A calm, matter-of-fact, non-judgemental approach is clinically important** — not simply courteous — because it elicits the accurate information about the object, its size, its shape and how long it has been there that determines whether transanal removal is safe or laparotomy is required. Ensure privacy and limit the number of people present.

**A/P:** Objects inserted transanally may migrate proximally beyond the rectosigmoid junction, where the sacral curve and the narrowing make transanal retrieval difficult. Prolonged retention causes mucosal pressure necrosis, and attempts at self-removal are a common cause of the perforation that brings the patient in.

> [!danger] Exclude perforation before attempting removal
> Peritonism, fever, tachycardia, guarding or free gas on imaging means **laparotomy, not transanal extraction.** Perforation may be caused by the object, by prolonged retention, or by attempts at removal at home.

> [!tip] Low versus high
> **Low (palpable on digital rectal examination, below the rectosigmoid):** often removable transanally in the emergency department or theatre with sedation and adequate sphincter relaxation.
> **High (not palpable, above the rectosigmoid):** generally requires theatre, with attempted transanal retrieval under general anaesthesia and laparotomy if unsuccessful.
> **Sigmoidoscopy after removal is important** to assess for mucosal injury and perforation, which may not be apparent until the object is out.

**S/Smx:** Anorectal pain, bleeding, tenesmus, constipation, incontinence, or systemic features if perforated. Some patients present with a complication rather than the object.

**Ix:** Abdominal and pelvic radiography (*why:* localises the object, defines its size and orientation, and identifies **free gas indicating perforation**, which changes the plan entirely; *what:* object, level, free subdiaphragmatic gas). Abdominal examination for peritonism (*why:* peritonism mandates surgery rather than extraction; *what:* guarding, rebound). Digital rectal examination (*why:* determines whether the object is low and palpable, which is the key determinant of approach; *what:* object position, sphincter tone, blood). CT abdomen and pelvis where the object is radiolucent, high, or perforation is suspected (*why:* localises non-radio-opaque objects and defines perforation and its extent; *what:* object, extraluminal gas, collection). FBC and inflammatory markers (*why:* baseline and evidence of established infection; *what:* leucocytosis). **Post-removal sigmoidoscopy** (*why:* mucosal laceration and perforation are common and may be occult until the object is removed; *what:* mucosal injury).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess for perforation. Analgesia. Imaging. Non-judgemental history. Surgical involvement early for high or large objects.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Transanal removal with sedation or anaesthesia and sphincter relaxation, or operative management. Post-removal sigmoidoscopy.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Sensitive discussion of safer practice where relevant, and assessment for underlying psychiatric illness, intellectual disability or self-harm where the circumstances suggest it. **In a child, this is a safeguarding presentation and requires child protection escalation.** Cross-refer [[P3]].

## 0.8 Vaginal Foreign Body

**D:** Object retained in the vagina, differing substantially in significance between children and adults.

> [!danger] In a prepubertal child, this is a safeguarding presentation
> **Offensive, bloody or persistent vaginal discharge in a prepubertal girl is a foreign body until proven otherwise** — most commonly small pieces of retained toilet paper. **However, vaginal foreign body in a child mandates consideration of sexual abuse**, and the assessment must include a safeguarding discussion with a senior clinician and, where indicated, child protection services.
> **Do not perform an intrusive vaginal examination on a child in the emergency department.** Examination and removal should be undertaken by an appropriately experienced clinician, usually under general anaesthesia, both to avoid traumatising the child and to ensure any forensic considerations are handled correctly.

**R/Causes:** *Children:* toilet paper, small toys, beads. *Adults:* **retained tampon** (the commonest), retained condom, pessary that has eroded or been forgotten, contraceptive device, and objects related to sexual activity.

**A/P:** A retained object causes local inflammation, bacterial overgrowth and mucosal ulceration → characteristically **foul-smelling discharge**, often blood-stained. Prolonged retention with a pessary can cause erosion into the bladder or rectum with fistula formation.

> [!warning] Toxic shock syndrome
> A retained tampon is a recognised though uncommon cause. **Fever, hypotension, a diffuse erythematous macular rash, vomiting, diarrhoea and multi-organ dysfunction** in a menstruating woman should prompt a search for a retained tampon and immediate removal, alongside resuscitation and antibiotics. Later desquamation of the palms and soles is characteristic. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.7.

**S/Smx:** Offensive vaginal discharge, often described as the worst thing about it · intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding · pelvic discomfort · dyspareunia · urinary symptoms from pressure. Frequently no history of insertion is given, either because it is forgotten (tampon, pessary) or not volunteered.

**Ix:** Speculum examination in an adult (*why:* usually both diagnostic and therapeutic, and directly visualises mucosal injury; *what:* object, ulceration, discharge). **Examination under anaesthesia in a child** (*why:* avoids traumatising the child, allows adequate assessment and removal, and preserves forensic integrity where abuse is a consideration; *what:* object, injury). High vaginal and endocervical swabs (*why:* excludes concurrent infection and, in a child, sexually transmitted infection has major safeguarding implications; *what:* organisms, STI screen where indicated). Pelvic ultrasound where a retained object is suspected but not seen (*why:* identifies objects above the level of speculum visualisation and pessary-related complications; *what:* object, collection, fistula). Observations and septic screen where systemically unwell (*why:* toxic shock syndrome; *what:* fever, hypotension, organ dysfunction).

### 0.8.1 Mx – Immediate
Removal in an adult, usually straightforward with a speculum and sponge forceps. **In a child, do not attempt bedside removal — escalate.** Resuscitate and treat empirically if toxic shock is suspected.

### 0.8.2 Mx – Definitive
Removal, treatment of associated infection, and repair of erosion or fistula where present.

### 0.8.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Pessary care education and **a scheduled review interval for anyone with a pessary in situ**, since forgotten pessaries are a recurring source of this presentation. Tampon safety advice. **In a child, ongoing child protection involvement.** Cross-refer [[O5]] Vulvovaginal Symptom and [[P3]].
