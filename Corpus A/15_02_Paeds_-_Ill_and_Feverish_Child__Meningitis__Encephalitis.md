## The ill and feverish child — traffic light assessment

- **Ax:** ABC, then traffic light assessment.

> [!info] Green light (low risk)
> Takes most feeds ok; normal colour (lips, tongue, skin); responds to social cues; content/smiles; alerts or wakens quickly; strong/normal cry; breathing calmly; normal skin and eyes; moist mucous membranes.

> [!warning] Amber light (intermediate risk)
> Taking ≤50% feeds; pale; not responding to social cues; hard to wake; ↓activity; no smile; nasal flaring; ↑RR; sats <95%; crepitations; CRT >3s; ↓urine output; dry mucous membranes; rigors; fever >5 days; temp ≥39°C; limb or joint swelling, non-weight bearing, not using an extremity.

> [!danger] Red light (high risk)
> Pale/mottled/ashen/blue; not responding to social cues; unarousable or doesn't stay awake when aroused; weak, high-pitched or continuous cry, or grunting; RR >50, chest indrawing; ↓skin turgor; non-blanching rash; bulging fontanelle; neck stiffness; status epilepticus; focal seizures; focal neurological signs; temp ≥38°C if <3 months old.

### Management by risk category

> [!info] Verified — the traffic light (green/amber/red) risk stratification system itself is internationally used and specifically referenced by Australian paediatric emergency guidelines (Queensland Health, NSW ACI, and RCH Melbourne's own "Febrile child" clinical practice guideline all use this same general framework), so the traffic light criteria and overall risk-stratified structure below don't need wholesale replacement. The specific investigation thresholds and antibiotic choices/doses should still be checked against current eTG or the relevant state paediatric emergency guideline directly before clinical use, given these are exactly the kind of figures that get refined between guideline versions — but the underlying triage logic and red-flag criteria are soundly based and consistent with Australian practice.

- **Children <3 months old:** Ix — urine, FBC, CRP, blood culture ± CXR if respiratory signs, stool culture if diarrhoea, nasopharyngeal aspirate and throat swab. LP: perform on all <1 month old, 1–3 months + unwell-looking, or 1–3 months + WCC <5 or >15.
- **Green features:** possibly manage at home. Always test urine in young children. Do not prescribe PO antibiotics if no clear source. Advice to parents: offer regular drinks or breastfeeding; look for signs of dehydration; look for a non-blanching rash; check on the child at night; do not tepid-sponge or undress to control fever, only use antipyretics if the child is distressed; keep off school while febrile; seek further help if the child becomes more unwell, dehydrated, has a fit, or fever >5 days — go to the Emergency Department if a non-blanching rash appears.
- **Amber features:** Ix — urine, FBC, blood culture ± LP if <1 year old ± CXR (especially if fever >39°C or WCC >20).
- **Red features:** Ix — urine, FBC & U&E, blood culture ± LP if <1 year old ± CXR ± ABG ± nasopharyngeal & throat swabs. Fluid bolus: 20mL/kg IV 0.9% NaCl. Antibiotics: max dose 3rd generation cephalosporin (e.g. ceftriaxone) if signs of shock, unrousable, or signs of meningitis — if <3 months old, cover for Listeria (e.g. ampicillin).

### Other notes

- If herpes simplex encephalitis is suspected, give IV aciclovir.
- If >3 months old with confirmed bacterial meningitis, give IV corticosteroids.
- Children with fever >5 days should be assessed by a paediatrician for Kawasaki disease.

## Meningitis

- **S/Smx:** poor feeding, lethargy, irritability, nausea/vomiting, headache, myalgia, arthralgia.

> [!danger] Combination of fever, headache, neck stiffness, and altered level of consciousness is a red flag.

> [!warning] Meningeal signs come on relatively late — not sensitive or specific. Neck stiffness; photophobia; Kernig's sign (resistance to extending the knee when the hip is flexed); Brudzinski's sign (hips flex on neck flexion); opisthotonus (back arching).

- **Ix:** FBC, CRP, U&Es, clotting screen, culture, meningococcal PCR, glucose, ABG. LP if not contraindicated ± urine, nose/throat swab, stool virology, CXR.

> [!danger] Contraindications for LP (any signs of ↑ICP): focal neuro signs; papilloedema; significant bulging of the fontanelle; DIC; signs of cerebral herniation; meningococcal septicaemia (do blood cultures & PCR instead).

### Meningitis: the organisms by age

| Age | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **Neonatal to 3 months** | GBS (usually vertical transmission, more common in low birth weight babies and following prolonged rupture of membranes); *E. coli* and other Gram-negatives; *Listeria monocytogenes* |
| **1 month to 6 years** | *Neisseria meningitidis* (meningococcus); *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (pneumococcus); *Haemophilus influenzae* |
| **>6 years** | *Neisseria meningitidis*; *Streptococcus pneumoniae* |

### Management

> [!info] Verified — ceftriaxone/cefotaxime as the base antibiotic choice for bacterial meningitis, and the age-based rationale for adding Listeria cover (ampicillin/benzylpenicillin) in young infants, is consistent with Australian practice — already independently verified for the adult presentation in [[04_Neurology]] (empirical ceftriaxone as the base regimen, with age-based Listeria cover added per the same age thresholds used here) — the same underlying logic applies in the paediatric context. The specific mg/kg doses below should still be checked against current eTG/local paediatric protocol before clinical use, given paediatric dosing is weight-based and genuinely institution/formulary-specific, but the drug choice itself doesn't need correcting.

1. **Antibiotics:** <3 months — IV amoxicillin + cefotaxime. >3 months — IV cefotaxime/ceftriaxone.
2. **Steroids** — not for <3 months. Consider dexamethasone if the LP shows frankly purulent CSF, CSF WBC >1000 ± protein >1g/L, or bacteria on Gram stain.
3. **Fluids:** treatment of shock.
4. **Cerebral monitoring:** mechanical ventilation if respiratory impairment.
5. **Public health notification** and antibiotic prophylaxis of contacts — ciprofloxacin.
- If ↑ICP: treat with hypertonic saline or mannitol; discuss with paediatric ICU.

### Giving IM benzylpenicillin before hospital admission

> [!info] Pre-hospital IM benzylpenicillin dosing
> - <1 year old: 300mg IM.
> - 1–9 years old: 600mg.
> - ≥10 years old: 1200mg.
> - When in doubt, give it. If penicillin-allergic, cefotaxime may be used (50mg/kg IM stat, if >12 years old, 1000mg).

### Complications

- **Acute:** seizures, ↑ICP, abscesses, infected subdural effusion.
- **Chronic:** hydrocephalus, ataxia, paralysis, deafness (steroids reduce this risk), ↓IQ, epilepsy.

### TB meningitis

- Consider in any child with ↓glucose, ↑protein, and ↑WBC in CSF.
- Prodromal and meningitic phases. Staging based on level of consciousness and neuro signs: (1) conscious with no neuro signs; (2) disturbed consciousness ± focal neurology; (3) comatose ± significant neuro deficit.
- **Complications:** communicating hydrocephalus, stroke, ↑ICP.
- **CSF:** first few samples might be normal, or show visible fibrin webs and widely varying cell counts.

## Encephalitis

- **S/Smx:** fever, altered mental status, seizures, focal neuro findings (e.g. weakness, visual disturbance, aphasia, cerebellar signs, behaviour changes).

> [!tip] Encephalitis vs meningitis: signs of meningeal irritation (neck stiffness, photophobia) are usually NOT present in encephalitis. Seizures and focal neurological signs are more common in encephalitis.

- **Causes:** infective vs non-infective (e.g. acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, ADEM — an acute demyelinating process following a non-CNS infection).

> [!info] DDx encephalopathies: metabolic (↓glycaemia, DKA, ↑NH4), toxic, hypertensive, autoimmune, sepsis; other CNS infection (meningitis, abscess); psychosis, lead or other poisoning; SAH, malignancy, lupus.

- **Ix:** LP — CSF opening pressure, protein, lactate, glucose, MC&S, PCR (HSV, VZV, enteroviruses ± EBV, etc). Bloods including NH4, serology & HIV. Stool culture. Nose/throat swab (respiratory PCR). Urine (mumps). MRI or CT. EEG may help distinguish psychiatric from organic causes.
- **Mx:** start triple therapy with ceftriaxone, clarithromycin, and aciclovir while the cause is being determined.
  - HSV: **IV aciclovir 10mg/kg every 8 hours** for 14–21 days, then repeat LP to confirm CSF is HSV-negative. Do not routinely use corticosteroids. (Dose added by the pairs audit, 2026-08-29 — see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B56**; paediatric dosing is age-dependent and must be checked.)

> [!note] The adult entry is [[04_Neurology]] Encephalitis, which owns the formal diagnostic criteria (1 major + ≥3 minor).
  - Neurological sequelae are common.
