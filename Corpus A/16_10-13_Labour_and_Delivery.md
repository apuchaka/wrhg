## Normal labour

**D**: onset of regular and painful contractions associated with cervical dilation and descent of the presenting part.
- Occurs after 37w gestation
- Results in spontaneous delivery within 24h of onset of spontaneous contractions
- Divided into 3 stages

**Pre-labour signs**
- "Show": shedding of cervical mucus plug and a little blood (membranes stripping from the os)
- ± Rupture of membranes

### Stage 1
From onset of true labour to full cervical dilation (~8–18h primip; 5–12h multip).
- **Latent phase**: painful, irregular contractions; cervix shortens + dilates to 3cm*
- **Established phase**: regular contractions with dilation from 3cm,* normally 1cm/h

> [!note] *Passmed cites 3cm as the established-phase threshold; OHCS cites 4cm. Source disagreement — verify against local protocol.

### Stage 2
From full dilation to delivery of fetus (~3h primip, 2h multip).
- **Passive phase**: complete cervical dilation but no pushing. 1–2h passive stage recommended in women with epidural anaesthesia to ↓instrumental delivery rate
- **Active phase**: pushing with abdominal muscles + Valsalva. Encourage comfortable maternal position. If contractions wane, oxytocin augmentation may be needed. Active phase ~1h; if >1h, consider ventouse extraction, forceps delivery, or C-section

### Stage 3
Delivery of the placenta.
- Uterus contracts to <24w size after birth
- Placenta separates from uterus, buckles; small retro-placental haemorrhage aids removal

### Monitoring in labour

> [!info] Monitoring frequency
> - Fetal heart rate: every 15 min, or continuous CTG
> - Contractions: assessed every 30 min
> - Maternal HR: every 1h
> - Maternal BP and temperature: every 4h
> - Vaginal exam: offered every 4h to check labour progression
> - Maternal urine: checked for ketones/protein every 4h

## Induction of labour

**Indications**
- Post-maturity pregnancy (1–2w after estimated delivery date)
- Prelabour premature rupture of membranes where labour doesn't start spontaneously
- Maternal medical problems: diabetic mother >38w, pre-eclampsia, obstetric cholestasis
- Rhesus disease
- Intrauterine fetal death

**Contraindications** (consider C-section instead)
- Malpresentation (incl. breech)
- Fetal distress
- Placenta praevia, vasa praevia
- Cord presentation
- Pelvic tumour (e.g. cervical fibroid)

### Modified Bishop score

> [!info] Modified Bishop score
> ≥8 indicates favourable cervix (high chance of spontaneous labour or response to induction). <5: labour unlikely to start without induction.

| Score | Cervical position | Consistency | Effacement | Dilation | Fetal station |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Posterior | Firm | 0–30% | <1cm | -3 |
| 1 | Intermediate | Intermediate | 40–50% | 1–2cm | -2 |
| 2 | Anterior | Soft | 60–70% | 3–4cm | -1, 0 |
| 3 | – | – | 80% | >5cm | +1, +2 |

### Methods of induction

- **Membrane sweep**: examining finger inserted through cervix, rotated against uterine wall to separate chorionic membrane from decidua. Can be done by midwife at antenatal clinic. Primip: offered at 40w, 41w. Multip: offered at 41w.
- **Vaginal prostaglandin E2** (dinoprostone) — pessary or gel; CTG monitoring before and for 30 min post-insertion
- **Oral prostaglandin E1** (misoprostol)
- **Maternal oxytocin infusion** (Syntocinon)
- **Amniotomy** ("breaking of waters") — rod-hook device ruptures the amniotic membrane
- **Cervical ripening balloon** — passed through endocervical canal, gently inflated to dilate cervix

**Mx by Bishop score**
- Bishop ≤6: vaginal prostaglandins or oral misoprostol; consider mechanical methods (e.g. balloon catheter) if higher hyperstimulation risk or previous C-section
- Bishop >6: amniotomy and IV oxytocin infusion

> [!danger] Complications of induction — uterine hyperstimulation (1–5%)
> Prolonged/frequent uterine contractions (tachysystole) — can cause fetal hypoxaemia/acidaemia and uterine rupture. **Mx**: remove vaginal prostaglandins, stop IV oxytocin, ± tocolysis.

## Pain relief in labour

**Non-pharmacological**
- Education on labour process
- Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)
- Water birth (NICE-recommended; **not verified against an Australian source**; see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` B32) — water temperature must be <37.5°C to prevent maternal pyrexia

**Pharmacological**

| Option | Notes |
|---|---|
| Nitrous oxide (Entonox, 50% in O2) | CI: pneumothorax. Short onset. SE: N&V, feeling faint |
| Opiate analgesia (e.g. pethidine 50–150mg IM) | Limited pain relief, significant SE (drowsiness, N&V; short-term neonatal respiratory depression/drowsiness); give with antiemetic (e.g. cyclizine 50mg IM) if IM/IV; **CI: water birth (cannot give <2h before entering birthing pool)** |
| Pudendal nerve block (S2-4) | e.g. 8–10mL 1% lidocaine; useful for instrumental delivery |
| Local anaesthesia (lidocaine) | Injected into perineum before episiotomy/suturing |
| Epidural analgesia | Targets T10-S5 fibres; aim for L3/4 space; can top up every 2h |
| Spinal anaesthesia | Used for most C-sections (easier to site than epidural); single dose into subarachnoid space; ↑risk of profound hypotension vs epidural |
| Combined spinal-epidural | Quicker pain relief; useful for C-section with risk of prolonged procedure |

**Epidural complications**: failure to site/patchy block, hypotension, dural puncture (<1:100), post-dural puncture headache, transient/permanent nerve damage (very rare), ↑risk of operative vaginal delivery. Can cause transient fetal bradycardia — recovers with IV fluids.

> [!info] Verified against the Queensland Clinical Guideline "VTE prophylaxis in pregnancy and the puerperium" (2025), already independently confirmed in [[16_01-05_Antenatal_Care]] — **the timing below is genuinely consistent with current Australian practice**, not UK-specific: 12h from last prophylactic dose to neuraxial block/catheter removal, 24h if on therapeutic dose. The "≥4h after block siting before next LMWH dose" is a reasonable minimum, though the Queensland guideline specifies 6h from block insertion to the next dose specifically (vs 4h from catheter *removal* to the next dose) — a small but genuine distinction between these two different steps worth noting, not a wholesale correction.
> - Wait 12h after prophylactic heparin/LMWH before block insertion/catheter removal
> - Wait 24h if on therapeutic dose
> - Wait 6h after block insertion, or 4h after catheter removal, before the next LMWH dose

## Multiple pregnancy

**E**: Twins 1:105 pregnancies; Triplets 1:10,000 pregnancies.

**R**: previous twins, FHx, ↑maternal age, ↑ovulation induction/IVF, race/ethnic origin (e.g. Japanese, Nigerian Yoruba).

**A/P**
- Zygote = fertilised ovum
- **Dizygotic/fraternal twins**: develop from separate ova, 2 zygotes fertilised at the same time
- **Monozygotic**: develop from one zygote splitting into two embryos
  - Dichorionic & diamniotic: separate amniotic sac and placenta each
  - Monochorionic & diamniotic: shared placenta, separate amniotic sacs
  - Monochorionic & monoamniotic: shared placenta and amniotic sac

**S/smx**: large uterus, hyperemesis, >2 poles felt, multiplicity of fetal parts felt, 2 fetal heart rates heard, polyhydramnios (later on).

**Complications during pregnancy**: polyhydramnios, pre-eclampsia (30% in twins), anaemia, ↑antepartum haemorrhage risk (2° ↑abruption and placenta praevia risk from large placenta), gestational diabetes, operative delivery.

**Fetal complications**: ↑perinatal mortality, prematurity (mean gestation 37w twins, 33w triplets), growth restriction, ↑malformation rates (especially if monozygotic), twin-twin transfusion syndrome (monochorionic twins — placental vascular anastomoses, one twin donor, other recipient), fetus papyraceous (fetus dies in utero, shrinks, mummifies).

**Complications during labour**: PPH (2x risk in twins), malpresentation common, vasa praevia rupture, cord prolapse, placental abruption, cord entanglement.

**Preparation for labour**
- Aspirin >12w if other pre-eclampsia risk factors present
- Educate mother on identifying pre-term labour
- Offer elective birth at 37w (uncomplicated dichorionic twins), 36w (uncomplicated monochorionic twins), 35w + steroids (uncomplicated triplets)
- Have paediatricians (1 per baby) at delivery in case resus needed

## Breech presentation

**D**: baby presenting for delivery with buttocks or feet first rather than head. 25% of pregnancies at 28w; 3% at or near term.

**R**: prematurity, SGA, nulliparity, fetal congenital anomalies, previous breech delivery, uterine malformations/fibroids, abnormal amniotic fluid volume (oligo/poly), placental abnormality (e.g. placenta praevia).

> [!info] Classification
> - **Frank/extended breech** (65–70% of breeches): buttocks lead into birth canal; hips flexed, knees extended, feet near head
> - **Complete breech** (15%): buttocks present first, hips/knees flexed (sitting cross-legged)
> - **Incomplete/footling breech**: foot/feet below the breech, lowermost in birth canal; associated with ↑cord prolapse risk (5–20%)

**Mx**
- If <36w: many fetuses turn spontaneously
- If still breech at 36w: offer external cephalic version (ECV) — obstetrician applies pressure via mother's abdomen to turn baby; offered at 36w (nullip) or 37w (multip)
  - Contraindicated if: C-section delivery required, antepartum haemorrhage within last 7 days, abnormal CTG, major uterine abnormality, ruptured membranes, multiple pregnancy
- Patient education: planned C-section reduces perinatal/neonatal mortality risk; mode of birth does not influence long-term health in breech presentation

## Meconium-related problems

**Meconium** = baby's first poop.

**Meconium-stained liquor**
- If passed in late pregnancy, stains amniotic fluid dull green
- May be passed as part of normal labour, or a sign of distress → transfer to consultant-led unit, commence continuous FHR monitoring

> [!danger] Prelabour rupture of membranes with meconium-stained liquor
> Request immediate induction of labour in a consultant-led unit with advanced neonatal life support available.

**Meconium aspiration syndrome**

**D**: respiratory distress in the newborn due to meconium presence in the trachea. Occurs in 1:1000 deliveries.

**R**: >42w pregnancy, pregnancy-induced HTN, maternal DM, maternal drug abuse/smoking, fetal distress, oligohydramnios, chorioamnionitis.

**Mx**: only suction airway if thick meconium present in oropharynx and baby requires resuscitation — should be done by neonatologists only. Babies may require O2 therapy ± ventilation, antibiotics (e.g. ampicillin and gentamicin).

## Reduced fetal movements (RFM)

**Normal movements**: mothers should start feeling movements between 18–20w (first onset = "quickening"). ↑strength/frequency until ~32w, then regular pattern until delivery.

> [!danger] Any reduction is a potentially important clinical sign of impending fetal demise.

**R**: fetal growth restriction, SGA, placental insufficiency, congenital malformations.

**Initial advice**: ask mother to lie in left lateral position and focus on fetal movements for 2h; if <10 movements in that period, go to the maternity assessment unit (or ED if none locally) ASAP — requires same-day assessment.

| Gestation | Mx |
|---|---|
| <28w | Handheld Doppler to confirm fetal heartbeat. If undetectable, offer immediate US. If present, CTG ≥20min to monitor FHR. If concerns remain despite CTG, urgent US to assess abdominal circumference, estimate fetal weight, amniotic fluid volume |
| 24–28w, or <24w with previous fetal movements felt | Handheld Doppler |
| <24w with no previous fetal movements | Refer to maternal fetal medicine unit |

**P**: 70% of pregnancies with a single RFM episode have no further complications. 40–55% of women who suffer stillbirth have RFM.

## Retained placenta

**D**: placenta not delivered by 30 min with active management, or by 60 min with physiological birth.

**R**: previous retained placenta or uterine surgery, preterm delivery, maternal age >35, placental weight <600g, parity >5, induced labour, pethidine use during labour.

**Mx**
- Avoid excessive cord traction (cord may snap, uterus may invert)
- Check placenta is not in vagina
- Palpate abdomen, try to precipitate a contraction
- Oxytocin: encourage breastfeeding (natural ↑); 20IU oxytocin in 20mL saline into umbilical vein + clamp cord proximally
- Empty bladder
- If still not delivered after further 30 min: offer exam to assess if manual removal needed (stop if painful) → transfer to theatre for regional anaesthesia; IV access + FBC, G&S + consent

**Manual removal**: lithotomy position; one hand stabilises uterus on abdomen; other hand inserted through cervix, works around placenta to separate it, then pulls cord to remove. Ensure complete removal. Give oxytocin + 1 dose of antibiotics. If still not separated (placenta accreta), senior help ASAP.

> [!danger] Main complication is haemorrhage — do not delay manual removal if needed as this may precipitate PPH.

## Instrumental vaginal delivery

**Indications**
- Maternal: prolonged 2nd stage, maternal exhaustion, medical avoidance of pushing (e.g. severe cardiac disease), pushing not possible
- Fetal: suspected fetal distress, for after-coming head in breech delivery

| Instrument | Notes |
|---|---|
| Forceps | Curved blades around fetal head, traction via handles. ↓maternal effort vs ventouse. Safer for baby but ↑maternal genital tract trauma. Fetal complications (rare): facial nerve palsy, skull fractures, orbital injury, ICH |
| Ventouse | Suction cup on fetal scalp. Causes head swelling ("chignon"), resolves in 24–48h — warn mother. ↓maternal genital tract trauma but ↑fetal trauma risk; more likely to fail. Fetal complications: cephalhaematoma (common), retinal haemorrhage, scalp lacerations/avulsions (more common if >3 pulls) |

**Other notes**: avoid sequential use of instruments. Abandon use if: no descent with each pull, delivery not imminent after 3 pulls, head impacted in pelvis and difficult to deliver → emergency C-section.

## Caesarean section

**D**: delivery of fetus through incision in abdominal wall and uterus. 25% of nullip deliveries; <5% of multip if no previous CS. Previous CS is the greatest predictor of CS.

**Indications**: fetal compromise (e.g. fetal bradycardia, scalp pH <7.20, cord prolapse), failure to progress/failed induction, malpresentation (e.g. transverse lie), severe pre-eclampsia, IUGR with absent/reversed end-diastolic flow, twin pregnancy with non-cephalic presenting twin, placenta praevia.

**Types**: lower segment CS (LSCS) — transverse or vertical incision, lower down; most commonly used (better outcomes, lower risk). Classical — rarely used now, almost like a laparotomy.

> [!info] CS categories
> | Category | Description | Timeframe |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 (crash) | Immediate threat to life of woman or fetus (e.g. placental abruption) | Deliver within 30 min |
> | 2 | Maternal/fetal compromise, not immediately life-threatening (e.g. failure to progress) | Deliver within 75 min |
> | 3 | Semi-elective (e.g. pre-eclampsia) | — |
> | 4 (elective) | E.g. term singleton breech; carried out after 39w unless risk changes category | — |

> [!info] Partially verified — the Category 1–4 caesarean urgency classification (30 min for Category 1, 75 min for Category 2) is not UK-specific; it's the same framework referenced in Australian state guidelines (e.g. Safer Care Victoria) and RANZCOG-aligned practice, so this structure doesn't need correcting. The specific crossmatch unit counts are genuinely more risk-stratified in Australian practice than a single fixed number per diagnosis: one NSW guideline specifically recommends group-and-hold routinely for placenta praevia, escalating to crossmatch (illustratively 2 units) specifically when the placenta is **anterior** — reflecting the genuinely higher bleeding risk from an anterior placenta at caesarean, rather than a flat "praevia = 6 units" rule. The actual unit count in any specific case should follow current local massive transfusion/patient blood management protocol and the individual risk profile (praevia type, accreta suspicion, anterior/posterior position, prior caesarean) rather than a fixed number — treat the figures below as illustrative of the general principle (bleeding risk should be actively risk-stratified, not treated as one-size-fits-all), not as a fixed Australian standard.

**Risks to mother**
- Frequent: persistent wound/abdominal discomfort (may last months), ↑risk of repeat CS, readmission, haemorrhage, infection (wound, endometritis)
- Serious: emergency hysterectomy, further surgery (e.g. curettage for retained placental tissue), ICU admission, thromboembolic disease, bladder/ureteric injury, death (1:12,000)
- Future pregnancies: ↑uterine rupture risk, ↑antepartum stillbirth risk, ↑placenta praevia/accreta risk

**Risks to baby**: lacerations (1–2:100).

**Vaginal birth after CS (VBAC)**
- Appropriate method for pregnant women ≥37w with single previous CS
- Successful in 70–75%
- Contraindications: previous uterine rupture, classical CS scar

## Stillbirth / intrauterine fetal death (IUFD)

> [!info] Verified — the Australian definition of stillbirth is death in utero at or after **20 weeks' gestation, or birthweight ≥400g** (already established for this project in [[17_03_Termination_of_Pregnancy_and_Miscarriage]] Miscarriage) — genuinely different from the UK's 24-week threshold, not a relabelling. Loss before this threshold is classified as miscarriage.

**D**: death in utero at or after 20 weeks' gestation (or birthweight ≥400g); loss before this threshold is classified as miscarriage. Occurs in roughly 1:200 births.

**Causes**: no cause found in 28%; placental cause 12%; ante-/intrapartum haemorrhage 11%; major congenital anomaly 9%; others (infection, HTN, renal disease, diabetes, IUGR).

**R**: multiple pregnancy, social deprivation, ↑maternal age, smoking, previous CS, IVF, obesity.

**S/smx**: ↓fetal movements.

**Ix/Dx**: handheld Doppler (RFM); US — absent heartbeat requires 2 independent practitioners to confirm; repeat US if mother requests.

**Mx**
- If mother Rh-negative: give anti-D
- Kleihauer test on all women to diagnose fetomaternal haemorrhage (screens/quantifies severity); repeat at 48h if large FMH to ensure all fetal RBCs cleared
- Advise delivery for pre-eclampsia, abruption, sepsis, coagulopathy, or membrane rupture
- Mother may deliver spontaneously or require induction
- Arrange follow-up with obstetrician to discuss cause and implications for future pregnancy

**Ix for cause**: Kleihauer + other bloods, blood culture, viral screen, MSU, thrombophilia screen, antibodies, cervical swab, fetal/placental swab + parental permission for postmortem.

*(See [[17_03_Termination_of_Pregnancy_and_Miscarriage]] Miscarriage, not repeated here.)*

## Placenta problems

### Placenta praevia

**D**: placenta overlying the cervical os. Found in ~0.5% of pregnancies. "Low-lying placenta" = <2cm from cervical os but not directly covering it.

**R**: previous C-section, multiparity, multiple pregnancy.

**A/P**: exact cause unknown; uterine scarring may interfere with placentation in some cases.

**S/smx**
- Shock in proportion to visible blood loss
- Lie and presentation may be abnormal
- **No pain, no uterine tenderness** ("painless praevia")
- Coagulation problems rare, fetal heart usually normal

> [!info] Gap-filled — praevia's "painless, shock proportional to visible loss" and abruption's "constant pain, shock disproportionate to visible loss" (see [[16_14-15_Obstetric_Emergencies]] Placental abruption, not repeated here) are presented as contrasting features in both entries without the anatomical mechanism explaining why. **In placenta praevia, bleeding originates from placental vessels overlying or near the cervix, disrupted as the lower uterine segment stretches and the cervix begins to efface/dilate later in pregnancy — this blood flows out externally through the cervix and vagina** ("revealed" haemorrhage) without involving the myometrium itself, so there's no tissue irritation or uterine distension to cause pain, and essentially all lost blood is externally visible, meaning shock genuinely does track with visible loss. **In placental abruption, bleeding occurs into the space between the placenta and uterine wall (retroplacental hemorrhage)** — this accumulating blood directly irritates and distends the myometrium, producing the pain and uterine tenderness/rigidity that's abruption's hallmark, and critically, a substantial proportion of this blood can remain **concealed** (trapped behind the placenta, never reaching the cervix to become externally visible) — which is precisely why shock in abruption can be dramatically out of proportion to the blood loss actually seen, given a large volume may be hidden entirely from view.

> [!danger] Ix
> US first — do **NOT** perform digital vaginal exam before US, as this may provoke haemorrhage. Usually picked up on routine 20w abdominal US; transvaginal US is more accurate and safe.

> [!info] Classical grading
> | Grade | Description |
> |---|---|
> | I | Placenta reaches lower segment but not internal os |
> | II | Reaches internal os but does not cover it |
> | III | Covers internal os before dilation, not when dilated |
> | IV (major) | Completely covers internal os |

**Mx**
- If identified at 20w: rescan at 32w; if still present, rescan q2w; final US at 36–37w to determine delivery method
- Elective C-section for grades III/IV between 37–38w; trial of vaginal delivery if grade I
- No need to limit activity/intercourse unless bleeding
- If known placenta praevia and labour starts before elective CS: emergency C-section to prevent PPH

> [!danger] Placenta praevia with bleeding
> Admit + ABCDE stabilisation. If patient cannot be stabilised, or in labour/term reached, perform emergency C-section.

### Placenta accreta

**D**: abnormal adherence of all or part of the placenta to the uterus.
- Placenta **increta** if myometrium infiltrated
- Placenta **percreta** if serosa/perimetrium infiltrated

**R**: previous C-section, placenta praevia.

**Dx**: colour Doppler US or MRI. All 3 types predispose to PPH.

**Mx**: planned delivery at 35–36w with elective C-section; G&S + crossmatch in preparation for potential haemorrhage; hysterectomy (with placenta left in situ) is standard of care, though experienced surgeons may attempt uterus-preserving surgery.

> [!warning] **These recommendations are attributed to the RCOG — a UK college — and are not confirmed as Australian practice.** This file's localisation box at the top verifies the **LMWH/regional anaesthesia timing**, the **caesarean urgency classification** and the blood-product content; **it does not cover this entry**, and a file-level "Localised for Australia" heading is easy to read as if it did. Elsewhere in the corpus an RCOG guideline is used legitimately because **RANZCOG explicitly adopts it** ([[16_06-07_Ante-Perinatal_Infections]] Varicella zoster in pregnancy says so directly) — no such adoption has been established for placenta accreta. **Confirm against RANZCOG's placenta accreta spectrum guidance**, particularly the **35–36 week delivery timing**, before treating this as the Australian standard. See `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B58**. Found by the G34 round, 2026-08-29 — sixth instance of the partial-verification-box pattern.

### Vasa praevia

**D**: malformation of fetal vessels running through the free placental membranes.

- Risk of fetal haemorrhage when membranes rupture
- Requires C-section — urgent if fetal compromise, elective if detected antenatally

### Other placental anomalies

| Condition | Description |
|---|---|
| Placental succenturia (5%) | Separate (succenturiate) lobe away from main placenta, may fail to separate normally — can cause PPH or puerperal sepsis |
| Placenta membranacea | Thin placenta surrounds baby; predisposes to antepartum haemorrhage, PPH, IUGR |
| Velamentous insertion (1%) | Umbilical vessels travel within membranes before placental insertion |

### Other causes of antepartum haemorrhage (APH)

**D**: genital tract bleeding from 24w gestation. 3–5% of pregnancies.

- Placental abruption (**painful**)
- Placenta praevia (**painless**)
- Vasa praevia, cervical polyps, erosions/carcinomas, cervicitis, vaginitis, vulval varicosities

## Perineal tears + episiotomy

> [!info] Perineal tear classification
> | Degree | Description | Management |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1st | Superficial, no muscle involvement | Generally no repair required |
> | 2nd | Perineal muscle injury, does NOT involve anal sphincter | Sutures (on ward) |
> | 3rd | Anal sphincter injury — 3a: external sphincter <50% torn; 3b: >50% torn; 3c: both external and internal sphincters torn | Repair in theatre + antibiotics |
> | 4th | Anal sphincter **and** rectal mucosa injury | Repair in theatre under epidural/GA + antibiotics |

**R (for tears)**: primigravida, large babies, precipitous (very quick) labour, shoulder dystocia, forceps delivery.

**Mx**
- Grade III & IV require repair in theatre with intraoperative antibiotic cover
- Advise high-fibre diet and laxatives for 10 days to avoid constipation
- Arrange pelvic floor physiotherapy and obstetric clinic follow-up at 6–12w postnatally
- If pain or incontinence develops: refer to specialist gynae or colorectal surgeon for endoanal US or manometry

**Episiotomy**: cut to the perineum to enlarge cervical outlet for faster delivery (e.g. fetal distress). Tissues cut: vaginal epithelium, perineal skin, bulbocavernosus muscle, superficial and deep transverse perineal muscles. Complications: bleeding, infection, breakdown, haematoma formation. Post-cut Mx: ice pack, salt baths, rectal diclofenac.

## Postpartum physiological changes

- Uterus: 1kg → 100g (afterpains may occur while shrinking, especially during breastfeeding)
- Cervix becomes firm over 3 days; internal os closes by 3 days; external os closes by 3 weeks
- Lochia (endometrial slough, red/white cells) passed per vaginum: red → yellow → white, until 6w
- Colostrum for first 3 days → milk; breasts swollen/tender with engorgement at 3–4 days

## Puerperal psychosis

**E**: 1:500 births.

**S/smx**: high suicidal drive, mania, severe depression, rarely schizophrenic symptoms with delusions. Presents by 7 days postpartum in 50%, by 3 months in 90%. Sudden onset with rapid deterioration.

**Mx**: admit + refer to perinatal psychiatry team or psychiatric liaison; ideally specialist mother and baby unit if possible.

**P**: risk of recurrence in a following pregnancy is high — **published estimates range roughly 25–50%, and [[14_03_Psych_-_Psychotic_Disorders_and_Antipsychotics]] Postpartum (Puerperal) Psychosis states >50%.** The two entries gave different figures for the same fact; neither has been resolved against an Australian source — see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B59**. Either way it is the single strongest risk factor and the reason pre-conception perinatal psychiatry planning is offered.

> [!note] **A prior history of bipolar disorder is the other major risk factor** (~30% risk of a postpartum episode), and postpartum psychosis is increasingly understood as sitting within the bipolar spectrum — the aetiological link this entry did not carry. [[14_03_Psych_-_Psychotic_Disorders_and_Antipsychotics]] Postpartum (Puerperal) Psychosis owns the full entry, including the organic causes to exclude (postpartum sepsis, significant blood loss, eclampsia-related complications) and **PANDA**, the Australian perinatal mental health helpline. Linked by the G35 round, 2026-08-29.
