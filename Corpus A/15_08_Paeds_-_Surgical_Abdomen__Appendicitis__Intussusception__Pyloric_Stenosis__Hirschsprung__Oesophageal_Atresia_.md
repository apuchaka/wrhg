## Appendicitis

- **D:** acute inflammation of the vermiform appendix.
- **A:** obstruction of the lumen of the appendix by a faecolith, normal stool, or lymphoid hyperplasia.
- **P:** obstruction → distension + bacteria multiply.
- **S/Smx:** abdominal pain — starting centrally, then localising to the RIF over 1–12h; constant with intermittent cramps; worse on movement, coughing. Location of the appendix affects the location of pain.
  - Use the Appendicitis Inflammatory Response (AIR) score to quantify risk — symptoms (vomiting, pain in RIF, migration of pain to RIF), exam (rebound tenderness, guarding, fever), bloods (↑neutrophils, ↑WBC, ↑CRP).
  - Anorexia is very common; patients are unlikely to be hungry.
  - DRE: boggy sensation, possible right-sided tenderness.
  - Rovsing's sign (palpation in LIF causing pain in RIF); psoas sign (pain on extending the hip if retrocaecal appendix).

> [!note] Uncommon in children <4 years old.

- **Dx/Ix:** establish IV access. If ↓BP/septic, catheterise + fluid balance. Request FBC (Hb, WCC), U&Es, CRP. Diagnosis can be clinical — in children, US first; US in females where pelvic organ pathology is suspected; CT scan only if inconclusive.
- **Mx:** appendectomy (usually laparoscopic). Prophylactic IV antibiotics. Patients with perforated appendicitis require copious abdominal lavage. Conservative management with antibiotics only is possible, depending on the surgeon.

*(See [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Appendicitis for the fuller adult-context entry, including the **Alvarado** score and its interpretation — note the two files use **different** scores: AIR here, Alvarado there and non-operative antibiotic-alone management trade-offs, not repeated here.)*

## Intussusception

- **D:** prolapse of one part of intestine into the lumen of the adjoining distal part, causing intestinal obstruction.
- **R:** M>F, age 6–12 months.
- **A:** unclear, but likely related to hyperplasia of Peyer's patches and lymphoid tissue in the intestinal wall after an antecedent viral infection. In older children/adults, a pathological lead point (anatomical abnormality, e.g. luminal polyps) may be found.
- **P:** telescoping of intestine. The mesentery is dragged in, obstructing venous return → oedema, mucosal bleeding, ↑pressure.
- **Features:** intermittent, severe, crampy, progressive abdominal pain; inconsolable crying. During a paroxysm, the infant will characteristically draw their knees up and turn pale — the infant is typically relatively settled between bouts of pain. Vomiting.

> [!warning] Bloodstained stool — "red-currant jelly" — is a LATE sign, directly reflecting the venous congestion and mucosal bleeding already described above (P:) — as obstructed venous return progressively worsens, mucosal ischaemia and sloughing mix with intestinal mucus to produce the characteristic jelly-like, blood-streaked appearance; being a late finding, its absence does not exclude intussusception, and clinical suspicion (the paroxysmal pain pattern above) should prompt urgent Ix regardless of stool appearance.

- Sausage-shaped mass in the RUQ.
- **Dx/Ix:** ultrasound (donut or target sign); plain XR (soft tissue mass, small bowel obstruction, free air).
- **Mx:**
  - IV fluids + ensure fluid balance. Analgesia + sedation (morphine 0.2mg/kg). Antibiotics + NGT. Cross-match + G&S 2 units.
  - Radiological reduction: air enema works in 75%. If irreducible or perforation, halt and convert to surgical management (recurrence risk 5–7%).
  - Surgical reduction: laparotomy without enema if evidence of peritonitis or perforation. Manual reduction by retrograde squeezing and gentle proximal traction. Resection and anastomosis if bowel viability is in doubt. Post-reduction septic shock may occur secondary to bacterial product release.
  - Resume oral feeding in 24–48h and discharge home in 4–5 days.

## Pyloric stenosis

- **D:** hypertrophy of the pyloric sphincter results in narrowing of the pyloric canal. Incidence 4 in 1000 live births. M>F 4×. 10–15% positive family history. Firstborns more affected.
- **A:** no single aetiology. Hypertrophy of pyloric smooth muscle in early infancy (3–6 weeks).
- **P:** prolonged vomiting → ↓Cl-, ↓K+ metabolic alkalosis, etc.

> [!info] Gap-filled — this classic electrolyte pattern was reduced to a bare arrow chain; the actual mechanism (and its genuinely important "paradoxical" twist) is one of the most frequently tested points in paediatric surgery. **The primary driver: vomiting loses gastric contents rich in hydrochloric acid** — direct loss of H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions is what causes both the **metabolic alkalosis** (losing acid raises pH) and the **hypochloraemia**, straightforwardly. **The hypokalaemia is more indirect and genuinely counterintuitive**: as ongoing vomiting causes volume depletion, the kidneys respond by activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system to conserve sodium and water — but aldosterone's sodium-retention mechanism in the distal tubule works by exchanging sodium for **both** potassium and hydrogen ions, so this compensatory response excretes potassium (worsening hypokalaemia) **and paradoxically excretes hydrogen ions despite the body already being alkalotic** ("paradoxical aciduria"). This is a genuinely important point: the kidney's own volume-conserving response actively perpetuates the alkalosis it should ideally be correcting, which is precisely why adequate saline (correcting the volume depletion that's driving this cycle) and potassium replacement — not just fluid alone — are both needed to break the cycle, as reflected in the specific fluid/electrolyte replacement regimen below.

- **S/Smx:** "projectile" vomiting, typically 30 minutes after a feed; ± constipation and dehydration; ± palpable mass in the upper abdomen; hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic alkalosis secondary to persistent vomiting. Test feeding will reveal a pyloric "tumour" (middle of the epigastrium). Vomit will not have bile, no diarrhoea (constipation is likely), baby is rarely obtunded.
- **Dx/Ix:** clinical. If in doubt, ultrasound — thickened (>4mm), elongated (>16mm) pyloric muscle, ↑muscle to lumen ratio with ↓fluid movement. Bloods: U&Es, ABG.
- **Mx:**
  - Resuscitate with IV fluids. Correct hypovolaemia: 10mL/kg 0.9% NS. Correct electrolyte imbalance: 0.45% NS/5% dextrose with KCl at a rate of 120–150mL/kg/24h.
  - NGT drainage to prevent aspiration.
  - Ramstedt pyloromyotomy — division of pyloric muscle fibres without opening the bowel lumen.
  - Start feeding within 4–6h post-op, increasing to full volume by 24h.

## Hirschsprung disease

- **D:** congenital condition characterised by partial or complete colonic functional obstruction associated with the absence of ganglion cells. 1 in 5000 births. M>F 3×. Associated with Down syndrome.
- **A:** genetic (associated with 24 genes).
- **P:** absence of ganglion cells, presence of hypertrophic nerves, ↑AChE. Failure in the development of tissue derived from the neural crest.
- **S/Smx:** neonatal period — e.g. failure or delay to pass meconium. Older children: constipation, abdominal distension.
- **Dx/Ix:** clinical symptoms. Enema XR looking for a transition zone. Rectal biopsy + histology.
- **Mx:** resuscitation + analgesia. Decompression of the colon with regular saline rectal washouts. Defunctioning stoma if decompression is not achieved, or if the whole colon is involved. Definitive surgery.

## Oesophageal atresia / Tracheo-oesophageal fistula

- **D:** congenital malformations resulting from defective separation of the common embryologic precursors to both the oesophagus and trachea. 1 in 2500–4500 live births. Can present with other anomalies.
- **Prenatal signs:** polyhydramnios; small/absent stomach bubble.
- **Postnatal signs:** respiratory distress, ↑secretions, aspirating feeds. Symptoms depend on anatomy — H-type TOF may present much later.
- **Dx/Ix:** antenatal US/MRI; XR chest/abdo will show an NGT coiled in the upper pouch.
- **Mx:** depends on anatomy. Generally, patients kept nil by mouth with continuous oesophageal pouch suction (Replogle tube). Primary surgical repair. Consider management of other anomalies.

> [!info] Gross types
> - **A.** Gap between two oesophageal blind pouches with no fistula.
> - **B.** Oesophageal atresia with proximal tracheoesophageal fistula.
> - **C.** Oesophageal atresia with distal tracheoesophageal fistula.
> - **D.** Oesophageal atresia with proximal AND distal tracheoesophageal fistula.
> - **E.** Tracheoesophageal fistula only.
