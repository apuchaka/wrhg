## 0.1 Knee — ligament and meniscal injuries

> [!info] Unhappy triad — usually secondary to a lateral blow to the knee. ACL + MCL + meniscus (medial or lateral) injury together.

| Structure | Key features |
|---|---|
| **ACL** | Secondary to twisting injuries; positive anterior drawer & Lachman's test |
| **PCL** | Dashboard injuries |
| **MCL** | Damage commonly from skiing and valgus stress; usually causes passive knee abduction |
| **LCL** | Isolated injury uncommon |
| **Menisci** | Secondary to twisting injuries; locking and giving way common; Thessaly's test — weight bearing at 20° of knee flexion, positive if pain on twisting the knee |

- **Ix:** MRI needed to visualise ligaments and menisci.
- **Mx:** refer to orthopaedics — arthroscopic or open surgical management.

## 0.2 Bursitis & cysts of the knee

- **Infrapatellar bursitis:** associated with kneeling ("Clergyman's knee").
- **Prepatellar bursitis:** associated with kneeling ("Housemaid's knee").
- **Ix (both):** primarily clinical, but the same key diagnostic task as olecranon bursitis in [[11_02_Ortho_-_Upper_Limb__Shoulder__Elbow__Distal_Radius_Fractures_]] applies — distinguishing aseptic from septic bursitis, given repetitive kneeling can cause skin breaches that predispose to infection; aspirate for microscopy, Gram stain, and culture if there's any suspicion of infection (marked warmth/erythema, fever, or an obvious skin breach overlying the bursa).
- **Mx (both):** the same aseptic-vs-septic distinction and management principle as olecranon bursitis in [[11_02_Ortho_-_Upper_Limb__Shoulder__Elbow__Distal_Radius_Fractures_]] applies here — not repeated in full, but in brief: **aseptic** — activity modification (avoiding/padding kneeling), ice, NSAIDs, aspiration for symptomatic relief of large effusions; **septic** — antibiotics ± aspiration/drainage, treated with the same urgency as other periarticular soft tissue infections.
- **Baker cyst:**
  - **D:** distension of the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa. Primary — no underlying pathology. Secondary — underlying problem, e.g. OA.
  - **S/Smx:** bulge behind the knee; if rupture occurs, may cause symptoms similar to DVT (but can also be asymptomatic).
  - **Ix:** clinical diagnosis in typical cases; ultrasound can confirm and distinguish from other popliteal masses, and is particularly useful when a ruptured cyst is mimicking DVT given the two are a classic diagnostic confusion — compression ultrasound can assess for both simultaneously, which is often the actual clinical question being asked.
  - **Mx:** in children, no treatment required. In adults, treat the underlying cause (e.g. OA management if secondary); aspiration ± corticosteroid injection can be considered for symptomatic cysts, though recurrence is common without addressing the underlying joint pathology driving fluid production.

## 0.3 Patella fracture

- **A:** direct injury (e.g. fall, dashboard injury); indirect injury caused by forceful contraction of the quadriceps (e.g. when trying to prevent a fall).
- **S/Smx:** swelling and bruising; look for associated fractures; pain and tenderness around the knee, well localised to the patella; test whether the patient can still extend their leg.
- **Ix:** XR (AP & lateral).
- **Mx:** undisplaced + intact extensor mechanism — does not require surgery; hinged knee brace for 6 weeks + ask the patient to fully weight bear. Otherwise, surgical management is likely required.

## 0.4 Iliotibial band syndrome

- **D:** inflammation of the iliotibial band.
- **R:** runners (1 in 10 people who run regularly).
- **S/Smx:** burning pain at or just under the lateral femoral epicondyle; tenderness 2–3cm above the lateral knee joint line.
- **Mx:** activity modification and iliotibial band stretches ± physiotherapy.

## 0.5 Ankle fractures

> [!info] Ottawa Rules for XR of ankles
> - Pain in the malleolar zone.
> - Bony tenderness at the lateral malleolar zone (from the tip of the lateral malleolus to include the lower 6cm of the posterior border of the fibula).
> - Bony tenderness at the medial malleolar zone (from the tip of the medial malleolus to the lower 6cm of the posterior border of the tibia).
> - Inability to walk four weight-bearing steps immediately after the injury and in the Emergency Department (ED).

> [!info] Weber classification
> - **A:** below the syndesmosis.
> - **B:** fracture starts at the level of the tibial plafond and may extend proximally to involve the syndesmosis.
> - **C:** above the syndesmosis, which may itself be damaged.
>
> A subtype known as a **Maisonneuve fracture** may occur with a spiral fibular fracture that leads to disruption of the syndesmosis with widening of the ankle joint — surgery is required.

- **Mx:** depends on stability of the ankle joint and patient co-morbidities.
  - All ankle fractures should be promptly reduced to remove pressure on the overlying skin and subsequent necrosis.
  - Young patients with unstable, high velocity, or proximal injuries will usually require surgical repair.
  - Elderly patients — conservative management is usually better.

## 0.6 Ankle sprains

- **D:** a sprain is a stretching, partial, or complete tear of a ligament.
  - High ankle sprains — involving the syndesmosis.
  - Low ankle sprains — involving the lateral collateral ligaments.

### 0.6.1 Low ankle sprains (90%)

- Injury to the ATFL is most common. Inversion injury is the most common mechanism.
- **S/Smx:** pain, swelling, tenderness over affected ligaments ± bruising; patients are usually able to weight bear unless severe.

> [!info] Grading
> - **Grade I (mild):** microtear of ligament.
> - **Grade II (moderate):** partial tear.
> - **Grade III (severe):** complete tear.

- **Ix:** XR by Ottawa rules ± MRI.
- **Mx:** RICE ± orthosis (cast, crutches). Severe — consider surgery.

### 0.6.2 High ankle sprains (10%)

- Usually secondary to an eversion mechanism.
- Weight bearing is difficult.

> [!tip] Hopkin's squeeze test — pain when the tibia and fibula are squeezed together mid-calf.

- **Ix:** XR by Ottawa rules ± MRI.
- **Mx:** if no separation — non-weight-bearing orthosis or cast until pain subsides. If separation — ORIF.

## 0.7 Achilles tendon

- **R:** quinolones (e.g. ciprofloxacin) associated with tendon disorders. Hypercholesterolaemia associated with tendon xanthomata.

### 0.7.1 Tendinitis

- Gradual onset of posterior heel pain, worse following activity; morning pain and stiffness.
- **Mx:** supportive — analgesia, reduce precipitating activities, physiotherapy (calf eccentric exercises).

### 0.7.2 Tendon rupture

> [!note] Gap-filled — this entry was genuinely thin relative to its High-yield CSV status: no examination technique, no Ix, and no Mx at all.

- **S/Smx:** sudden pop + sudden pain + inability to walk/continue playing, often during forceful push-off (classically sport); palpable gap in the tendon on examination; weak or absent active plantarflexion.
- **Ix — Simmonds-Thompson test (the "calf squeeze" test), the key bedside diagnostic tool:** patient prone with the foot hanging freely off the end of the bed; squeeze the calf — **normal plantarflexion of the foot is a negative test; absent/minimal plantarflexion compared to the unaffected side is positive**, indicating rupture. Genuinely high accuracy (~96% sensitivity, ~93% specificity) — more sensitive for acute rupture than imaging. A **false negative can occur if the plantaris tendon remains intact**, since it can independently produce some plantarflexion despite a complete Achilles rupture — a genuinely important pitfall given it can falsely reassure. Ultrasound or MRI reserved for equivocal clinical findings or to measure the tendon gap for surgical planning, not needed routinely when the clinical picture and calf squeeze test are clearly positive.
- **Mx:** genuinely an area of ongoing debate rather than one settled answer — **current evidence shows no significant difference in long-term functional outcomes between surgical repair and conservative (functional rehabilitation with early mobilisation in a boot) management** for most patients, a meaningful shift from the older belief that surgery was clearly superior. Conservative management carries a **higher re-rupture risk** but avoids surgical complications (wound infection, sural nerve injury); surgical repair carries lower re-rupture risk but the complication profile above. The traditional cast-immobilisation-alone approach (without early functional rehabilitation) is now recognised as inferior to both modern options, given a high historical re-rupture rate (10–30%). Management should be individualised (patient age, activity level, tendon gap size) and discussed with orthopaedics rather than defaulting to either option as a fixed rule.

> [!info] Simmond's triad — ask the patient to lie down, feet hanging off the bed:
> 1. Rupture may lead to ↑dorsiflexion.
> 2. Feel for a gap in the tendon.
> 3. Squeeze the calf muscle — rupture will cause the muscle to stay in a neutral position.

- **Dx:** by ultrasound.
- **Mx:** urgent referral to orthopaedics.
