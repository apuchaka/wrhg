## CMV (Cytomegalovirus) in pregnancy

🐞 CMV — subtype of Herpes group.

**E**: 5:1000 live births infected. 20% develop late, usually minor problems (1:1000). 10% symptomatic — 1/3 die, 2/3 have long-term problems (congenital CMV).

**Maternal s/smx**: mononucleosis-like — flu-like symptoms, ↑temperature, lymphadenopathy, rash, sore throat.

**Fetal s/smx (congenital CMV)**: growth retardation, pinpoint petechial "blueberry muffin" skin lesions, microcephaly, sensorineural deafness, encephalitis (seizures), hepatosplenomegaly → jaundice.

**Ix**: generally refer to lab; amniocentesis at >20w + shell viral culture can detect fetal transmission; throat swab, urine culture, baby's serum after birth.

**Prevention**: avoid exposure to toddlers' urine.

## Toxoplasmosis in pregnancy

🐞 *Toxoplasma gondii* — 🔀 oocytes in cats/rats → GIT, lungs, broken skin.

**E**: affects 2–7:1000 pregnancies; 40% of fetuses affected if mother has illness. Earlier in pregnancy = more damage but less transmissible.

**Maternal s/smx**: similar to glandular fever ± fever, rash, eosinophilia.

**Fetal s/smx**: >90% asymptomatic, picked up by serology. If severe: intracranial calcification, hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis. Others: encephalitis, epilepsy, developmental delay, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, thrombocytopenia, skin rashes.

**Ix**: diagnosis by reference lab IgG/IgM.

> [!info] Verified against SA Health's Toxoplasmosis in Pregnancy Perinatal Practice Guideline, Aug 2026 — **the drug choices and general dosing structure below are genuinely consistent with Australian practice**, not UK-specific figures needing wholesale replacement. The single most important Australia-specific point: **spiramycin, pyrimethamine, and sulfadiazine are not TGA-registered in Australia**, though all remain available (typically via the Special Access Scheme or as named-patient supply through a tertiary/reference centre) — a genuinely practical access point an Australian clinician needs to know before assuming these can simply be prescribed and dispensed through a normal community pharmacy pathway. Management of confirmed/suspected toxoplasmosis in pregnancy should involve specialist input (maternal-fetal medicine/infectious diseases) given both the diagnostic complexity and this medication-access issue. "Avoid sheep during lambing" in the prevention advice below reflects a genuine additional transmission route (infected ewes shedding oocysts around lambing) relevant to rural/farming exposure, not a UK-specific quirk.
> **Maternal Mx**
> - If <18w gestation: start spiramycin 1g PO q8h ASAP
> - In symptomatic non-immune women: test every 10w through pregnancy
> - If infected, consider amniocentesis to check fetal infection
> - If ≥18w and fetal infection confirmed: pyrimethamine 50mg BD day 1, then 1mg/kg/day thereafter; **and** sulfadiazine 50mg/kg/12h; **and** calcium folinate 15mg twice weekly until delivery

**Prevention**: avoid raw meat; wash hands after touching raw meat; wear gloves gardening/handling cat litter; avoid sheep during lambing.

## Intrauterine syphilis

🐞 *Treponema pallidum*

**Fetal/neonatal s/smx**: rhinitis & snuffles, rash, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, anaemia, jaundice, ascites, hydrops, nephrosis, meningitis, keratitis, sensorineural hearing loss.

**Ix**: XR (perichondritis), nasal discharge exam (spirochetes), CSF (↑monocytes and protein with +ve serology).

> [!info] Verified against Melbourne Sexual Health Centre's syphilis-in-pregnancy treatment guidelines and current O&G Magazine RANZCOG-context guidance, Aug 2026 — **this is a genuine, substantial correction, not a units/naming adjustment**: Australian maternal treatment uses **benzathine penicillin (a long-acting depot formulation) 2.4 million units IM as a single dose** for early/symptomatic syphilis, or **three doses of 2.4 million units IM at weekly intervals** for late latent or unknown-duration syphilis — a completely different drug formulation, dose, and schedule from the original "benzylpenicillin 600mg/24h IM daily for 10 days" (benzylpenicillin and benzathine penicillin are pharmacologically different products; the daily-dosing regimen in the original note doesn't reflect Australian practice for this indication at all). **Maternal treatment is only considered adequate if completed at least 30 days before delivery** — a genuinely important practical threshold, since infants born within 30 days of maternal treatment require careful individual neonatal evaluation regardless of the treatment given. For the neonate: a single dose of benzathine penicillin 50,000 U/kg is used specifically for lower-risk infants (mother treated ≥30 days before delivery, with neonatal RPR titre <4× the maternal titre) — higher-risk or inadequately-treated scenarios need a more intensive IV benzylpenicillin course and specialist paediatric input, not the single-dose regimen. Given the complexity of syphilis serology interpretation and treatment adequacy assessment, specialist involvement (infectious diseases/sexual health) is strongly recommended for all cases, not just an optional escalation.
> **Mx**
> - Maternal: benzathine penicillin 2.4 million units IM — single dose for early/symptomatic syphilis; 3 doses at weekly intervals for late latent/unknown duration
> - Neonate, lower risk (mother treated ≥30 days before delivery, neonatal RPR <4× maternal): benzathine penicillin 50,000 U/kg IM single dose
> - Neonate, higher risk or inadequately/not treated: more intensive IV benzylpenicillin course per current local/eTG neonatal protocol and specialist input — not the single-dose regimen above

## Parvovirus B19

🐞 DNA virus — 🔀 respiratory droplets. **E**: 50% of women in UK are immune.

**Maternal s/smx**: usually asymptomatic; if symptomatic — "slapped cheek" rash, maculopapular rash, fever, arthralgia. Usually no maternal complications.

> [!danger] If mother is immunocompromised or susceptible (e.g. sickle cell disease), sudden haemolysis may occur requiring transfusion.

**Fetal s/smx**: affects 30% of fetuses — suppression of erythropoiesis and cardiac toxicity → heart failure and fetal hydrops. 10% of fetuses affected at <20w will die. No teratogenicity.

**Ix**: paired samples in acute and convalescent phases (>10 days apart) — IgM antibodies appear, IgG titres rise.

**Mx**: serial US for signs of fetal anaemia (fetal hydrops, abnormal MCA Dopplers). If fetal anaemia develops, manage in tertiary fetal medicine unit; consider in-utero RBC transfusion.

## Listeria

🐞 Gram +ve rod, *Listeria monocytogenes* — 🔀 soil, food contamination (soft cheeses, unpasteurised milk, deli meats/salads, meat spreads, pâté). Rarely spreads vertically. Affects 6–15:100,000 pregnancies.

**Maternal s/smx**: fever, shivering, myalgia, headache, sore throat, cough, vomiting, diarrhoea, vaginitis. Rarely: miscarriage (± recurrent), premature labour, stillbirth.

> [!info] Maternal Ix/Dx — blood cultures in any pregnant patient with unexplained fever ≥48h. Serology, vaginal and rectal swabs do NOT help. This is a universal diagnostic principle for Listeria, not jurisdiction-specific guidance.

**Fetal s/smx**: 20% affected are stillborn; fetal distress in labour common. Early postnatal: respiratory distress from pneumonia. Others: convulsions, hepatosplenomegaly, pustular/petechial rashes, conjunctivitis, fever, leukopenia, meningitis.

**Fetal Ix/Dx**: blood cultures ± CSF, meconium, placenta cultures.

> [!danger] Mx
> **Isolate baby.** Treat with ampicillin (dosing broadly similar internationally, commonly cited around 50–100mg/kg/dose 6-hourly, though this should be checked against current local protocol) and gentamicin until 1 week after fever subsides. Monitor abx levels.
>
> [!info] Verified, Aug 2026 — the ampicillin + gentamicin combination itself is genuinely standard first-line therapy for neonatal listeriosis internationally, including in Australia, so the drug choice doesn't need correcting. **The gentamicin dose specifically should not be taken as a single fixed number**: a 2025 Australian study (Flinders Medical Centre/SA Health) comparing neonatal gentamicin dosing guidelines found **5 different unique Australian guidelines in use**, with doses ranging 4.5–7mg/kg and intervals of 24–48 hours depending on gestational age and postnatal age at treatment — a deliberately individualised approach, not a single flat mg/kg/interval figure like the original "3mg/kg/12h". Always use the current local NICU/neonatal protocol for the specific gentamicin dose and interval, with therapeutic drug monitoring (peak/trough levels) given the narrow therapeutic index and real nephrotoxicity/ototoxicity risk — this monitoring principle from the original note remains correct and important.

## Hepatitis B in pregnancy

🐞 DNA virus — 🔀 blood, bodily fluids, vertically.

**Maternal infection**: can be asymptomatic. Acute infection in mid/3rd trimester → high risk of perinatal infection. Risk of maternal death 0.5–3%. All mothers screened for HBsAg (then full hepatitis panel) — carriers have HBsAg >6 months; HBeAg+ve indicates ↑infectivity.

**Fetal infection**: without immunisation, 95% of babies born to these mothers develop Hep B; 93% become chronic carriers at 6 months. Most infections occur at birth, some transplacental (apparent vaccination failure). Lifetime HCC risk in infected infants: 50% (M), 20% (F) — most develop cirrhosis.

**Mx**: immunoglobulin (200U IM) + vaccinate babies of carriers/infected mothers at birth. In uncomplicated Hep B, anti-HBc and anti-HBe antibodies develop, antigens disappear ~3 months. Serology of vaccinated baby at 12–15 months; if HBsAg-ve and anti-HBs+ve, child is protected.

- Not transmitted by breastfeeding
- Little evidence that C-section reduces vertical transmission

## Hepatitis E in pregnancy

**Maternal infection**: ↑maternal mortality risk — 25% in 3rd trimester. Can cause fulminant hepatic failure, coma, massive PPH → death.

**Fetal infection**: 33–50% of babies become infected.

## Herpes simplex (HSV) in pregnancy

🐞 HSV1, HSV2.

**Maternal infection**: prevalence of past infection 25%; recurrence in pregnancy rare.

If mother develops **primary genital herpes** in pregnancy:
- Refer to GUM to screen for other STIs + confirm primary infection (PCR)
- If 3rd trimester: PO aciclovir or valaciclovir
- ± elective C-section if primary infection within 6w of due date

If mother has primary infection lesions at time of delivery:
- LSCS recommended
- If vaginal delivery: give mother and newborn high-dose aciclovir
- Avoid fetal blood sampling, scalp electrodes, instrumental delivery

**Fetal infection**: if vaginal delivery, risk to baby 41% → high-dose aciclovir; PCR for baby at birth. Neonatal infection appears at 5–21 days: grouped vesicles/pustules on red base (tends to be on presenting part or trauma sites e.g. scalp electrode); ± periocular/conjunctival lesions; ± non-vesicular rash.

> [!danger] Complications: blindness, ↓IQ, epilepsy, jaundice, respiratory distress, DIC, death (in 30% even with treatment).

## Varicella zoster in pregnancy

🐞 DNA virus (Herpes group).

**Maternal infection**: symptoms as per normal VZV. 5x ↑risk of pneumonitis.

**Risks to baby**
- **Fetal varicella syndrome**: 1% risk if infection before 20w — skin scarring, eye defects (microphthalmia), limb hypoplasia, microcephaly, learning disabilities
- **Shingles in infancy**: 1–2% risk if maternal exposure in 2nd/3rd trimester
- **Severe neonatal varicella**: fatal in 20% of cases — risk if mother develops rash between 5 days before and 2 days after birth (↑↑transmission risk)

> [!info] Verified, Aug 2026 — genuinely different finding from most other flags in this file: **RANZCOG hosts and directly adopts the RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 13 (Chickenpox in Pregnancy) for Australian practice**, rather than having a substantially different Australian-specific protocol — so the overall approach below (VZIG for non-immune exposed women, effective up to 10 days post-exposure, not given once a rash has already developed, aciclovir for established infection) is genuinely consistent with Australian guidance, not a UK-specific approach needing wholesale replacement. The main Australian-specific detail: the product used in Australia is typically **VariZIG**, dosed by weight (125 IU/10kg, up to a maximum of 625 IU) rather than a flat UK-style dose — and, as in the UK, supply can be limited, so availability should be confirmed with the local immunisation/blood product service before offering it. Ideally given within 96 hours of exposure, though effective up to 10 days.
> - If in doubt, check maternal blood for varicella antibodies urgently
> - If ≤20w and not immune: give **VZIG (varicella zoster immunoglobulin)** ASAP — effective up to 10 days post-exposure, and the deadline exists because preformed antibody can only pre-empt an infection that has not yet established (see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Passive Immunisation — Immunoglobulin After an Exposure)
> - If >20w and not immune: VZIG, or PO antivirals from day 7–14 post-exposure
>
> **Mx of chickenpox in pregnancy**: refer to specialist. RCOG suggests PO aciclovir if ≥20w and presenting within 24h of rash onset. If <20w, PO aciclovir considered with caution.

## Chlamydia trachomatis (perinatal)

🐞 *Chlamydia trachomatis*

**Fetal infection/complications**: associated with low birthweight, premature membrane rupture, fetal death; 30% of infected mothers have affected babies. Conjunctivitis develops 5–14 days after birth (late) — inflammation/pus. Complications: chlamydial pneumonitis, pharyngitis, otitis media.

> [!info] Verified, Aug 2026 — the erythromycin dose/duration below is broadly consistent with current international paediatric dosing (oral erythromycin ~50mg/kg/day divided into 4 doses, i.e. ~12.5mg/kg/6h, for 14 days is the most commonly cited regimen; some sources use up to 3 weeks given erythromycin's efficacy is only ~80%, sometimes requiring a second course) — check current eTG for the exact Australian-specified duration, but the drug choice and general dosing structure don't need wholesale replacement. Azithromycin is an accepted alternative with a shorter course where compliance with the 2–3 week erythromycin course is a concern.
> Local eye cleansing. Erythromycin ~12.5mg/kg/6h PO for 2–3 weeks eliminates lung organisms. Give parents erythromycin or azithromycin 1g PO single dose.

## Gonorrhoea (perinatal)

🐞 *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*

**Fetal infection/complications**: occurs within 4 days of birth (early), purulent discharge, lid swelling ± corneal hazing, corneal rupture, panophthalmitis. 50% have concurrent chlamydial infection.

> [!info] Verified, Aug 2026 — **genuinely important Australian-specific practice point, per Children's Health Queensland guidance**: Australia does **not** use routine ocular prophylaxis (silver nitrate or antibiotic ointment/drops instilled at birth) as a preventive measure, unlike historical US/Canadian practice — this means chemical conjunctivitis from prophylaxis (a recognised early differential in some other jurisdictions) is essentially not a consideration in Australian neonates. **For active infection, current international dosing consistently favours a single dose of ceftriaxone (25–50mg/kg IV/IM, max 125mg) as the primary systemic treatment**, with frequent saline eye irrigation as an adjunct rather than routine additional topical antibiotic (e.g. chloramphenicol) once systemic ceftriaxone has been given — a genuinely different emphasis from the original file's cefotaxime-plus-topical-chloramphenicol structure. Hospitalise given gonococcal conjunctivitis can rapidly penetrate an intact cornea (a genuine ocular emergency) — check current eTG for the exact Australian-specified dose/duration before use.
> - Infants of mothers with known untreated gonorrhoea, or infants with confirmed gonococcal conjunctivitis: single dose ceftriaxone (dose per current eTG/local paediatric protocol, typically in the 25–50mg/kg IV/IM range, max 125mg) + frequent saline eye irrigation; hospitalise and evaluate for disseminated infection.

## Ophthalmia neonatorum

**D**: purulent discharge from eye of a neonate <21 days old.

**A**: blocked lacrimal glands, infection (chlamydia, herpes virus, staph, strep, pneumococci, *E. coli*).

**Dx**: swab for bacterial/viral culture, microscopy (look for intracellular gonococci), chlamydia testing.

**Mx**: treat underlying infection; bathe eyes with cooled boiled water or expressed breast milk.

## Clostridium perfringens (puerperal)

**D**: Gram +ve bacilli.

**R**: illegal termination of pregnancy (improperly cleaned equipment).

**S/smx**: endometritis → septicaemia, gangrene → myoglobinuria → renal failure → death. In-utero fetal death. Local infection of anaerobic sites (e.g. haematomas).

**Ix/Dx**: swab genital site — intracellular encapsulated Gram +ve rods.

**Mx**: surgical debridement of all devitalised tissue; hyperbaric O2; high-dose IV benzylpenicillin (erythromycin if severe penicillin allergy).

## TB in pregnancy

- BCG vaccination after birth for: all babies born into households with TB; mothers from areas with high TB prevalence; all babies who will travel to such areas
- Give all other vaccinations per normal schedule; avoid BCG arm for 3 months
- Separate babies from mothers with active/open TB until 2 weeks of treatment and sputum-negative
- Vaccinate baby with BCG and treat with isoniazid until +ve skin reaction (Mantoux)
- Consider CXR in pregnant women with cough, fever, or weight loss
- Encourage breastfeeding

## Group B Streptococcus (GBS)

🔀 Mother to child — 20–40% of mothers carry GBS in bowel flora.

**Risk factors for transmission**: premature birth, prolonged rupture of membranes, previous sibling GBS infection, maternal pyrexia.

**Which screening strategy triggers prophylaxis is service-dependent** — Australian guidance permits either universal culture-based screening at 35–37 weeks or a risk-factor-only approach, and there is no single national standard. Set out in [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Group B Strep (*Streptococcus agalactiae*), not repeated here; the intrapartum dosing below is owned by this file.

**Fetal infection/complications**: severe, early onset — 20% mortality. Pneumonia, meningitis, septicaemia.

> [!info] Verified against Safer Care Victoria's Group B Streptococcus (GBS) — screening and management guideline, Aug 2026 — **the risk-factor list below is broadly consistent with Australian practice** (GBS-positive swab, GBS bacteriuria, preterm labour, prolonged rupture of membranes, intrapartum fever are all genuine Australian indications for intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis, IAP). The **maintenance dose needed a genuine correction**: Australian dosing is benzylpenicillin **3g IV loading, then 1.8g every 4 hours** (not 1.5g) until birth — a real, if modest, difference from the UK figure, not just a units/terminology issue. **Adequate prophylaxis is specifically defined as the first dose given at least 4 hours before birth** — a practical threshold worth stating explicitly, given a shorter interval is considered inadequate regardless of how many doses were given. For penicillin allergy, **Australian first-line alternative is cephazolin** (2g IV loading, then 1g every 8 hours) rather than clindamycin as the primary alternative — clindamycin remains an option in some state guidelines specifically for those with a history of anaphylaxis to penicillin (given cephazolin carries some cross-reactivity risk in that specific group), so the choice of alternative agent should follow the allergy severity, not be a blanket clindamycin default.
>
> **Timing is part of the dose.** **IAP should be given ≥4 hours before birth** wherever possible — prophylaxis started later is substantially less effective at preventing early-onset neonatal disease, which is why the decision to start is made on suspicion rather than after confirmation. (Stated in [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Group B Strep (*Streptococcus agalactiae*) and absent from this entry — the one that owns the regimen — until the Phase 4 close GBS check, 2026-08-29.)
>
> **Dosing**: benzylpenicillin 3g IV loading dose, then 1.8g q4h throughout labour. If penicillin-allergic (no anaphylaxis history): cephazolin 2g IV loading, then 1g q8h. If history of anaphylaxis to penicillin: clindamycin (confirm susceptibility where possible) — check current local/eTG guidance for the specific alternative given practice varies somewhat by state.
