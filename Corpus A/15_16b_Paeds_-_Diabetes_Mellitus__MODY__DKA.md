## Type 1 diabetes mellitus

- **D:** metabolic disorder characterised by hyperglycaemia due to absolute insulin deficiency.
- **R:** genetics/family history — risk is 5% with 1 affected family member.
- **A/P:** autoimmune pancreatic β-cell destruction in genetically susceptible individuals. When 80–90% of β-cells are destroyed, hyperglycaemia develops.
- **S/Smx:** peak age of onset 5–7 years. Weeks of polyuria, polydipsia, lethargy, weight loss ± infection, poor growth, DKA. Secondary nocturnal enuresis.

> [!danger] If suspected in children, refer SAME DAY to the paediatric diabetes MDT.

- **Dx (WHO criteria):** symptoms + ↑blood glucose ≥11.1mmol/L (random); symptoms + ↑blood glucose ≥7mmol/L (fasting); ↑blood glucose on 2 separate occasions.
- **DDx:** T2DM (obesity, Asian or Black ethnicity). Consider monogenic or mitochondrial diabetes in children if diagnosed in the first year of life, absence of ketonaemia during hyperglycaemia, or syndromic features.
- **Ix:** diabetes-specific antibodies can be helpful. C-peptide levels low.
- **Mx:** give BG and ketone monitors. Education of the child + caregivers.

### Insulin regimens

- **Basal bolus:** combination of regular longer-acting insulin with "carb counting" and short- or rapid-acting insulin before meals.
- **Continuous SQ:** insulin pump.
- 1–3 regular injections of mixed insulin.

**Insulin requirements:** start with 0.5–0.75 U/kg/24h (total daily dose).
- If using basal-bolus: divide total daily dose as 1/2 basal insulin + 1/2 in 3 doses before meals.
- If using mixed insulins: give 2/3 pre-breakfast, 1/3 pre-dinner.
- Adjust based on general trends.

### Hypoglycaemia

> [!warning] **This entry treats hypoglycaemia without ever defining it.** No blood glucose threshold is stated anywhere in it — a reader has nothing to act on except the symptoms. The corpus holds **<3.3 mmol/L** for adults ([[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Hypoglycaemia) and **<2.6 mmol/L** for newborns ([[15_23a_Paeds_-_NEC__Neonatal_Hypoglycaemia__Hypotonia]] Neonatal hypoglycaemia, which notes there is no fixed definition), and **neither of those is the figure for a child with diabetes**. Reading the adult number across is the error this box exists to prevent. **The paediatric threshold is not stated here because it has not been verified against an Australian source** — see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B55**. Added by the pairs audit, 2026-08-29.

- **S/Smx:** weakness, dizziness, shaking, palpitations, sweating, anxiety, hunger, vomiting; headache, confusion, blurred vision, lethargy, coma, convulsions. Behavioural changes: agitation, irritability.
- **Mx:**
  - 10–20g of fast-acting glucose by mouth + complex carbs. If not possible, give oral glucose gel — 1 teaspoon of sugar moistened with water under the tongue every 10–12 minutes if nothing else available.
  - Or 5mL/kg IV 10% glucose.
  - Severe, out of hospital: glucagon 1mg IM (500mcg if <8 years old or <25kg).
  - Expect quick return to consciousness; recheck BG within 15 minutes.

> [!warning] If BG is normal after hypo treatment, this may be a post-ictal state after a hypoglycaemic fit — DO NOT give more glucose, as this may cause or worsen cerebral oedema.

## Maturity onset diabetes of the young (MODY)

- Autosomal dominant, non-ketotic diabetes. A single gene defect, as opposed to T1DM/T2DM, which have polygenic and environmental causes.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| MODY2 | Mild, asymptomatic. Drugs rarely needed, complications rare. |
| MODY3 | Most common type. Severe hyperglycaemia after puberty → often misdiagnosed as T1DM. Sensitive to sulfonylureas — some children may be able to stop insulin. Diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy often occur. |
| MODY5 | Associated with pancreatic atrophy, renal abnormalities, genital tract malformations. |
| MODY1, 4, 6 | Rare. |

- **Dx:** clinical suspicion (young age of onset, often non-obese, strong autosomal dominant family history spanning multiple generations, absence of diabetes-specific autoantibodies and typically preserved C-peptide — features that distinguish MODY from both T1DM and T2DM) confirmed by genetic testing, given the specific gene identified directly determines the management approach below rather than being purely academic.
- **Mx:** genuinely gene-subtype-specific, which is why establishing the exact MODY type matters clinically and isn't just diagnostic labelling — **MODY2** typically needs no pharmacological treatment at all given its mild, stable course (diet/lifestyle monitoring only); **MODY3** is characteristically sulfonylurea-sensitive, and a child previously started on insulin (having been misdiagnosed as T1DM, as above) can often be safely transitioned to a sulfonylurea instead once correctly diagnosed — genuinely important given this changes a lifelong insulin-dependent diagnosis into an oral-therapy-manageable one; the rarer subtypes are managed according to their specific features and associated complications (e.g. MODY5's renal and genital tract anomalies need their own dedicated follow-up beyond glycaemic control alone).

## Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

- **D:** acute metabolic complication of diabetes, with a biochemical triad of hyperglycaemia (or history of diabetes), ketonaemia, and metabolic acidosis.
- **R:** inadequate or inappropriate insulin therapy, infection, MI, etc.
- **A:** ↓insulin + ↑glucagon, catecholamines, cortisol, growth hormone → metabolic derangements.
- **P:** leads to hyperglycaemia, volume depletion, electrolyte imbalance. Insulin deficiency leads to release of FFA from adipose tissue, hepatic fatty acid oxidation, and formation of ketone bodies → ketonaemia + acidosis.
- **S/Smx:** abdominal pain; polyuria, polydipsia, dehydration; Kussmaul respiration (deep hyperventilation); acetone-smelling breath ("pear-drops" smell).
- **Dx:** glucose >11 or known diabetes; pH <7.3; bicarbonate <15mmol/L; ketones >3 or urine ketones ++.
- **DDx:** hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) — ↑↑BG >30, little or no acidosis or ketones.

> [!note] The **adult** protocol is [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) — same diagnostic triad and same resolution criteria (pH >7.3, ketones <0.6, bicarbonate >15, within 24h), but a materially different fluid and insulin approach. **Do not read across between them.**

### Management

> [!info] Verified against SA Health's Paediatric Clinical Practice Guideline for DKA in Children, and Queensland Children's Health guidance, Aug 2026 — the overall protocol structure below (deficit replacement over 48h, insulin infusion started only after the first 1–2h of fluids, no insulin bolus, cerebral oedema vigilance) is genuinely consistent with current South Australian and broader Australian practice, not a UK-specific structure needing wholesale replacement. Two genuinely useful SA-specific additions not in the original note: **the corrected sodium formula used to properly interpret sodium trends during treatment — Corrected Na = Measured Na + [0.4 × (glucose − 5.5)] mmol/L** (glucose in mmol/L) — given raw sodium readings are misleading in the hyperglycaemic state and this correction is specifically how SA guidance recommends interpreting them; and **explicit monitoring frequency** — neurological status (GCS) at least hourly (more frequently if any concern, given the cerebral oedema risk below), with bloods (Na, K, Cl, urea, creatinine, calcium, acid-base, blood ketones) 2–4 hourly. SA guidance also specifically recommends **early consultation with a paediatric endocrinologist and the state paediatric retrieval service** given how high-risk and protocol-specific this presentation is — a genuinely SA-specific process point. The exact fluid maintenance rates, potassium replacement thresholds, and other specific numbers below should still be checked against the current local/state protocol directly before clinical use, given these are the kind of figures reasonably updated between guideline versions.

- Severity categorised by degree of acidosis — mild or moderate (pH >7.1), severe (pH <7.1).
- Resuscitation: fluids only if shocked (10mL/kg 0.9% NaCl bolus) — isotonic saline only.
- Rapidly confirm diagnosis: BG, ABG, blood ketones, etc.
- Monitor: GCS (every 30 minutes), ECG (look for hyperkalaemia).
- Start IV fluids: requirement = fluid deficit + maintenance fluids, corrected over 48h. Use lower fluid maintenance rates than APLS.
  - K replacement: if K >5.5, none. If K 3.5–5.5, 40mmol/L. If <3.5, senior review.
- Formal investigations: weight, FBC, U&Es, lab glucose, Ca, infection screen.
- Start IV insulin only after 1–2h of fluids. DO NOT give an insulin bolus — start a fast-acting insulin infusion at 0.1U/kg/h.
- Once BG <14, add glucose to the fluids so the insulin infusion can continue without causing hypoglycaemia.

### DKA resolution

- Defined as pH >7.3 + blood ketones <0.6 + bicarbonate >15mmol/L.
- Should resolve within 24h — if not, senior review needed.
- If resolved and the patient is eating & drinking, switch to SQ insulin — stop the infusion 30–60 minutes after SQ insulin.

### Complications (from DKA or treatment)

- Gastric stasis; thromboembolism; arrhythmias secondary to hyperkalaemia or iatrogenic hypokalaemia.

> [!danger] Cerebral oedema (iatrogenic) — children are especially vulnerable, especially 4–12h after treatment starts. 1:1 nursing + neuro obs essential. If suspected, CT head + senior review.

- ARDS; AKI.
