## Phimosis

- **D:** non-retractable foreskin with associated scarring that will not resolve spontaneously.
- **A:** physiological in boys ≤~4 years old; by age 11, prevalence is <8%. Recurrent balanoposthitis, balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO) — foreskin looks pale, thickened, scarred, with repeated forced retraction.
- **P:** circumferential scarring.
- **S/Smx:** non-retractable foreskin — may present with ballooning during micturition. Retention of urine. Paraphimosis (engorgement). Obstruction.
- **Dx/Ix:** clinical.
- **Mx:**
  - Analgesia.

> [!danger] Paraphimosis is a medical emergency — manipulation with ice packs, compression, osmotic agent; puncture technique; surgical reduction + circumcision.

  - Phimosis: wait-and-see approach; topical corticosteroids (especially before age 8); stretching exercises BD for 15 days, then OD for 15 days.
  - General: personal hygiene.

## Cryptorchidism

- **D:** undescended testis — one or both testes are not present within the dependent portion of the scrotal sac by 3 months. Present in ≤5% of boys at birth, 1–2% at 3 months. May be associated with other defects.
- **A:** incomplete migration of the testis during embryogenesis, from the original retroperitoneal position near the kidneys to the final position in the scrotum.
- **Dx/Ix:** physical exam in the supine position.
- **S/Smx:** malpositioned or absent testis; palpable cryptorchid testis (unable to be pulled into the scrotum, or returns to a higher position after pulling); non-palpable testis; ± testicular asymmetry, scrotal hypoplasia.
- **DDx:** retractile testis (where the testis can be manipulated into the scrotum).
- **Mx:** orchidopexy at 6–18 months — inguinal exploration, mobilisation of the testis, and implantation into a dartos pouch. If presenting late (e.g. teenage years), orchidectomy may be preferred due to increased malignancy risk.

> [!info] Reasons for operating: ↓risk of infertility; allows testes to be examined for testicular cancer (undescended testis is 40× as likely to develop seminoma — the higher the testis is in the abdomen, the higher the risk); avoid testicular torsion; cosmesis.

## Hypospadias

- **D:** the urethra is abnormally located on the ventral surface of the penis. 1 in 350 male births. Associated with cryptorchidism (10%), inguinal hernia.
- **R:** family history (further male children risk 5–15%).
- **Features:** ventral (anterior) urethral meatus; hooded prepuce; chordee (ventral curvature of the penis) in more severe forms; urethral meatus may open more proximally in severe variants (25%).
- **Mx:** corrective surgery at ~12 months.

> [!danger] DO NOT circumcise before surgery, as the foreskin may be needed for the repair.

- In boys with very distal disease, no treatment may be needed.

## Horseshoe kidney

- **D:** fusion defect of the kidneys secondary to abnormal position, rotation, and vascular supply of the kidneys. 1 in 500. 90% U-shaped (lower pole). M>F 2:1. Possibly associated with chromosomal disorders.
- **Features:** usually asymptomatic, identified incidentally. May present with abdominal pain, UTI.
- **Dx/Ix:** US or CT/MRI. CT urogram may help identify stones, blockages, and ureteropelvic junction blocks.
- **DDx:** crossed fusion renal ectopia or fused pelvic kidney.
- **Mx:** symptomatic — regular corrective surgery not recommended.
- **↑risk of cancers** including transitional cell tumours (3–4×), Wilms tumour (2×), carcinoid cancer (62–82×).

## Wilms' tumour (nephroblastoma)

- **D:** the most common childhood renal malignancy; undifferentiated mesodermal tumour of the intermediate cell mass. Presents in children <5 years old.
- Associated with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, WAGR syndrome, hemihypertrophy. 1/3 associated with loss of function mutation in the WT1 gene on chromosome 11.
- **Features:** abdominal mass (most common) — ballotable, does not move on inspiration. Painless haematuria. Flank pain. Others: anorexia, fever. Unilateral in 95% of cases. Metastases found in 20% of patients (most commonly lung).
- **Dx/Ix:** abdominal US, CT/MRI (staging, planning operation).
- **Mx:** urgent (48h) paediatric review for children with unexplained enlarged abdominal mass. Nephrectomy. Chemotherapy. Radiotherapy if advanced disease.
- **P:** good, with 80–90% cure rate. If associated with other genetic conditions, much poorer prognosis.

> [!info] Staging
> 1. Tumour confined to the kidney.
> 2. Extrarenal spread, but resectable.
> 3. Extensive abdominal disease.
> 4. Distant metastases.
> 5. Bilateral disease.

## Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS)

- **D:** microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury. 90% in children, due to Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*.
- **R:** family history, prematurity (15–30%), low birth weight and/or SGA.
- **A:** unknown (sporadic); toxin induces damage to the endothelium of the glomerular capillary bed, causing thrombotic microangiopathy. In familial HUS, dysregulation in the complement cascade triggers atypical HUS.
- **P:** endothelial injury leads to microvascular thrombosis → MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI.
- **Dx/Ix:** FBC (anaemia — Hb <8, Coombs-negative), thrombocytopenia, fragmented blood film (schistocytes and helmet cells), U&Es (AKI), stool culture (looking for STEC infection, PCR for Shiga toxins).
- **Mx:** supportive (fluids, blood transfusion, dialysis).

> [!danger] Antibiotics NOT recommended. Plasma exchange only in severe cases not associated with diarrhoea.

> [!note] The adult/haematology entry is [[10_05_Haemonc_-_Normocytic_Anaemia_and_Sickle_Cell_Disease]] Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS), which also lists the non-STEC causes (pneumococcal infection, HIV, SLE, drugs, cancer) relevant outside childhood.
