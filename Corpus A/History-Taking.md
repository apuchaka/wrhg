# 1 History-Taking (Template A — presentation-led)

This file collects every CSV row that names a symptom/presentation rather than a diagnosis, across all systems, using a consistent structure. Differentials are cross-referenced to the relevant disease-template entries in the system files rather than repeated here — this file is about the *history-taking process*, not the differential's clinical detail.

> [!info] Content status update — this file's individual entries have since received substantial targeted content verification across many subsequent passes (e.g. the AU-specific detail added to Skin Lesion/Rash, Fever, Bruising and Bleeding, Joint Pain, and other entries below), so this blanket "not yet verified" note is now stale for those entries specifically. The general **structure** (SPIKES-style systematic history-taking approach) remains correctly described as not jurisdiction-specific — history-taking technique itself doesn't meaningfully differ between Australian and international curricula, unlike drug doses or referral pathways which do. One honest limitation that remains genuinely unresolved: comparing this file's specific content/emphasis against a formal Flinders or AMC-specific internal OSCE mark scheme isn't achievable via public search, since these institutional assessment rubrics generally aren't published — this is a different kind of gap from "couldn't find it yet," and is realistically something only cross-checkable against actual Flinders course materials the user may have direct access to, not further search-based verification on my end.

## 1.1 Chest Pain

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce yourself, confirm patient's name and DOB, explain you'd like to ask about their chest pain, gain consent, ensure privacy/comfort.

**Presenting complaint (SOCRATES):**
- **S**ite — central, lateral, retrosternal?
- **O**nset — sudden vs gradual; what were they doing when it started?
- **C**haracter — sharp/stabbing, crushing/heavy, tearing, burning?
- **R**adiation — to jaw, arm, back (interscapular — think dissection)?
- **A**ssociated symptoms — see below
- **T**iming — constant vs intermittent; duration of episode
- **E**xacerbating/relieving — exertion, position (worse lying flat/leaning forward), breathing (pleuritic), food, GTN response
- **S**everity — 0–10 scale

**ICE:** ideas, concerns (many patients fear "heart attack" specifically — ask directly), expectations for the consultation.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** dyspnoea, palpitations, sweating, nausea/vomiting, syncope/pre-syncope, cough, fever, leg swelling/pain (DVT), recent immobility/travel/surgery, haemoptysis.

> [!danger] Red flags for chest pain
> Sudden tearing pain radiating to the back (dissection); crushing pain with diaphoresis/nausea (ACS); sudden pleuritic pain with dyspnoea, recent immobility/surgery/malignancy (PE); sudden pleuritic pain in a young thin patient (pneumothorax); pain with haemodynamic instability, muffled heart sounds, raised JVP (tamponade); fever with pleuritic pain and productive cough (pneumonia).

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** prior MI/angina/PCI/CABG, HTN, dyslipidaemia, DM, prior VTE, malignancy, connective tissue disease (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos — dissection risk)
- **DHx/allergies:** antiplatelets/anticoagulants, recent PDE5 inhibitor use (relevant to GTN), oral contraceptive/HRT (VTE risk)
- **FHx:** premature CAD, sudden cardiac death, Marfan/connective tissue disease, VTE
- **SHx:** smoking, alcohol, occupation (asbestos exposure for pleuritic causes), recent long-haul travel/immobility, illicit drug use (cocaine — ACS risk)

**Differential this history narrows down:** cardiac (ACS/[[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.1, stable angina, pericarditis, aortic dissection) vs respiratory (PE, pneumothorax, pneumonia) vs GI (reflux, oesophageal spasm) vs musculoskeletal (costochondritis) vs psychogenic. Cross-reference the relevant disease sections for full detail rather than repeating here.

**Closing:** Summarise back what you've heard, check you've understood correctly, explain likely next steps (examination, ECG, bloods), safety-net (advise re-presentation if pain worsens or new red-flag symptoms develop).

## 1.2 Collapse (Conscious and Unconscious)

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent. If the patient didn't witness their own collapse, actively seek a collateral/witness history — this is often more valuable than the patient's own account.

**Presenting complaint structure (not classic SOCRATES — use a structured collapse-specific approach):**
- **Before:** what were they doing (exertion, standing, micturition, coughing, emotional trigger)? Any warning/prodrome (light-headedness, palpitations, chest pain, aura, visual disturbance, sweating, nausea)?
- **During:** duration of loss of consciousness; any witnessed jerking movements (and timing relative to the fall — brief myoclonic jerks can occur in simple syncope too, not just seizure); tongue-biting (lateral suggests seizure); incontinence; colour change (pale vs cyanosed).
- **After:** how quickly did they recover — instant (favours cardiac/vasovagal syncope) vs prolonged confusion/post-ictal drowsiness (favours seizure)? Any injury from the fall?

**ICE:** many patients are frightened about driving implications or an underlying serious diagnosis — ask directly.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** chest pain, palpitations, dyspnoea (cardiac); headache, focal neurology, aura (neurological); fever, infection symptoms; recent illness/dehydration/prolonged standing (vasovagal).

> [!danger] Red flags for collapse
> Collapse during exertion (structural cardiac cause, e.g. aortic stenosis, HOCM); collapse with chest pain or palpitations beforehand; no warning/prodrome at all (arrhythmic cause); family history of sudden cardiac death <40yo (inherited arrhythmia syndrome — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.16 Long QT Syndrome, 0.17 Short QT Syndrome and 0.18 Brugada Syndrome); focal neurological signs post-episode; injury from the fall suggesting no warning.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** known cardiac disease (arrhythmia, structural heart disease, prior MI), epilepsy, diabetes (hypoglycaemia), psychiatric history
- **DHx/allergies:** antihypertensives (postural hypotension), QT-prolonging drugs (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Long QT Syndrome), insulin/sulfonylureas, recreational drugs/alcohol
- **FHx:** sudden cardiac death <40yo, epilepsy, inherited arrhythmia syndromes
- **SHx:** occupation (driving/machinery-operating implications — see driving standards in [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.34.5), alcohol, driving history

**Differential this history narrows down:** cardiac syncope (arrhythmia, structural — aortic stenosis, HOCM), vasovagal/reflex syncope, orthostatic hypotension, seizure, hypoglycaemia, psychogenic non-epileptic episode. Cross-reference [[01_Cardiovascular]] for cardiac causes and [[04_Neurology]] Syncope and Seizures and Epilepsy for the neurological workup rather than repeating here.

**Closing:** Summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination incl. lying/standing BP, ECG, bloods incl. glucose), discuss driving restrictions if relevant (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.34.5 for the Austroads cardiovascular driving table, or [[04_Neurology]] Austroads Driving Standards for the seizure/epilepsy-specific standards if a seizure cause is suspected), safety-net.

## 1.3 Palpitations

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Ask the patient to describe the sensation in their own words first, then — if helpful — ask them to tap out the rhythm they felt on the desk/their hand (surprisingly useful for distinguishing regular vs irregular, and fast vs slow).
- Onset — sudden ("like a switch") vs gradual (favours sinus tachycardia over a paroxysmal arrhythmia)
- Duration of episodes — seconds, minutes, hours, or ongoing at time of consultation
- Regularity — regular vs irregular (irregular favours AF/ectopics)
- Rate — fast vs slow, if the patient can estimate
- Frequency — first episode vs recurrent, and if recurrent, how often
- Triggers — exertion, caffeine, alcohol, stress, standing up, specific posture, certain foods
- Termination — spontaneous, or did a specific manoeuvre stop it (e.g. Valsalva — suggestive of SVT, see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.9.1)

**ICE:** many patients fear a serious cardiac cause specifically — ask directly what's worrying them.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** chest pain, dyspnoea, dizziness/pre-syncope/syncope (a key red flag — see below), sweating, anxiety symptoms, weight loss/heat intolerance/tremor (thyrotoxicosis), symptoms of anaemia.

> [!danger] Red flags for palpitations
> Associated syncope or near-syncope; palpitations during exertion; chest pain accompanying the palpitations; family history of sudden cardiac death <40yo or known inherited arrhythmia syndrome; known structural heart disease (increases risk that an arrhythmia is haemodynamically significant).

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** known cardiac disease, thyroid disease, anxiety/panic disorder, prior arrhythmia diagnosis
- **DHx/allergies:** stimulants (caffeine, decongestants, illicit stimulants), thyroxine, QT-prolonging drugs, β-agonist inhalers
- **FHx:** sudden cardiac death <40yo, inherited arrhythmia syndromes, thyroid disease
- **SHx:** caffeine/alcohol/recreational drug use, smoking, stress levels/sleep

**Differential this history narrows down:** sinus tachycardia (anxiety, anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, fever, dehydration), AF/flutter, SVT, ventricular ectopics/PACs (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.40), more concerning ventricular arrhythmias in the context of structural heart disease or red flags above, panic/anxiety-related. Cross-reference the relevant [[01_Cardiovascular]] sections for the disease-level detail on each rather than repeating here.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (ECG, Holter monitoring if intermittent, bloods incl. TFT/FBC), safety-net (advise urgent review if syncope, chest pain, or worsening occurs).

## 1.6 Cough

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Duration — acute (<3 weeks), subacute (3–8 weeks), or chronic (>8 weeks) — this single distinction drives most of the differential and Ix approach.
- Character — dry vs productive; if productive, ask about sputum colour, volume, and any blood (see Haemoptysis below).
- Timing/pattern — worse at night (asthma, GORD, heart failure/PND), worse on waking (COPD/bronchiectasis "clearing" cough), seasonal/triggered (allergic asthma).
- Associated triggers — exertion, cold air, allergens, lying flat, eating/drinking (aspiration risk — see [[02_Respiratory]] Aspiration Pneumonia and Pneumonitis).

**ICE:** many patients are worried about lung cancer specifically, especially with a smoking history or chronic cough — ask directly.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** fever, weight loss, night sweats, haemoptysis, dyspnoea, chest pain, hoarseness, reflux symptoms, post-nasal drip symptoms.

> [!danger] Red flags for cough
> Haemoptysis; weight loss; night sweats; chronic cough (>3 weeks) in a smoker or ex-smoker (consider lung cancer/TB — see [[02_Respiratory]] Lung Cancers, Tuberculosis); hoarseness alongside cough (consider malignancy, recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement — see [[13_06a_ENT_-_Dysphonia_and_HNSCC]] Dysphonia (hoarseness) for why hoarseness >6 weeks specifically warrants investigation, not repeated here); stridor (upper airway emergency); significant dyspnoea or chest pain accompanying the cough.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** asthma, COPD, GORD, known malignancy, immunocompromise, prior TB
- **DHx/allergies:** ACE inhibitors (a classic drug cause of chronic dry cough — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.34.1), other new medications
- **FHx:** lung cancer, TB contact, atopy
- **SHx:** smoking (pack-years), occupational exposures, TB risk factors (travel/birth in endemic country, contact with active TB), pet/allergen exposure. For the occupational exposures themselves see [[02_Respiratory]] Pneumoconioses (Occupational Lung Disease — brief overview)

**Differential this history narrows down:** acute — URTI/acute bronchitis (see [[02_Respiratory]]), pneumonia, asthma/COPD exacerbation, PE (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.29); chronic — asthma, COPD, GORD, post-nasal drip, ACE inhibitor, bronchiectasis, lung cancer, TB, IPF (see the relevant [[02_Respiratory]] sections for each rather than repeating here).

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination, CXR if red flags/chronic, spirometry if obstructive picture suspected), safety-net.

## 1.7 Haemoptysis

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent — this symptom is often frightening for patients, so acknowledge that directly.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Confirm it's genuinely haemoptysis (from the lungs/airway) rather than haematemesis (vomited, GI source) or blood from the nose/mouth/gums (upper airway source) — ask about the colour/character (bright red, frothy, mixed with sputum favours a respiratory source) and whether it was coughed vs vomited.
- Volume — streaking/blood-tinged sputum vs frank blood vs massive haemoptysis (a specific emergency, generally defined as a large volume over a short period, e.g. >100–200mL in 24h, though exact cut-offs vary) — massive haemoptysis is immediately life-threatening from airway flooding/asphyxiation, not primarily from blood loss.
- Duration/frequency — single episode vs recurrent.

**ICE:** as with chronic cough, many patients fear lung cancer specifically — address this directly.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** weight loss, night sweats, fever, chest pain, dyspnoea, symptoms of DVT (calf swelling/pain — relevant to PE as a cause), symptoms of a bleeding disorder/easy bruising.

> [!danger] Red flags for haemoptysis
> Massive haemoptysis (airway emergency); haemoptysis with weight loss/night sweats/smoking history (malignancy, TB); haemoptysis with pleuritic chest pain and risk factors for VTE (PE — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.29); haemoptysis in a patient on anticoagulation with an unexpectedly high INR/supratherapeutic anticoagulant level.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** known malignancy, bronchiectasis, TB (past or current), cardiac disease (mitral stenosis is a classic though less common cause), recent chest trauma/procedure
- **DHx/allergies:** anticoagulants/antiplatelets, recent anticoagulant dose changes
- **FHx:** lung cancer, bleeding disorders
- **SHx:** smoking (pack-years), TB risk factors, occupational exposures

**Differential this history narrows down:** infective (bronchiectasis, TB, pneumonia, lung abscess — see relevant [[02_Respiratory]] sections), malignant (lung cancer — see [[02_Respiratory]] Lung Cancers), vascular (PE — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.29), cardiac (mitral stenosis — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.21), coagulopathy/anticoagulation-related, and — a common cause worth normalising for anxious patients — a benign, self-limiting cause such as a vigorous coughing fit with minor airway trauma.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination, CXR ± CT depending on risk factors, bronchoscopy if indicated), safety-net for worsening/massive haemoptysis (urgent presentation).

## 1.8 Shortness of Breath (Dyspnoea)

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Onset — acute (minutes-hours: PE, pneumothorax, acute asthma/anaphylaxis, acute pulmonary oedema) vs chronic/progressive (COPD, IPF, chronic HF, anaemia).
- Timing/pattern — orthopnoea (number of pillows), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (both suggest cardiac causes — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Chronic Heart Failure), exertional vs at-rest, positional variation.
- Severity — quantify functional impact using a scale the patient can relate to (e.g. "how far can you walk before you need to stop" — maps to the MRC Dyspnoea Scale, see [[02_Respiratory]] COPD).
- Associated triggers — exertion, allergen exposure, cold air, position.

**ICE:** breathlessness is often frightening and can trigger significant anxiety, which itself can worsen the sensation of dyspnoea (a bidirectional relationship worth acknowledging) — ask what the patient is most worried it might be.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** chest pain, cough (± haemoptysis), wheeze, palpitations, leg swelling, fever, weight loss/night sweats, symptoms of anaemia (fatigue, pallor).

> [!danger] Red flags for dyspnoea
> Sudden onset (PE, pneumothorax, anaphylaxis); accompanying chest pain; stridor (upper airway emergency); inability to speak in full sentences/accessory muscle use (severe respiratory distress — see [[02_Respiratory]] Asthma acute exacerbation severity grading); haemoptysis; new significant leg swelling (DVT/PE risk); orthopnoea/PND (decompensated heart failure).

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** asthma, COPD, cardiac disease, anaemia, prior VTE, anxiety/panic disorder
- **DHx/allergies:** β-blockers (can worsen reactive airway disease), new medications, recreational drugs
- **FHx:** cardiac disease, VTE, atopy
- **SHx:** smoking, occupational exposures, exercise tolerance at baseline, recent immobility/travel (VTE risk)

**Differential this history narrows down:** respiratory (asthma, COPD, pneumonia, PE, pneumothorax — see [[02_Respiratory]] and [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.29), cardiac (heart failure, ACS, arrhythmia — see [[01_Cardiovascular]]), other (anaemia, anxiety/panic, metabolic acidosis, obesity/deconditioning). Cross-reference the relevant disease sections for full detail.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination, O2 sats, CXR, ECG, bloods incl. FBC/BNP as relevant), safety-net.

## 1.10 Abdominal Pain

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent, ensure privacy/comfort.

**Presenting complaint (SOCRATES):**
- **S**ite — use the regional anatomy to guide the differential (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Abdominal Pain — Regional Anatomy and DDx for the full anatomical-region-to-differential mapping and system-based DDx list, not repeated here)
- **O**nset — sudden (perforation, torsion, rupture, ischaemia) vs gradual (inflammatory, obstructive)
- **C**haracter — colicky/cramping (obstruction — biliary, ureteric, bowel) vs constant/sharp (peritonitic — inflammation, perforation) vs tearing (vascular — dissection, ruptured AAA)
- **R**adiation — e.g. to the back (pancreatitis, AAA, renal colic), to the shoulder tip (diaphragmatic irritation — ruptured ectopic, splenic pathology, subphrenic collection), to the groin (renal/ureteric colic)
- **A**ssociated symptoms — see below
- **T**iming — constant vs intermittent (colic implies intermittent smooth-muscle spasm); relation to meals (worse after eating — biliary, gastric; relieved by eating — some duodenal ulcers)
- **E**xacerbating/relieving — movement/coughing (peritonism — worse; patients with peritonitis lie still, unlike the restlessness of colic), position (pancreatitis relieved by sitting forward), eating
- **S**everity — 0–10 scale, and specifically whether the pain is "the worst pain of their life" (raises concern for a vascular catastrophe — dissection, ruptured AAA, mesenteric ischaemia)

**ICE:** ask directly what the patient is most worried it might be — abdominal pain often triggers fear of cancer or a surgical emergency.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** nausea/vomiting (and its character — bilious/faeculent suggests obstruction), change in bowel habit, PR bleeding/melaena, jaundice, fever, urinary symptoms, and in women of reproductive age — last menstrual period, pregnancy status, gynaecological symptoms (essential given the overlap between gynaecological and GI causes of lower abdominal pain).

> [!danger] Red flags for abdominal pain (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Abdominal Pain — Regional Anatomy and DDx for the full list)
> Severe pain, signs of shock, peritoneal signs (rebound tenderness, guarding, reduced bowel sounds), abdominal distension, blood in stool/urine, anorexia/unexplained weight loss, abdominal mass/organomegaly, fever, jaundice, pain that wakes the patient at night.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** previous abdominal surgery (adhesion risk), known gallstones/PUD/IBD/diverticular disease, cardiovascular disease (mesenteric ischaemia, AAA risk)
- **DHx/allergies:** NSAIDs/steroids (PUD, GI bleed risk), anticoagulants (bleeding risk, retroperitoneal haemorrhage), opioids (constipation, masking symptoms)
- **FHx:** IBD, coeliac disease, bowel/ovarian cancer, AAA
- **SHx:** alcohol (pancreatitis, liver disease), smoking, diet

**Differential this history narrows down:** cross-reference [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Abdominal Pain — Regional Anatomy and DDx for the full regional/systems-based differential, and the individual disease sections throughout [[03_Gastrointestinal]] and [[01_Cardiovascular]] (vascular causes) for detailed disease-level content — not repeated here.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination, bloods, urinalysis, imaging as indicated by the differential), safety-net.

## 1.11 Nausea and Vomiting

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Onset and duration — acute (infective, obstructive, drug-related) vs chronic (structural, metabolic, functional)
- Character of vomit — bilious/green (obstruction distal to the pylorus), faeculent (late/distal bowel obstruction), blood-stained/coffee-ground (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Upper GI Bleed (UGIB) Bleed), undigested food from hours earlier (gastric outlet obstruction/gastroparesis)
- Timing relative to meals and relation to pain
- Associated relief — does vomiting relieve abdominal pain (suggests obstruction) or headache (suggests raised ICP)?

**ICE:** ask what the patient is most concerned about — persistent vomiting is often frightening, particularly if associated with weight loss.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** abdominal pain, fever, diarrhoea/constipation, headache/neurological symptoms (raised ICP as a cause), vertigo (vestibular cause), pregnancy status in women of reproductive age.

> [!danger] Red flags: haematemesis, signs of bowel obstruction, signs of raised ICP (headache, visual disturbance, neurological signs), significant dehydration, weight loss, PR bleeding/melaena.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** known GI disease, diabetes (gastroparesis), migraine, previous abdominal surgery
- **DHx/allergies:** chemotherapy, opioids, digoxin toxicity, new medications
- **FHx:** relevant GI conditions
- **SHx:** alcohol, pregnancy status/LMP

**Differential this history narrows down:** GI (obstruction, gastroenteritis, PUD — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]]), metabolic (DKA, uraemia, hypercalcaemia), neurological (raised ICP, vestibular), drug-induced, pregnancy-related. Cross-reference the relevant disease sections rather than repeating here.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination, bloods, imaging as indicated), safety-net for dehydration/red flags.

## 1.12 Constipation

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Establish the patient's own definition first — "constipation" means different things to different people (reduced frequency, hard stool, straining, incomplete evacuation sensation)
- Onset — acute change (concerning if new in an older patient — see red flags) vs longstanding/lifelong pattern
- Stool characteristics — use the Bristol Stool Chart if helpful for the patient to describe
- Associated straining, incomplete evacuation, need for digital assistance

**ICE:** many patients are specifically worried about bowel cancer — ask directly, especially if there's been a recent change.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** PR bleeding, weight loss, abdominal pain/distension, overflow diarrhoea (can mimic the opposite problem), urinary symptoms (pelvic floor dysfunction overlap).

> [!danger] Red flags: new constipation in an older patient (especially with weight loss or PR bleeding — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Colorectal Cancer), family history of bowel/ovarian cancer, palpable abdominal/rectal mass, iron-deficiency anaemia.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** hypothyroidism, diabetes, neurological disease (Parkinson's, spinal cord pathology), previous pelvic surgery
- **DHx/allergies:** opioids, anticholinergics, iron supplements, calcium channel blockers
- **FHx:** bowel/ovarian cancer
- **SHx:** diet (fibre/fluid intake), activity level, mobility

**Differential this history narrows down:** functional/lifestyle (most common), obstructive (colorectal cancer, stricture — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Colorectal Cancer, Diverticular Disease), metabolic (hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia), neurological, drug-induced, IBS-constipation-predominant (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) Syndrome).

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination incl. DRE where appropriate, bloods, colonoscopy if red flags present), safety-net.

## 1.14 Diarrhoea (Acute and Chronic, incl. Infective/Traveller's)

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Duration — acute (<2 weeks, usually infective), persistent (2–4 weeks), or chronic (>4 weeks) — this drives the differential more than almost any other single feature
- Stool characteristics — frequency, consistency (Bristol Stool Chart if helpful), blood or mucus, steatorrhoea features (pale, greasy, hard to flush — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Malabsorption)
- Pattern — nocturnal diarrhoea (suggests organic rather than functional cause), relation to eating, watery vs bloody
- Associated pain, tenesmus, urgency

**ICE:** ask what's worrying the patient most — persistent diarrhoea often raises fear of cancer or a serious chronic diagnosis.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** fever (infective, IBD), weight loss (malignancy, IBD, coeliac, hyperthyroidism), blood/mucus in stool (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Lower GI Bleed (LGIB), Inflammatory Bowel Disease), abdominal pain, extra-intestinal features if IBD suspected (joint pain, eye symptoms, skin changes — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Inflammatory Bowel Disease extra-intestinal features table).

> [!danger] Red flags: PR bleeding, unintentional weight loss, nocturnal symptoms, family history of bowel/ovarian cancer or IBD, onset >60yo, anaemia, systemic illness (fever, dehydration, tachycardia).

**Specific exposure history for acute/infective diarrhoea:**
- Recent travel (destination, duration, food/water sources — "traveller's diarrhoea," typically *E. coli*, occurring within the first 1–2 weeks of travel to a resource-limited destination)
- Food history — recent takeaway/restaurant meals, undercooked meat/eggs, shellfish, unpasteurised dairy
- Sick contacts / others affected (suggests a common-source outbreak or person-to-person spread, e.g. norovirus)
- Recent antibiotic use (*C. difficile* risk)
- Hospital/institutional exposure
- Animal/farm contact (zoonotic pathogens)
- Immunocompromise (broader/opportunistic differential)

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** known IBD, coeliac disease, previous GI surgery, thyroid disease, diabetes (autonomic neuropathy)
- **DHx/allergies:** recent antibiotics, laxatives, metformin, PPIs (small increased infective diarrhoea risk), NSAIDs (microscopic colitis)
- **FHx:** IBD, coeliac disease, bowel/ovarian cancer
- **SHx:** travel, occupation (food handler — public health relevance), alcohol

**Differential this history narrows down:** acute/infective — viral (norovirus, rotavirus), bacterial (*Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *E. coli*, *Shigella*), traveller's diarrhoea, *C. difficile* (antibiotic-associated); chronic — IBS (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) Syndrome), IBD (see Inflammatory Bowel Disease), coeliac disease (see Coeliac Disease), malabsorption (see Malabsorption), colorectal cancer (see Colorectal Cancer), thyrotoxicosis, microscopic colitis. Cross-reference the relevant [[03_Gastrointestinal]] sections for full disease-level detail rather than repeating here.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (stool culture/microscopy if infective suspected, faecal calprotectin/coeliac serology if chronic, examination), safety-net for dehydration and red flags; public health notification advice if a notifiable infective cause and/or the patient is a food handler/healthcare worker.

## 1.16 Weakness (Motor)

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Onset — sudden (vascular: stroke, spinal cord infarct), rapid over hours-days (GBS, myasthenic crisis, cord compression), subacute over weeks (inflammatory, some myopathies), chronic/insidious (MND, muscular dystrophy, most neurodegenerative causes) — onset tempo is often the single most useful discriminator in this presentation, more so than the pattern of weakness itself
- Distribution — focal/unilateral (stroke, space-occupying lesion, mononeuropathy) vs symmetrical/bilateral (GBS, myopathy, myasthenia) vs ascending (GBS) vs proximal-predominant (myopathy, e.g. difficulty rising from a chair or raising arms overhead) vs distal-predominant (peripheral neuropathy, e.g. difficulty with buttons, foot drop)
- Associated sensory symptoms — present (suggests a nerve root/peripheral nerve/cord lesion) vs absent (suggests a pure motor neuron, NMJ, or myopathic process) — see [[04_Neurology]] Weakness — Differential Diagnosis for the full structured DDx this distinction feeds into, not repeated here
- Fatiguability — worse with sustained/repeated activity and improving with rest (myasthenia gravis) vs constant (most other causes) vs the reverse pattern, improving with repeated use (Lambert-Eaton syndrome)
- Bulbar symptoms — dysarthria, dysphagia, diplopia (suggests brainstem, NMJ, or bulbar-onset MND involvement — ask specifically, since patients may not volunteer these as "weakness")
- Bladder/bowel involvement — a red flag for cord-level pathology (see [[04_Neurology]] Cauda Equina Syndrome and Malignant Spinal Cord Compression — ask about this explicitly and urgently if suspected, given the time-critical nature of these diagnoses)

**ICE:** ask directly what the patient is most worried about — sudden weakness often triggers fear of stroke or a life-changing diagnosis (MND, MS); acknowledge this directly rather than only focusing on the physical history.

> [!danger] Red flags: sudden onset (stroke until proven otherwise), bilateral leg weakness + saddle anaesthesia + bladder/bowel dysfunction (cauda equina), back pain + bilateral leg weakness in a patient with known malignancy (MSCC), respiratory muscle involvement/reduced FVC (GBS, myasthenic crisis — both can progress to respiratory failure), rapidly ascending weakness.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** known malignancy (MSCC), diabetes (mononeuropathy, peripheral neuropathy), autoimmune disease, thyroid disease
- **DHx/allergies:** statins (myopathy), steroids (myopathy), recent vaccination or infection (GBS — see [[04_Neurology]] Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) for the ~6-week preceding-infection window), drugs worsening myasthenia (see [[04_Neurology]] Myasthenia Gravis for the specific list)
- **FHx:** muscular dystrophy, Charcot-Marie-Tooth, other hereditary neuromuscular conditions
- **SHx:** alcohol (neuropathy, myopathy), occupation/driving implications if diagnosis confirmed

**Differential this history narrows down:** cross-reference [[04_Neurology]] Weakness — Differential Diagnosis for the full structured approach (UMN vs LMN vs NMJ vs myopathic, with vs without sensory involvement) — not repeated here.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (neurological examination, relevant bloods/imaging/neurophysiology per the suspected level), safety-net specifically for red flags above given several causes are genuine emergencies.

## 1.17 Confusion, Disorientation, and Altered Conscious State

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent. As with Collapse above, actively seek collateral history from family/carers/other staff — the patient themselves may not be a reliable historian, and collateral information about baseline cognitive function is often the single most useful piece of history in this presentation.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- Establish the baseline — what is this person normally like cognitively? A new deficit against a normal baseline points toward delirium; a gradual decline over months-years against a progressively lower baseline points toward dementia — this distinction fundamentally changes the rest of the work-up (see [[04_Neurology]] Delirium and Dementias for the full disease-level content this history feeds into)
- Onset and course — acute/hours-days and fluctuating (classic delirium) vs insidious over months-years (dementia) vs episodic with full return to baseline between episodes (e.g. postictal confusion, hypoglycaemia, TIA)
- Associated symptoms — fever/infective symptoms, focal neurological deficit, headache, recent falls/head injury, recent medication changes, alcohol/substance use or withdrawal

**ICE:** family members are often distressed and may fear this represents irreversible decline — where the picture is more consistent with delirium (a potentially reversible, precipitant-driven process), this distinction is worth explaining clearly and can be genuinely reassuring, without overpromising a specific outcome before the work-up is complete.

**Associated/systemic symptoms to screen for:** urinary symptoms (UTI as a common delirium precipitant, especially in the elderly), respiratory symptoms, constipation, pain (undertreated pain is a common and under-recognised delirium precipitant), recent surgery/hospitalisation, sleep disturbance.

> [!danger] Red flags: focal neurological deficit (stroke, mass lesion, encephalitis), fever + neck stiffness (meningitis), reduced GCS/rapidly declining consciousness, head injury, signs of raised ICP.

**Relevant Hx prompts:**
- **PMHx:** known dementia or prior cognitive impairment (changes interpretation entirely — see above), diabetes (hypo/hyperglycaemia), epilepsy, previous delirium episodes (a risk factor for recurrence)
- **DHx/allergies:** any new medication, opioids/benzodiazepines/anticholinergics (common precipitants), recent medication cessation (withdrawal, including alcohol — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ArLD) → Alcohol withdrawal)
- **FHx:** dementia (relevant to baseline risk, though not directly diagnostic for an acute presentation)
- **SHx:** alcohol use and recent pattern/cessation, living situation and level of normal support (relevant to both precipitant identification and safe discharge planning)

**Differential this history narrows down:** delirium (see [[04_Neurology]] Delirium for the full precipitant list and Mx), dementia subtypes (see [[04_Neurology]] Dementias), stroke/TIA, seizure (postictal state), metabolic derangement (hypoglycaemia, hepatic/uraemic encephalopathy — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Hepatic encephalopathy), CNS infection (see [[04_Neurology]] CNS Infections), intoxication/withdrawal. Cross-reference the relevant disease sections rather than repeating differential detail here.

**Closing:** summarise back to the patient/family as appropriate, check understanding, explain next steps (cognitive testing, bloods, examination, imaging if focal signs/red flags present), safety-net; address safety planning (falls risk, ability to return home safely) as part of the closing discussion given how directly this affects discharge planning.

## 1.18 Speech Disorder (Dysphasia/Aphasia vs Dysarthria)

> [!note] This is predominantly an examination/classification distinction rather than a history-taking template in the SOCRATES sense — the key skill is distinguishing dysphasia (a language problem) from dysarthria (a speech articulation problem) at the bedside, then localising further. The detailed aphasia subtype table (Wernicke's, Broca's, conduction, global — with site, blood supply, comprehension, fluency, and repetition for each) is already built in [[04_Neurology]] Aphasia — not repeated here.

**Key bedside distinction:**
- **Dysphasia/aphasia** — a disorder of language itself (comprehension and/or production of meaningful speech/writing) — the words are hard to find or don't make sense, but articulation of individual sounds is normal. Localises to a dominant-hemisphere lesion (almost always the left hemisphere, even in most left-handed people) — see [[04_Neurology]] Aphasia table and Brain Lesion Localisation for the full localising detail.
- **Dysarthria** — a disorder of the mechanical production of speech (articulation) — language content and comprehension are normal, but the speech sounds slurred/imprecise. Can arise from a cranial nerve/brainstem lesion (e.g. stroke, MND — see [[04_Neurology]]), cerebellar dysfunction (part of the DANISH cerebellar sign cluster — see Brain Lesion Localisation), neuromuscular junction disease (myasthenia gravis — fatiguable dysarthria worsening through a conversation), or a local mechanical cause (e.g. Bell's palsy affecting articulation — see [[04_Neurology]] Bell's Palsy).

**History prompts specific to this presentation:** onset tempo (sudden — stroke; fluctuating/fatiguable — myasthenia; progressive — MND, dementia-associated language decline); associated symptoms (facial weakness, limb weakness, swallowing difficulty, cognitive change); whether the patient's own insight into the problem is intact (patients with Wernicke's aphasia characteristically lack insight into their own nonsensical speech, unlike conduction aphasia where insight is preserved — see the Aphasia table for this distinction).

> [!danger] Sudden-onset speech disturbance of either type should be treated as a possible stroke until proven otherwise — see [[04_Neurology]] Strokes for the acute pathway.

**Closing:** summarise back (adapting communication style if the patient has expressive difficulty — allow extra time, use closed/yes-no questions where helpful, involve family for collateral understanding of what the patient is trying to convey), explain next steps, safety-net.

## 1.19 Red Eye / Painful Eye

> [!note] The disease-level differential is already built as a structured DDx table in [[05_Ophthalmology]] The Red Eye — Regional Approach and DDx (organised by pain and vision-affected status) — not repeated here. This entry covers the history-taking-specific questions that populate that DDx, since "what does the history need to establish" is genuinely different content from the DDx table itself.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure — the two triage questions that matter most:**
- **Pain or no pain?** — and if pain, characterise it: gritty/foreign-body sensation (suggests a surface problem — conjunctivitis, corneal abrasion, foreign body, dry eye) vs deep, boring ache (suggests scleritis, or acute angle-closure glaucoma if accompanied by headache/nausea) vs sharp and severe (keratitis, acute angle-closure glaucoma, anterior uveitis).
- **Vision affected or preserved?** — reduced vision alongside a red eye is a red flag pointing toward the more urgent causes (see [[05_Ophthalmology]] The Red Eye — Regional Approach and DDx) and should prompt more urgent assessment regardless of what else the history reveals.

**Further history prompts:**
- **Onset and laterality:** sudden vs gradual; unilateral (more typical of trauma, foreign body, unilateral infective conjunctivitis, acute angle-closure glaucoma, uveitis) vs bilateral (more typical of allergic or viral conjunctivitis, blepharitis, though this is a tendency rather than an absolute rule)
- **Discharge:** purulent/sticky (bacterial conjunctivitis) vs watery/serous (viral conjunctivitis, allergic) vs none (most other causes) — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Conjunctivitis for the fuller S/Smx detail
- **Itch vs pain:** itch strongly favours allergic conjunctivitis over infective or more serious causes — a useful, simple discriminator
- **Photophobia:** a red flag pointing toward keratitis, anterior uveitis, or acute angle-closure glaucoma rather than simple conjunctivitis
- **Contact lens use:** significantly raises concern for microbial keratitis, including more severe organisms (*Pseudomonas*, acanthamoeba) — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Keratitis; ask specifically about lens hygiene and whether lenses were worn overnight/while swimming
- **Trauma or foreign body sensation:** raises concern for corneal abrasion or a retained foreign body — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Corneal Abrasion
- **Recent eye surgery:** raises concern for endophthalmitis, a genuine emergency — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Endophthalmitis; ask specifically given how easily this history detail could otherwise be missed if not asked directly
- **Systemic symptoms/PMH:** headache, nausea/vomiting (acute angle-closure glaucoma); joint pain, back pain, bowel symptoms (HLA-B27-associated conditions, IBD — relevant to anterior uveitis, see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Anterior Uveitis); known Graves'/thyroid disease (thyroid eye disease)

**ICE:** patients are often anxious about permanent vision loss — this is a reasonable concern to acknowledge directly, and for most causes of a red eye (the painless, vision-preserved category) genuine reassurance is appropriate once red flags have been excluded.

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent same-day ophthalmology review: reduced visual acuity, severe pain, photophobia, a fixed/irregular/poorly-reactive pupil, corneal opacity, hypopyon, recent intraocular surgery, contact lens wear with significant pain, and any history of significant trauma — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] The Red Eye — Regional Approach and DDx for the fuller red flag list.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (visual acuity testing, slit-lamp/fluorescein examination as indicated, urgent referral if red flags present), safety-net for worsening symptoms.

## 1.20 Weight Change (Gain and Loss)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Weight change," Endocrine & Metabolic category, High yield) — a presentation-led topic without a dedicated history-taking template anywhere in the project files, despite the relevant disease-level differentials (hyper-/hypothyroidism, T1DM/T2DM, Cushing's/Addison's, malignancy) all being built in [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]]. This entry covers the history-taking approach; cross-references the disease sections for differential detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure — establish first whether this is genuinely unintentional:**
- **Quantify:** how much weight, over what time period — as a rough guide, >5% of body weight lost unintentionally over 6–12 months is a widely used threshold for "clinically significant" weight loss warranting active work-up, though any unexplained, unintentional weight change deserves attention regardless of whether it crosses a specific numeric threshold.
- **Intentional or unintentional:** has the patient been actively trying to lose/gain weight (diet, exercise change), or has this happened without a clear reason? Unintentional change is the more concerning pattern and drives most of the differential below.
- **Associated appetite change:** weight loss with increased appetite (hyperthyroidism, T1DM/T2DM — polyphagia despite catabolism) points differently than weight loss with reduced appetite (malignancy, depression, chronic disease, malabsorption); weight gain with increased appetite (hypothyroidism can cause both increased and unchanged appetite depending on presentation) vs weight gain without clear appetite change (fluid retention — heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, Cushing's) also narrows the differential.

**Further history prompts:**
- **Systemic/red flag symptoms:** fevers, night sweats, fatigue, lymphadenopathy (malignancy/infection screen); bowel habit change, rectal bleeding (GI malignancy); new lumps or masses
- **Endocrine screen:** heat/cold intolerance, tremor, palpitations, sweating (hyperthyroidism — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Hyperthyroidism); cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair thinning (hypothyroidism — see Hypothyroidism); polyuria, polydipsia (diabetes — see Diabetes Mellitus); proximal muscle weakness, easy bruising, central weight gain with striae (Cushing's — see Cushing's Syndrome/Disease); salt craving, postural dizziness, hyperpigmentation (Addison's — see Addison's Disease)
- **GI screen:** diarrhoea/malabsorption symptoms, dysphagia (see [[13_06b_ENT_-_Dysphagia_and_Oesophageal_Pathology]] Dysphagia — approach for the dedicated screening questions and differential, not repeated here), abdominal pain — relevant to both malabsorptive weight loss and the GI malignancy differential (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] for the relevant disease sections)
- **Psychiatric/social screen:** mood, appetite in the context of mood, disordered eating patterns, social circumstances affecting food access/intake, alcohol/substance use — genuinely important given how commonly psychosocial factors drive unintentional weight change, and easy to under-ask if the consultation focuses only on organic disease
- **Medication review:** many drugs cause weight change as a side effect (e.g. steroids, some antipsychotics, and some diabetes medications for gain; stimulants, SSRIs variably, and metformin/GLP-1 agonists/SGLT2i for loss — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Drugs for T2DM for the specific weight-loss-associated diabetes medications) — a medication change coinciding with the weight change timeline is a useful clue.
- **Fluid-specific screen for weight gain:** ankle swelling, orthopnoea, PND (heart failure — see [[01_Cardiovascular]]), frothy urine (nephrotic syndrome) — distinguishes true tissue weight gain from fluid accumulation, which can present as rapid "weight gain" with a very different differential and urgency.

**ICE:** weight change — especially loss — is a common trigger for patient anxiety about malignancy; this concern is often present even when not voiced directly, and is worth acknowledging, while also not assuming malignancy is the most likely explanation without the supporting history (endocrine and psychosocial causes are more common overall).

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent/expedited investigation: unintentional weight loss with any red flag symptom above (particularly in a patient >50yo), a palpable mass, unexplained lymphadenopathy, or rectal bleeding — these warrant urgent cancer-pathway-style investigation rather than routine work-up.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (bloods — TFTs, glucose/HbA1c, FBC/inflammatory markers, LFTs/coeliac screen as guided by the history; further imaging/endoscopy if red flags present), safety-net.

## 1.21 Skin Lesion / Rash

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Dermatology category: "Rash," "Skin lesions," "Itch," "Non-blanching rashes" among others, Medium-High yield) — a presentation-led topic without a dedicated history-taking template despite 9 full disease-category files having been built ([[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]] through [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]]). This entry covers the history-taking approach; cross-references the disease sections for differential detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure — systematically characterise the lesion(s) before jumping to a diagnosis:**
- **Onset and evolution:** sudden vs gradual onset; how has it changed since first noticed (spreading, new lesions appearing, changing colour/size/shape) — evolution is one of the single most diagnostically useful features (e.g. the target-lesion evolution of erythema multiforme, the colour-change-over-time of erythema nodosum, or the ABCDE "evolution" criterion for melanoma — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] Melanoma, not repeated here).
- **Distribution:** single lesion vs multiple; symmetrical vs asymmetrical; specific body sites involved (extensor vs flexor surfaces, sun-exposed vs sun-protected areas, dependent areas, mucosal involvement) — distribution alone often substantially narrows the differential (e.g. flexural in eczema, extensor in psoriasis, dependent limbs in vasculitis/purpura, dermatomal and unilateral in shingles).
- **Character:** itchy, painful, burning, or asymptomatic — pruritus vs pain points toward different disease categories (inflammatory/allergic vs infective/vascular/neuropathic respectively, though there is substantial overlap).
- **Blanching:** does it fade under pressure (a glass test where relevant) — see [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Non-blanching rashes — approach and differential for the full differential and urgency framework this feeds into, not repeated here; this single question can flag a time-critical presentation (meningococcal septicaemia) and should not be skipped.

**Further history prompts:**
- **Systemic/red flag symptoms:** fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy, joint pains — raise suspicion for an infective or systemic inflammatory cause rather than a purely localised skin process; mucosal involvement (oral, genital, ocular) alongside a rash is a specific red flag for the SJS/TEN spectrum or erythema multiforme major (see [[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]] Stevens-Johnson syndrome / Toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN) necrolysis) and should prompt urgent assessment.
- **Exposure history:** new medications (drug reaction, including the SJS/TEN differential above), new cosmetics/soaps/detergents (contact dermatitis), recent travel (tropical/vector-borne rashes — see the relevant [[08_05-06_Infectious_Disease_-_Viral_Infections]] and [[08_07_Infectious_Disease_-_Protozoan_Infections]] entries), occupational exposures, sun exposure (photosensitive eruptions, or as a risk factor for skin cancer — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] and [[09_03a_Dermatology_-_Non-Melanoma_Skin_Cancer]]), animal or insect contact (scabies, tick bites, insect bite reactions), sexual contact history where genital lesions are involved (see [[08_08_Infectious_Disease_-_Genitourinary_Infections_and_STIs]]).
- **Infectious contact/outbreak history:** unwell contacts with a similar rash, recent school/childcare attendance (many of the classic childhood exanthems — see [[08_05-06_Infectious_Disease_-_Viral_Infections]] Measles, Rubella, and the Viral Exanthemata "numbered rashes" table, not repeated here), recent vaccination.
- **Past medical/family history:** personal or family history of atopy (eczema, asthma, allergic rhinitis — supports an atopic dermatitis differential), personal or family history of psoriasis or other chronic dermatoses, immunosuppression (broadens the infective differential and increases severity risk for many conditions covered throughout the Dermatology and Infectious Disease files).
- **Impact and prior treatment:** what has been tried already and with what effect (informs both diagnosis, via treatment response, and next-step management); functional/psychosocial impact, given visible skin disease can carry a disproportionate quality-of-life burden worth actively asking about rather than assuming from lesion severity alone.

**ICE:** visible skin conditions carry a distinct psychosocial burden (embarrassment, social withdrawal, assumptions from others about contagiousness) that may not correlate with objective disease severity — worth exploring directly rather than assuming from the clinical picture alone.

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent assessment: fever with a rapidly progressing non-blanching rash (meningococcal septicaemia — see [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Non-blanching rashes — approach and differential), widespread blistering/skin detachment with mucosal involvement (SJS/TEN spectrum), pain out of proportion to visible skin findings (necrotising fasciitis — see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]]), and any rapidly evolving or bleeding pigmented lesion (possible melanoma).

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination — see [[Examination]] Dermatological Examination for the systematic approach; targeted investigation as guided by the differential reached), safety-net for any red flag features developing.

## 1.22 Fever and Suspected Infection

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Infectious Disease category: "Fever," "Fever of unknown origin," "Return traveller fever," "Bacteraemia and septicaemia" among others, High yield) — a presentation-led topic without a dedicated history-taking template despite the extensive Infectious Disease disease-level content built across [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] through [[08_10_Infectious_Disease_-_Diarrhoea_DDx_and_Gastroenteritis]]. This entry covers the history-taking approach; cross-references the disease sections for differential detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure:**
- **Onset, pattern, and duration:** sudden vs gradual onset; continuous vs intermittent/cyclical fever (e.g. the classic 48h/72h cyclical pattern of non-falciparum malaria — see [[08_07_Infectious_Disease_-_Protozoan_Infections]] Malaria, not repeated here); duration — fever persisting beyond 3 weeks without a clear source, resistant to a week of inpatient work-up, meets the classic definition of pyrexia of unknown origin (see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Pyrexia of unknown origin (PUO) / Fever of unknown origin (FUO) origin).
- **Associated localising symptoms — the single most useful step for narrowing the differential:** systematically screen by system (respiratory — cough, dyspnoea, pleuritic pain; urinary — dysuria, frequency, loin pain; GI — diarrhoea, abdominal pain, vomiting; skin — rash, wound, cellulitis; neurological — headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, confusion; ENT — sore throat, ear pain; joint — swelling, pain) — a positive finding in any system should trigger the relevant disease-specific work-up already built elsewhere in this project rather than treating "fever" as an undifferentiated diagnosis in itself.

**Further history prompts:**
- **Travel history — critical and easily missed if not explicitly asked:** where, when, duration, activities (rural/urban, freshwater/seawater exposure, animal contact, insect bites), pre-travel vaccinations and malaria prophylaxis (taken as prescribed or not), food/water sources — directly informs the tropical/travel-related differential built across [[08_05-06_Infectious_Disease_-_Viral_Infections]] (Dengue, Yellow Fever), [[08_07_Infectious_Disease_-_Protozoan_Infections]] (Malaria, Schistosomiasis, Trypanosomiasis), and elsewhere; malaria specifically must be actively excluded in any febrile returned traveller from an endemic area, given the time-critical nature of falciparum disease. See [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Approach to Fever in the Returned Traveller for the incubation-period-based framework this history feeds into, not repeated here.
- **Exposure and risk factor screen:** sick contacts, occupational exposure (healthcare, farming/abattoir work, animal handling — relevant to zoonotic causes like Q fever, leptospirosis, brucellosis), recent surgery or invasive procedures (surgical site infection, line-associated bacteraemia), IVDU (endocarditis, skin/soft tissue infection), sexual history (STI-related causes — see [[08_08_Infectious_Disease_-_Genitourinary_Infections_and_STIs]]), recent hospitalisation (healthcare-associated infection, *C. difficile* — see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Clostridioides difficile), tick/insect bites, animal bites or scratches (see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Animal & human bites, and Cat Scratch Disease in [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]]).
- **Immunisation status:** relevant both to excluding vaccine-preventable causes and to informing risk if a vaccine-preventable disease is suspected — see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Vaccination Schedule (Australia — National Immunisation Program) for the current AU-specific schedule, not repeated here.
- **Host risk factors:** immunosuppression (broadens the differential substantially and lowers the threshold for urgent work-up — see the CD4-stratified opportunistic infection framework in [[08_05-06_Infectious_Disease_-_Viral_Infections]] Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) for one detailed example), asplenia/splenectomy (see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Post-splenectomy sepsis — a genuinely time-critical scenario given the rapid deterioration risk), diabetes, indwelling devices/prostheses (lines, catheters, prosthetic joints/valves — all potential infection foci), recent antibiotic use (relevant both to resistant organism risk and to *C. difficile* risk).
- **Systemic/constitutional screen:** night sweats, weight loss, fatigue, rigors (true rigors — shaking chills — are a more specific marker of bacteraemia than fever alone and worth specifically asking about rather than assuming from "feeling hot").

**ICE:** fever often triggers disproportionate anxiety in patients/parents (particularly for paediatric presentations) relative to its actual severity — addressing this directly, alongside clear safety-netting advice, is often as important as the clinical work-up itself.

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent assessment/escalation: fever with any feature of sepsis (see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Sepsis and the Australian "SEPSIS KILLS" pathway), fever with a non-blanching rash (see [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Non-blanching rashes), fever in a returned traveller from a malaria-endemic area (exclude malaria urgently regardless of how mild the presentation looks), fever in an asplenic or otherwise significantly immunosuppressed patient (treat as a medical emergency given the deterioration risk), fever with neck stiffness/photophobia/non-blanching rash (meningococcal disease until proven otherwise).

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination, targeted investigation guided by localising symptoms — see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Blood Cultures and Microbiology Basics for the general specimen-collection principles, not repeated here), safety-net explicitly for any red flag features developing.

## 1.23 Psychiatric History

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Psychiatric history and mental state examination," High yield) — completes the pairing with [[Examination]] Mental State Examination (MSE), not repeated here. The disease-specific diagnostic criteria boxes throughout [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] through [[14_07_Psych_-_Attention_Deficit_Hyperactivity_Disorder]] cover the *current symptom* domain of a psychiatric assessment, but a full psychiatric history has several further biopsychosocial domains that no single disease box captures — this entry covers that broader structure.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent — the same general principles as any history, though a psychiatric history often benefits from extra attention to rapport-building and privacy given the sensitivity of the content, and from explicitly explaining confidentiality and its limits (e.g. the duty to act on disclosed risk to self or others) before the person shares sensitive material.

**Presenting complaint and history of presenting complaint** — the same general open-to-closed structure as any HPC, but specifically explore: onset and duration, any identifiable trigger or precipitant, the symptom pattern and its impact on daily functioning (work/study, relationships, self-care), and what specifically prompted presentation now rather than earlier or later — this last question often reveals genuinely useful context (a crisis point, a family member's concern, a practical deadline) beyond the symptoms themselves.

**The broader psychiatric history structure — domains not captured by any single disease-specific diagnostic criteria box:**
- **Past psychiatric history:** previous episodes of the same or different presentations, previous diagnoses, previous treatments (medications, psychological therapies, hospital admissions) and their outcomes/tolerability — genuinely important given past treatment response is often the single most useful predictor of what will work again, and past diagnoses reshape the current differential.
- **Past medical history:** relevant given the extensive organic differentials threaded throughout the Psychiatry disease files (e.g. thyroid dysfunction in mood/anxiety presentations, per [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] and [[14_02_Psych_-_Anxiety_and_Related_Disorders]]) — a psychiatric presentation should never be assumed purely psychiatric without this screen.
- **Drug history:** current prescribed medications (including adherence, which is often more revealing than the prescription list alone), over-the-counter and complementary preparations, and specifically substances that can cause or worsen psychiatric symptoms as a side effect (steroids, some other drug classes — see the relevant disease-specific medication-trigger notes throughout the Psychiatry files, not repeated here); allergies.
- **Family history:** psychiatric illness in first-degree relatives specifically (a genuinely strong risk factor across almost every condition in this project's Psychiatry category — see the individual disease files for condition-specific heritability detail, not repeated here), including family history of suicide, given this is both a risk factor in its own right (per [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Suicide) and something families may not volunteer unprompted.
- **Personal (developmental) history:** birth and early development, childhood (family environment, any history of abuse/neglect/trauma — asked sensitively, and only as deeply as clinically relevant and the person is comfortable with), educational history and achievement, occupational history, relationship history — this domain gives context for personality, attachment patterns, and current functioning that the presenting complaint alone doesn't reveal.
- **Social history:** current living situation and who the person lives with, relationship/marital status, financial circumstances, employment status, social support network — directly relevant to risk (see the "social supports lacking" SADPERSONS factor in [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Suicide, not repeated here) and to practical safety planning and discharge/follow-up arrangements.
- **Substance use history:** alcohol, tobacco, and other substances — see [[14a-1_Psych_-_Substance_Misuse__Recreational_Drug_Profiles_]] for the individual substance profiles this screen feeds into, not repeated here; ask directly and non-judgementally, given underreporting is common if the question is asked in a way that feels accusatory.
- **Forensic history:** any history of criminal charges, convictions, or involvement with the legal system — relevant both diagnostically (e.g. per the antisocial personality disorder criteria in [[14_04_Psych_-_Personality_Disorders]]) and to risk assessment and safety planning; ask matter-of-factly rather than apologetically, given an overly hesitant approach to this question can itself communicate judgement.
- **Premorbid personality:** how the person describes themselves and their usual functioning **before** the onset of the current presentation — genuinely useful for distinguishing an acute change in mental state from a longstanding personality pattern, and for gauging how much the current presentation represents a departure from baseline.

**Risk assessment — woven throughout the history, not a single isolated question at the end:**
- Risk to self: suicidal ideation, intent, and plan (see [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Suicide for the SADPERSONS risk factors and the full approach, not repeated here), self-harm history and current urges, and — where relevant to the presentation — deliberate self-neglect.
- Risk to others: any current thoughts, intent, or plan to harm another specific person, and any history of violence.
- Risk from others: current safety in living situation, any current abuse or exploitation — relevant given psychiatric presentations can both result from and coexist with being a victim of harm.
- Risk to dependants: particularly relevant in the perinatal context (see [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Perinatal depression and [[14_03_Psych_-_Psychotic_Disorders_and_Antipsychotics]] Postpartum (Puerperal) Psychosis) or where the person has caring responsibilities for a child or dependent adult.

**ICE:** particularly important in psychiatric presentations given the risk of stigma-driven reluctance to disclose — explicitly ask what the person is hoping for from the consultation, and what concerns (about diagnosis, treatment, confidentiality, or being judged) might be affecting what they're comfortable sharing.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (proceeding to the Mental State Examination — see [[Examination]] Mental State Examination (MSE) — and any further assessment/investigation the presentation suggests), safety-net explicitly for risk (what to do and who to contact if things worsen before the next review), given this is a genuinely essential closing step for this category of history in a way it isn't always for a purely physical presentation.

**Cross-reference:** see [[Examination]] Mental State Examination (MSE) for the paired examination this history feeds into, and the individual Psychiatry disease files for condition-specific diagnostic criteria and management, not repeated here.

## 1.24 Clinical Formulation — Turning a History Into an Explanation

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Clinical formulation," Public Health/Epidemiology–adjacent row, carried into the queue with N2/N5). Genuinely absent on the teach-vs-mention test: the Psychiatric History entry above collects the domains a formulation is built from, and [[Examination]] Mental State Examination (MSE) collects the cross-sectional findings — but **nothing in the corpus taught the synthesis step between them**, and corpus-wide search returned zero hits for *4 Ps*, *5 Ps*, *predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating*, or *formulation* in this sense (every "formulation" match was pharmaceutical). Placed here rather than in `Clinical-Process-EBM-Consent-Capacity.md` as the queue's remainder table proposed: this is the step immediately after the history that feeds it and the MSE it pairs with, and separating it from both would leave the reader arriving at the synthesis with the two inputs in another file. Verified against the **RANZCP** mood-disorders guidelines' biopsychosocial-lifestyle framing and Selzer & Ellen, *Formulation for beginners*, **Australasian Psychiatry 2014;22(4):397–401** — see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` B34.

**What a formulation is, and what it is not.** A **diagnosis** names the condition — it answers *what*. A **formulation** is a set of explanatory hypotheses about **why this person is unwell in this way, at this time** — it answers *why now, and why them*. Two patients with an identical diagnosis of major depression can have formulations with almost nothing in common, and it is the formulation, not the diagnosis, that tells you what to actually do for either of them.

> [!danger] **The most common error is writing a summary and calling it a formulation.** "45-year-old man with a three-month history of low mood, poor sleep and anhedonia, on a background of hypothyroidism" is a *summary* — it restates the history in shorter form. A formulation must contain at least one **explanatory link**: something that says *this* factor made *that* outcome more likely, and why. If you can write it without using the word "because" or its equivalent, you have not formulated.

### 1.24.1 The 4 Ps — the grid that does the work

Ask the four questions in order. The fourth is the one most often skipped and the one that most changes management.

| | The question it answers | Examples of what belongs here |
|---|---|---|
| **Predisposing** | What made this person **vulnerable** in the first place? | Family history, early adversity or trauma, chronic illness, personality traits, developmental factors. Usually long-standing and often not modifiable. |
| **Precipitating** | What **triggered it now**? | A relationship breakdown, job loss, bereavement, a new medication, a physical illness, a substance. This is the answer to "why this month and not last year". |
| **Perpetuating** | What is **keeping it going**? | Ongoing conflict, unemployment, alcohol, social isolation, untreated pain, poor sleep, an unaddressed side effect. **These are usually the most modifiable factors in the whole grid**, which is why this row generates most of the management plan. |
| **Protective** | What is **working in their favour**? | Supportive relationships, employment, insight, previous good treatment response, engagement with services, absence of substance use. Skipping this row produces a formulation that reads as a list of problems and gives you nothing to build on. |

**The biopsychosocial axis, crossed with the four Ps.** Each of the four is asked in three domains — **biological** (genetics, physical illness, medication, substances), **psychological** (beliefs, coping style, trauma, thinking patterns), and **social** (housing, work, finances, relationships, culture, legal). The value of the grid is that it forces you into cells you would otherwise leave empty: most clinicians default to one domain, and the crossed structure makes the omission visible.

> [!tip] **A worked example, deliberately brief.** *Predisposing:* family history of depression (bio), childhood bereavement (psych). *Precipitating:* redundancy six weeks ago (social), started on a beta-blocker two months ago (bio). *Perpetuating:* drinking most nights to sleep (bio/psych), has withdrawn from his football club (social). *Protective:* stable marriage, responded well to sertraline eight years ago, wants help (psych/social). Notice that the management plan writes itself from the third row — review the alcohol, review the beta-blocker, re-engage the social contact — and the fourth row tells you where to start.

> [!info] **This is not a psychiatry-only skill, which is the reason it sits in this file rather than in the Psychiatry group.** The same grid explains the frequently-readmitted patient with heart failure (predisposing: cardiomyopathy; precipitating: a chest infection; perpetuating: cannot afford the medicines and lives alone with no one to prompt them; protective: a daughter nearby who is willing to help), the patient with recurrent DKA, and the older person with repeated falls. Whenever the medical facts alone do not explain the pattern, the missing content is in the psychosocial cells (see [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Discharge Planning and Home Safety Assessment and [[19_General_Practice_and_Preventive_Medicine]] Hospital Avoidance and Potentially Preventable Hospitalisations, which are the same reasoning applied to admissions).

> [!info] **Equity, and it is specific to this skill rather than general (Step 10).** The formulation is the one part of the clinical record where **social and historical determinants enter the reasoning explicitly rather than as background**. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients this matters concretely: dispossession, forced removal from family and its intergenerational effects, experience of racism as a chronic stressor, and distance from country and kin are **predisposing and perpetuating factors with clinical consequences**, and a formulation that omits them is not neutral — it silently reattributes the whole picture to the biological and psychological cells. The corresponding protective factors are equally real and equally easy to omit: connection to family, community and country, and cultural identity. The practical instruction is to ask, and to write what you are told into the grid, rather than to assume either the adversity or the support.

> [!tip] **What an intern is actually expected to produce.** Three or four sentences, spoken on a ward round or written at the top of an assessment — *this is who they are, this is what set it off, this is what is keeping it going, this is what we have to work with* — followed by a plan that visibly follows from the perpetuating and protective rows. Not a page. The test of whether it is any good is whether someone who has not met the patient could predict what the plan is going to say.

**Cross-reference:** Psychiatric History above in this file for the domains this is built from; [[Examination]] Mental State Examination (MSE) for the cross-sectional findings it incorporates; [[Communication]] Clinical Handover (ISBAR) and Prioritisation of Jobs for how the formulation compresses into the "situation/background" of a handover.

## 1.25 Adolescent Psychosocial Assessment (HEADSS/HEEADSSS)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Adolescent health care incl - HEADSS," Medium yield) — genuinely absent despite being a distinctly structured, widely-taught framework in its own right, not a variant of the general psychiatric history above; built here given how commonly this specific structured approach is examined and used in adolescent consultations across all specialties, not just psychiatry.

**Opening:** for an adolescent specifically, **explicitly discuss confidentiality before beginning** — explain what will and won't be shared with parents/caregivers, and the specific circumstances that would require breaking confidentiality (imminent danger to self or others, abuse, and other statutory reporting obligations that vary by jurisdiction) — this explicit confidentiality discussion is itself a core part of the assessment, not a preamble to skip, given adolescents are significantly more likely to disclose sensitive information once they understand the boundaries. Where possible and age-appropriate, conduct at least part of the consultation with the adolescent alone, without the parent/caregiver present (with the adolescent's and, where relevant, the parent's agreement) — many of the most clinically important disclosures won't happen with a parent in the room.

**Structure — progress deliberately from least to most sensitive topics, in this order, given this sequencing itself builds the trust needed for later, more sensitive questions to be answered honestly:**

1. **Home:** who lives at home, relationships with family members, any recent changes (separation, new partners, moves), whether the adolescent feels safe and supported at home.
2. **Education/Employment:** school attendance and performance, relationships with teachers and peers, any bullying (as victim or perpetrator), future plans/employment if relevant.
3. **Eating:** body image, any dieting or weight concerns, eating patterns — a genuinely important domain to screen given the peak onset age for eating disorders overlaps significantly with adolescence, see [[14_05a_Psych_-_Eating_Disorders]] for the disease-level detail this screen feeds into, not repeated here.
4. **Activities:** peer relationships and friendships, hobbies/interests, exercise, use of social media and screen time — genuinely revealing of both social functioning and risk (excessive isolation, or activities themselves carrying risk).
5. **Drugs:** tobacco, alcohol, and other substance use — ask directly and non-judgementally, given underreporting is common if the question feels accusatory; see [[14a-1_Psych_-_Substance_Misuse__Recreational_Drug_Profiles_]] for the individual substance profiles this screen feeds into, not repeated here.
6. **Sexuality:** relationships, sexual activity, contraception use, gender identity and sexual orientation (asked respectfully and without assumption), and screening for any non-consensual experiences — approached with genuine respect and without judgement, given how easily this domain can be handled poorly and damage the entire therapeutic relationship.
7. **Suicide/Depression:** mood, self-harm, suicidal ideation — see [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Suicide for the fuller risk assessment approach this connects to, not repeated here; this domain should never be skipped or rushed given its direct safety implications.
8. **Safety:** exposure to violence (at home, at school, in relationships), access to weapons, risk-taking behaviour (e.g. driving safety) — the "second S" in the extended HEEADSSS version of this framework, increasingly included given its genuine clinical relevance.

> [!tip] The framework is commonly taught under two closely related names — **HEADSS** (the original 5-domain version) and **HEEADSSS** (an expanded, updated version adding Eating and Safety as explicit separate domains) — both describe the same underlying progressive, least-to-most-sensitive structure; use whichever acronym is locally taught, given the underlying approach is identical.

**Practical notes:**
- The assessment does not need to be completed in a single consultation — for a guarded or complex adolescent, spreading it across multiple visits and prioritising trust-building over completeness in any single sitting is entirely appropriate, and rushing to "finish the form" can itself undermine disclosure.
- Document findings clearly, including any safety concerns identified and the specific follow-up/referral plan, given this is often a shared record informing future consultations with the same young person.

**ICE:** adolescents are often acutely aware of, and sensitive to, being judged — explicitly normalising the breadth of questions ("I ask all young people about these things, not just you") can reduce defensiveness and improve the quality of disclosure.

**Cross-reference:** see the Psychiatric History entry above in this file for the broader psychiatric history structure this pairs with when a specific mental health concern is the presenting complaint, and the individual disease files cross-referenced throughout above for domain-specific detail.

## 1.26 Growth Failure (Faltering Growth)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Growth Failure," High yield) — genuinely absent as a presentation-led history despite "faltering growth" being referenced constantly throughout the Paediatrics disease files (congenital hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, malabsorption, cystic fibrosis, and many genetic syndromes) without a dedicated approach to the history itself. Cross-references [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Growth Charts and Percentile Interpretation for the objective growth-chart assessment this history pairs with, not repeated here.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose sensitively (growth/weight concerns can be a source of parental guilt or anxiety, so framing matters), consent.

**Presenting complaint structure — establishing the actual growth pattern is the essential first step, ideally from the child's own growth chart/personal health record rather than from parental recall alone, given recall of past weights is often inaccurate:**
- When was the growth concern first noticed, and by whom (parent, health visitor/nurse, incidental finding at a routine check)?
- Has growth crossed percentile lines, or been consistently low/high along one line — see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Growth Charts and Percentile Interpretation for why this distinction matters, not repeated here.
- Is weight, height, or both affected, and in what pattern — weight preferentially affected suggests a different differential from proportionate height-and-weight involvement (see the same cross-reference above for the disproportionate-vs-proportionate reasoning, not repeated here).

**Feeding/nutritional history — often the single most revealing part of this history:**
- Detailed dietary history — what, how much, and how often the child is actually eating/drinking, ideally including a typical day's intake rather than a general impression; for infants, feeding type (breast/formula), volumes, and frequency.
- Feeding difficulties — reflux/vomiting after feeds, choking/coughing with feeds (raises concern for an underlying swallowing or anatomical problem), food refusal or extreme fussiness, prolonged feed duration.
- Availability of food and broader social/financial circumstances — food insecurity is a genuine, sometimes under-asked cause or contributor, and needs to be explored without judgement.
- Any dietary restrictions (medical, cultural, parental choice) that might be inadvertently limiting adequate intake.

**Systemic/organic screen — actively screening for an underlying medical cause, not assuming a purely nutritional/behavioural explanation:**
- GI symptoms: diarrhoea, steatorrhoea, abdominal distension/pain, vomiting — raises coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis, or other malabsorptive causes, see [[15_07_Paeds_-_Abdominal_Pain__Neuroblastoma__Coeliac_Disease__Malnutrition__Diarrhoea_and_Vomiting]] Coeliac disease in children, not repeated here.
- Respiratory symptoms: recurrent chest infections, chronic cough — raises cystic fibrosis specifically.
- Endocrine screen: cold intolerance, constipation, prolonged neonatal jaundice (congenital hypothyroidism — see [[15_16a_Paeds_-_Hypothyroidism]], not repeated here); polyuria/polydipsia (diabetes).
- Cardiac/respiratory effort during feeds in infants — increased work of breathing or excessive fatigue during feeding can reflect an underlying cardiac cause (feeding is effectively an "exercise test" for infants with significant congenital heart disease), see [[15_05_Paeds_-_Acyanotic_Congenital_Heart_Disease]] and [[15_06_Paeds_-_Cyanotic_CHD__Kawasaki_Disease__Murmurs]], not repeated here.
- Developmental history — a global developmental delay alongside growth failure broadens the differential toward a genetic/syndromic or chronic systemic cause, see [[15_19a_Paeds_-_Developmental_Milestones_and_Delay]] Global developmental delay, not repeated here.

**Birth and early history:** birth weight, gestational age, any neonatal problems (prematurity, birth asphyxia, neonatal hypoglycaemia) — relevant given several of these are themselves risk factors for later growth and developmental problems, and given a low birth weight changes the interpretation of subsequent growth trajectory.

**Family and social history:**
- Parental heights (for mid-parental height estimation, per [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Growth Charts and Percentile Interpretation, not repeated here) and parental growth/pubertal patterns (a family history of constitutional delay is reassuring in a specific way that other causes aren't).
- Family history of coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis, or other relevant genetic/GI conditions.
- Social circumstances — parental mental health, substance use, domestic circumstances, and the quality of parent-child interaction during feeding, given these can all genuinely contribute to non-organic faltering growth (previously sometimes termed "psychosocial" or "non-organic" failure to thrive) — this must be explored sensitively and without assuming neglect, given the majority of cases have a combination of contributing factors rather than a single cause.

**ICE:** explore parental concerns and any pressure or anxiety they're experiencing around feeding specifically, given feeding difficulties can become a significant source of family stress that itself sometimes worsens the feeding relationship — acknowledging this directly, without judgement, is important for both rapport and management planning.

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent assessment: severe or rapidly progressive growth failure; signs of dehydration or acute illness; developmental regression (losing previously-acquired skills, as opposed to simple delay); signs raising concern for safeguarding (see [[15_24a_Paeds_-_Non-Accidental_Injury_and_Sexual_Abuse]], not repeated here) — faltering growth can itself be a presentation of neglect, and this possibility should be held in mind without it being the default assumption.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (plotting/reviewing the growth chart per [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Growth Charts and Percentile Interpretation, examination including a full systemic screen, and targeted investigation guided by the pattern established above), safety-net for any red flag symptoms developing, and offer practical, non-judgemental support around feeding given how emotionally loaded this presentation often is for families.

## 1.27 Vertigo and Dizziness

> [!note] Gap-filled — genuinely absent as a history-taking template despite the extensive central vs peripheral vertigo differential already built in [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] and the individual peripheral vertigo disease entries in [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]]. This entry covers the history-taking approach; cross-references the disease sections for differential detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure — clarifying what the patient actually means by "dizzy" is the essential first step, given the word is used loosely for several genuinely different sensations with different differentials:**
- **"Is it a spinning/rotational sensation, or something else (light-headedness, unsteadiness, or a feeling you might faint)?"** — true vertigo (the illusion of rotary movement of self or environment, per [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Vertigo) has a different differential from pre-syncope (suggesting a cardiovascular/postural cause) or disequilibrium/imbalance without a rotational component (suggesting a proprioceptive, cerebellar, or visual cause) — this single clarifying question does more diagnostic work than almost anything else in the history, and a patient's spontaneous word choice ("dizzy") shouldn't be taken at face value without confirming which sensation they mean.
- **Timing and duration — a genuinely high-yield discriminator once true vertigo is confirmed:** seconds (BPPV — see [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)); minutes to hours, episodic (Ménière's disease — see [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Ménière's disease); days, single prolonged episode (vestibular neuronitis or labyrinthitis — see [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Vestibular neuronitis and (Viral) labyrinthitis); constant/progressive over weeks (raises a central or space-occupying cause, e.g. vestibular schwannoma — see [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma), or a central cause per the list below).
- **Triggers:** specifically ask whether vertigo is provoked by head/position change (turning over in bed, looking up, bending down — classic for BPPV) versus present at rest or constant regardless of position (points away from BPPV toward the other peripheral or central causes).
- **Associated ear symptoms — hearing loss and tinnitus are genuinely important discriminators:** vertigo **with** hearing loss/tinnitus points toward Ménière's disease or labyrinthitis; vertigo **without** hearing loss/tinnitus points toward vestibular neuronitis or BPPV — see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Vertigo for the fuller peripheral-cause differential this maps onto, not repeated here.

**Central vs peripheral screen — the other major branch point, alongside timing/associated symptoms above:**
- **Peripheral features:** unidirectional nystagmus, gradual onset, often severe/incapacitating vertigo but with preserved ability to walk (with support), typically improves over days-weeks.
- **Central features (red flags):** bidirectional or vertical nystagmus, additional neurological symptoms (diplopia, dysarthria, limb weakness/ataxia, facial numbness — see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Central vestibular dysfunction for the fuller cause list, not repeated here), severe truncal ataxia/inability to walk at all even with support, sudden onset in a patient with vascular risk factors (raising posterior circulation stroke/TIA as a differential that must be actively excluded, not just "vertigo" assumed benign).
- **Vascular risk factor screen:** age, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, atrial fibrillation, prior stroke/TIA — relevant given central vertigo from vertebrobasilar ischaemia is a genuine, easily-missed stroke mimic/presentation in older patients, particularly if other central features above are present.

**Further history prompts:**
- **Preceding illness:** recent viral URTI (vestibular neuronitis, labyrinthitis), recent head trauma (BPPV can be trauma-triggered, or consider a temporal bone fracture).
- **Medication and substance history:** ototoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, some chemotherapy agents), alcohol, sedatives.
- **Cardiovascular symptoms** if pre-syncope rather than true vertigo is suspected per the opening clarifying question — palpitations, chest pain, exertional symptoms; postural symptoms specifically (worse on standing) raise orthostatic hypotension.
- **Functional impact and falls risk:** falls history, impact on daily activities, driving — relevant both to safety-netting and to the Austroads driving-notification principle already established in [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Ménière's disease, not repeated here.

**ICE:** vertigo is often genuinely frightening, particularly on first presentation — patients commonly fear a stroke or brain tumour even when the cause is benign; worth exploring this directly and providing proportionate reassurance once red flags have been actively excluded (not assumed absent).

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent assessment/imaging: any central feature above (particularly sudden onset with vascular risk factors, or additional neurological symptoms/signs), new hearing loss with vertigo (Ménière's/labyrinthitis can usually be managed non-urgently, but an atypical or rapidly progressive picture warrants prompt ENT assessment), or vertigo following head trauma with any other injury red flags.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination — see [[Examination]] ABCDE Assessment (Acutely Unwell / Deteriorating Patient) if acutely unwell, or targeted cranial nerve/cerebellar/otoscopic examination and the Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre if BPPV is suspected per [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), not repeated here), safety-net for any red flag symptoms developing.

## 1.28 Bruising and Bleeding

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Bleeding disorders," Haematology category, High yield) — a presentation-led topic without a dedicated history-taking template despite extensive disease-level content built in [[10_07_Haemonc_-_Platelet_and_Clotting_Disorders__Neutropaenia]] (ITP, von Willebrand disease, haemophilia) and [[10_06b_Haemonc_-_Thrombophilia__APS__Thrombocytosis__Methaemoglobinaemia]]. This entry covers the history-taking approach; cross-references the disease sections for differential detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure — the pattern of bleeding does most of the diagnostic work:**
- **Site and pattern are the single most useful discriminator:** mucocutaneous bleeding (easy bruising, epistaxis, gum bleeding, menorrhagia, petechiae) points toward a **platelet or vascular** disorder (see [[10_07_Haemonc_-_Platelet_and_Clotting_Disorders__Neutropaenia]] Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and Von Willebrand disease); deep bleeding (haemarthroses, intramuscular haematomas, delayed post-surgical bleeding) points toward a **coagulation factor** disorder (see [[10_07_Haemonc_-_Platelet_and_Clotting_Disorders__Neutropaenia]] Haemophilia) — this single distinction (superficial/mucocutaneous vs deep) is worth establishing early, since it substantially narrows the differential before any test is even ordered.
- **Onset and timing:** lifelong (congenital — vWD, haemophilia) vs new onset (acquired — ITP, liver disease, drug-induced, malignancy-related); spontaneous vs only with trauma/surgery/dental work (a history of uneventful prior surgery or dental extraction is genuinely reassuring against a significant bleeding disorder, while excessive bleeding after a previous minor procedure is a useful positive clue).
- **Severity and triggers:** what specifically prompted the bleeding (spontaneous, minor trauma, surgery, dental work, childbirth) and how it was managed (required transfusion, packing, re-operation) — objective markers of severity like these are more reliable than a patient's subjective sense of "a lot of bleeding."

**Further history prompts:**
- **Family history — essential given many bleeding disorders are inherited:** specifically ask about bleeding tendency in relatives, not just a named diagnosis, since many patients/families aren't aware of a formal diagnosis; note the inheritance pattern clues — X-linked recessive (haemophilia — affects males, carrier females usually asymptomatic) vs autosomal dominant (most vWD) vs autosomal recessive (severe vWD Type 3) point toward different specific diagnoses.
- **Medication history:** anticoagulants and antiplatelets (see [[10_09a_Haemonc_-_Anticoagulants_and_Antiplatelets]] for the specific drug classes, not repeated here), NSAIDs (can unmask or worsen an underlying mild bleeding tendency), herbal/complementary medicines (some have genuine antiplatelet effects, e.g. fish oil, garlic supplements, and are easy to omit if not specifically asked about).
- **Menstrual history in women:** menorrhagia is a common presentation of an underlying bleeding disorder (particularly vWD) that can be missed if not specifically explored, given heavy periods are sometimes normalised by patients rather than volunteered.
- **Associated systemic symptoms:** fever, weight loss, lymphadenopathy, bone pain (raises concern for a haematological malignancy as the underlying cause — see [[10_01_Haemonc_-_Leukaemias_and_Myeloproliferative_Disorders]] and [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]]); liver disease symptoms (jaundice, ascites, known chronic liver disease — relevant given hepatic synthetic dysfunction impairs clotting factor production); renal disease (uraemia causes platelet dysfunction); recent infection (can trigger ITP, particularly in children).
- **Diet and alcohol:** alcohol excess (liver disease, direct marrow suppression); poor diet (vitamin K or vitamin C deficiency, though genuinely rare causes worth considering only when other explanations are exhausted).

**ICE:** bleeding/bruising symptoms often carry disproportionate anxiety (patients sometimes fear leukaemia specifically) — worth acknowledging directly while taking a systematic, reassuring approach to the history rather than either dismissing the concern or amplifying it.

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent assessment: bleeding into a closed space (intracranial, retroperitoneal, compartment syndrome from intramuscular bleeding), heavy bleeding not responding to simple first-aid measures, bleeding with haemodynamic compromise, or new bleeding tendency with systemic/constitutional symptoms (raising concern for an underlying haematological malignancy or severe acquired coagulopathy such as DIC — see [[10_05_Haemonc_-_Normocytic_Anaemia_and_Sickle_Cell_Disease]] Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)).

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination for bruising pattern/joint swelling/lymphadenopathy/splenomegaly; FBC, coagulation screen, and further targeted testing guided by the pattern established above), safety-net for any red flag symptoms developing.

## 1.29 Joint Pain (Generalised and Local)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Joint pain (generalised and local)," High yield; "Joint swelling or red, hot, painful joints," Medium yield) — a presentation-led topic without a dedicated history-taking template despite the extensive Orthopaedics and Rheumatology disease-level content built across [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] through [[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]]. This entry covers the history-taking approach; cross-references the disease sections for differential detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent.

**Presenting complaint structure — the pattern of joint involvement does most of the diagnostic work, before any test is ordered:**
- **Number and distribution of joints:** monoarticular vs oligoarticular (≤4) vs polyarticular (≥5) — monoarticular acute joint pain should always prompt active consideration of septic arthritis (see [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Septic arthritis, not repeated here) and crystal arthropathy (gout/pseudogout — see [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]] Gout and Pseudogout) before broader polyarticular causes; symmetrical small-joint polyarthritis points toward RA (see [[12_01_Rheum_-_Rheumatoid_Arthritis__Osteoarthritis__Psoriatic_Arthritis]] Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)); asymmetrical oligoarthritis with lower-limb predominance raises the seronegative spondyloarthropathies (psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis — see the relevant entries in [[12_01_Rheum_-_Rheumatoid_Arthritis__Osteoarthritis__Psoriatic_Arthritis]] and [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]]).
- **Inflammatory vs mechanical pattern — one of the single most useful discriminators in the whole history:** inflammatory pain is characteristically **worse with rest and improves with activity**, with **morning stiffness lasting >30–60 minutes** (see [[12_01_Rheum_-_Rheumatoid_Arthritis__Osteoarthritis__Psoriatic_Arthritis]] Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) for the specific stiffness-duration teaching point, not repeated here); mechanical pain is characteristically **worse with activity and improves with rest**, with brief (<30 minutes) or absent morning stiffness — this single distinction does more diagnostic work than almost any other single question, and should be asked explicitly rather than inferred.
- **Onset and tempo:** sudden/acute onset (hours) favours septic arthritis, crystal arthropathy, or trauma; gradual onset over weeks-months favours osteoarthritis or an evolving inflammatory arthropathy; a relapsing-remitting pattern (resolving completely between episodes) is characteristic of crystal arthropathy and palindromic presentations, distinct from a persistent, gradually progressive inflammatory course.

**Further history prompts:**
- **Red hot swollen joint screen:** fever, feeling generally unwell, recent skin breach/infection near the joint, IVDU, immunosuppression, prosthetic joint — all raise suspicion for septic arthritis specifically, given the "hot, swollen, acutely painful joint = septic arthritis until proven otherwise" principle in [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Septic arthritis, not repeated here; **age and sexual history specifically** — consider disseminated gonococcal infection in young, sexually active patients with joint symptoms, per the same entry.
- **Extra-articular/systemic screen:** skin (psoriasis — psoriatic arthritis; malar/discoid rash — SLE; photosensitivity; nodules), eyes (dry eyes/mouth — Sjögren's; uveitis — spondyloarthropathy/JIA; scleritis — vasculitis), respiratory (dyspnoea, cough — interstitial lung disease in RA/connective tissue disease/vasculitis), GI (diarrhoea — IBD-associated arthritis, or a preceding GI infection triggering reactive arthritis), GU (urethritis/discharge — reactive arthritis, gonococcal arthritis), constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats — raise concern for infection, malignancy, or active systemic inflammatory disease).
- **Preceding illness/trigger:** recent sore throat (rheumatic fever, reactive/post-streptococcal arthritis), recent GI or GU infection (reactive arthritis — see [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]] Reactive arthritis), recent viral illness (transient synovitis in children — see [[11_10_Ortho_-_Paediatric_Orthopaedics]] Transient synovitis of the hip), trauma (mechanical injury, or occasionally a trigger for gout/pseudogout).
- **Diet, medication, and lifestyle triggers relevant to gout specifically:** alcohol, red meat/seafood intake, diuretic use, recent hospitalisation/fasting/dehydration — see [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]] Gout for the disease-level detail, not repeated here.
- **Family history:** psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis/HLA-B27-associated disease, RA, gout, autoimmune disease more broadly — genuinely useful given the strong genetic component in several of these conditions.
- **Functional impact:** effect on activities of daily living, occupation, and mobility — relevant both to management planning and as an indirect severity marker.

**ICE:** joint pain — particularly if progressive or associated with visible deformity — commonly triggers anxiety about long-term disability or "arthritis" being an inevitably worsening diagnosis; worth exploring and addressing directly, particularly since many causes (reactive arthritis, gout, several inflammatory arthropathies with modern treatment) have a genuinely good functional prognosis when properly managed.

> [!danger] Red flags warranting urgent assessment: a hot, swollen, acutely painful single joint with fever/systemic upset (septic arthritis until proven otherwise), joint pain with new neurological deficit or red flag back pain features (see [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Back pain red flags → urgent MRI and Cauda equina syndrome), sudden visual disturbance/jaw claudication in a patient >50yo with new headache (giant cell arteritis — see [[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] Giant cell arteritis (GCA), a genuine same-day ophthalmology-review emergency given the irreversible vision loss risk).

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination — see [[Examination]] GALS Screen (Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine) for the systematic approach; targeted investigation guided by the pattern established above — joint aspiration if a hot swollen joint, autoimmune serology if inflammatory pattern with systemic features, per [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Autoimmune Markers — Systematic Reference), safety-net for any red flag features developing.

## 1.30 Scrotal Pain and Swelling

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Scrotal swelling or pain," High yield; "Groin swelling or pain," Medium yield) — the disease-level differential is already built in detail in [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Testicular Torsion and Testicular Lumps (a full comparison table covering inguinal hernia, hydrocele, epididymal cyst, epididymo-orchitis, testicular tumour, testicular torsion, and varicocele) — not repeated here. This entry covers the history-taking approach, which for this presentation is dominated by one overriding priority: excluding torsion first, before working through the rest of the differential.

**Opening:** Wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, consent — approach this examination/history with particular sensitivity given its intimate nature, and explain each step before proceeding.

**The single most important triage question: could this be torsion?**
- **Onset and severity:** sudden, severe onset strongly favours torsion; gradual onset over hours-days favours epididymo-orchitis or another less urgent cause. This single discriminator does more work than any other individual history point — see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Testicular Torsion for the full red-flag S/Smx (high-riding testicle, absent cremasteric reflex, negative Prehn's sign), not repeated here.
- **Age:** torsion is most common in adolescents/young adults (peak 13–15yo) — a sudden severe presentation in this age group should be treated as torsion until proven otherwise, regardless of how the rest of the history unfolds.
- **Any doubt at all should prompt immediate urological referral** — this is one of the few presentations in medicine where the correct default, given diagnostic uncertainty, is to act on the worst-case possibility rather than wait for more clarity, given how directly testicular salvage depends on time to surgery.

**Further history prompts (once torsion has been actively considered/excluded from the acute-emergency pathway):**
- **Sexual history:** unprotected sex, new/multiple partners, discharge — raises suspicion for STI-related epididymo-orchitis (for the **full sexual history structure and what a standard STI check actually consists of**, see [[08_08_Infectious_Disease_-_Genitourinary_Infections_and_STIs]] The STI Check — Sexual History, What to Test, and When, not repeated here — this line is the red-flag screen only) (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Epididymitis for the full AU-verified Mx this feeds into)
- **Urinary symptoms:** dysuria, frequency, recent UTI — raises suspicion for a UTI-related (rather than STI-related) epididymo-orchitis, more typical in older men without sexual risk factors
- **Timing/positional factors:** worse standing, relieved lying down (varicocele — "bag of worms" character); present since birth or noticed incidentally (hydrocele, epididymal cyst); reducible, worse with straining/coughing (inguinal hernia)
- **Associated systemic symptoms:** fever (favours infective causes — epididymo-orchitis, or occasionally an incarcerated/strangulated hernia); nausea/vomiting (can accompany torsion via visceral referred pain, and shouldn't be mistaken for a primary GI complaint without examining the scrotum)
- **Trauma history:** recent injury — relevant to haematoma, or occasionally as a precipitant for torsion in a predisposed (bell-clapper) anatomy

**ICE:** patients — particularly younger patients or those with a parent/guardian present — are often embarrassed or anxious about this presentation; a calm, matter-of-fact, non-judgmental approach helps elicit an accurate history, and explicitly normalising the examination ("I need to examine down there to work out what's going on, is that okay?") builds trust for what can be an uncomfortable consultation.

> [!danger] Red flags mandating immediate same-visit urological referral: sudden severe onset (especially in a young patient), high-riding or horizontally-lying testis, absent cremasteric reflex, any diagnostic uncertainty about torsion — do not wait for imaging to refer if clinical suspicion is high. See [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Testicular Torsion for the full "time is testicle" urgency principle.

**Closing:** summarise back, check understanding, explain next steps (examination, urgent same-visit surgical/urological referral if torsion cannot be confidently excluded; otherwise targeted investigation per the likely cause — STI screen, urinalysis, or ultrasound as guided by the history), safety-net explicitly for worsening pain or new systemic symptoms.
