## Sepsis in the puerperium

**D**: life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.

**Common causative organisms**
- Most common overall: Group A Strep and *E. coli*
- Chorioamnionitis: mixed Gram-negative and Gram-positive organisms
- UTI, preterm prolonged rupture of membranes, cervical cerclage: Gram-negative bacilli

**S/smx**: as per other infections; specifically in pregnancy — abdominal/pelvic pain, offensive vaginal discharge (smelly ≈ anaerobes; serosanguinous ≈ Strep).

> [!info] Sepsis diagnostic criteria (may develop later in pregnant women)
> - Temp >38°C or <36°C
> - HR >100
> - RR >20
> - SBP <90 or MAP <70 mmHg
> - Impaired mental state
> - ↑inflammatory markers
> - ↑lactate (>4 mmol/L)

**Ix/Mx**
- Managed similarly to sepsis in any other patient
- Sepsis is associated with preterm labour — alert neonatal unit
- If chorioamnionitis suspected: expedite delivery
- Lower threshold for ICU transfer
- In labour: continuous fetal monitoring recommended; avoid spinal/epidural analgesia — use GA for C-section

## Eclampsia

**D**: seizures associated with pre-eclampsia (pregnancy-induced HTN + proteinuria + tonic-clonic seizures).

**R**: as per pre-eclampsia. 1% of pre-eclampsia progresses to eclampsia.

**S/smx**: tonic-clonic seizure — may be the first presentation of eclampsia/pre-eclampsia. 38% fit antenatally, 18% during labour, 44% postnatally.

**Mx**
- Call for help ASAP; ABCDE stabilisation

> [!info] Verified against Queensland Clinical Guidelines "Management of eclampsia," Aug 2026 — **the loading dose infusion time needed a genuine correction**: Australian guidance specifies magnesium sulfate 4g IV given over **20 minutes** via a controlled infusion device — the file's original "5–10 min" is meaningfully faster than Australian practice and matters clinically, given infusing magnesium too quickly increases the risk of toxicity (respiratory depression, cardiac effects) without added seizure-control benefit. The maintenance dose (1g/hour, continuing 24 hours after birth or the last seizure) is consistent with Australian practice — that part didn't need correcting. For recurrent/ongoing seizures while magnesium is being prepared or after an inadequate response, Australian guidance offers **diazepam, clonazepam, or midazolam** as options (not diazepam alone) — check current local protocol for the preferred agent. The toxicity thresholds (RR <12/min, loss of tendon reflexes, oliguria) and calcium gluconate as the antidote are consistent with Australian practice.
>
> - Magnesium sulfate to prevent/treat seizures: 4g IV over **20 minutes** via controlled infusion device, then 1g/h for 24h; continue for 24h after last seizure or delivery
> - Treat further seizures with 2g bolus + diazepam, clonazepam, or midazolam (per local protocol)
> - Stop if RR <12/min, loss of tendon reflexes, or UO <20 mL/h
> - If MgSO4 toxicity (respiratory depression): give calcium gluconate over 10 min
>
> **BP management** — verified against Safer Care Victoria's Hypertension in Pregnancy guideline; the labetalol regimen below is consistent with Australian practice (used in the same escalating pattern by Australian state maternity services), not a UK-specific figure needing correction.
> - If >160/110 or MAP >125: labetalol 20mg IV, increasing after 10-min intervals to 40mg then 80mg
> - Switch to hydralazine if still uncontrolled (or use hydralazine first, then labetalol)

- Rule out intracranial haemorrhage
- Catheterise for hourly UO, obs every 15 min
- Restrict fluids to 80 mL/h until clinically/biochemically improving (unless haemorrhaging)
- Monitor fetal HR with CTG
- Deliver once mother is stable — C-section is quickest but vaginal delivery is not contraindicated

> [!danger] Manage 3rd stage with oxytocin only — other uterotonic drugs (e.g. ergometrine) are contraindicated in eclampsia/pre-eclampsia.

## Placental abruption

**D**: premature separation of a normally located placenta from the uterine wall occurring before delivery of the fetus. 1:200 pregnancies.

**R**: chronic HTN, pre-eclampsia, smoking, cocaine use, trauma, chorioamnionitis, uterine malformations, prior placental abruption, oligohydramnios.

**A**: direct or indirect trauma; cocaine use (→ vasospasm → bleeding).

**S/smx**
- Shock out of keeping with visible blood loss (see [[16_10-13_Labour_and_Delivery]] Placenta praevia for the full mechanism explaining why — concealed retroplacental bleeding vs praevia's fully externally-visible bleeding — not repeated here)
- Constant pain, tender and tense uterus
- Coagulation problems (e.g. DIC)
- Baby usually in normal lie/presentation, but fetal heart sounds may be absent or distressed

**Ix**: fetal monitoring, Hb + Hct, coagulation studies, US.

**Mx**: stabilise mother and baby.
- Resuscitate as needed; blood transfusion if evidence of anaemia
- Anti-D Ig in Rh-negative women

| Gestation & stability | Mx |
|---|---|
| >34w + baby stable | Vaginal delivery |
| >34w + baby or mother unstable | C-section delivery |
| <34w + baby & mother stable | Manage conservatively, delay delivery |
| <34w + baby or mother unstable | Deliver (vaginal or C-section) |

**P**: for baby — depends on gestational age at abruption (younger = worse outcomes). For mother — monitor subsequent pregnancies carefully (5.8% recurrence); also associated with lifetime ↑cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease risk.

## Cord prolapse

**D**: presentation of the umbilical cord before the fetal presenting part.

**R**: 2nd twin, footling breech, prematurity, polyhydramnios, unengaged head, transverse or unstable lie, male fetus more than female.

**A/P**: cord compression and vasospasm from cord exposure causes fetal asphyxia.

**S/smx**: may be obvious if cord visible; however, fetal bradycardia or variable fetal heart decelerations may be the only sign.

**Ix**: fetal monitoring.

**Mx**
- Get senior help + emergency call (per local hospital emergency call protocol — Australian hospitals typically use "777" or a similar site-specific code for a MET/code blue call, not the UK's "2222"; confirm the specific number for the relevant facility)
- Minimise cord compression and vasocompression
  - Presenting fetal part may be pushed back into uterus to avoid compression
  - If cord is past the external vaginal opening: minimal handling + keep warm (prevents vasospasm)
  - Position patient "all fours" or left lateral (knees to chest) until prep for C-section
  - Tocolytics may reduce contractions
  - Retrofilling bladder with 500mL saline may help elevate presenting part (empty bladder before delivery)

## Shoulder dystocia

**D**: delivery requiring additional obstetric manoeuvres to release the shoulders after gentle downward traction has failed. 1:200 pregnancies.

**R**: large/post-mature fetus, maternal BMI >30, induced labours, prolonged 1st or 2nd stage, assisted vaginal delivery, previous shoulder dystocia.

> [!danger] Mx — SPEED IS KEY
> - Get senior++ help
> - Episiotomy to create space for internal manoeuvres
> - **McRoberts' manoeuvre**: flexion + abduction of maternal hips bringing thighs to abdomen (hyperflexed lithotomy); increases relative anterior-posterior pelvic angle (90% success)
> - Suprapubic pressure to displace the anterior shoulder
> - Internal manoeuvres: rotate fetal shoulders (180° to make posterior shoulder anterior), or deliver posterior shoulder first
> - Roll mother to all fours if these fail
> - Other measures: maternal symphysiotomy, Zavanelli manoeuvre (replace baby into uterus + C-section)
> - If baby dies before delivery: cut through both clavicles to allow delivery
> - Check baby for damage (e.g. Erb's palsy, fractured clavicle); beware maternal PPH or 3rd/4th degree tears

**Complications**
- Mother: PPH in 11%, perineal tears in 3.8%
- Baby: brachial plexus injuries 4–16% (1:2300 live births UK) — 10% left with permanent disability
- Common cause of litigation — parents need counselling about this risk for vaginal deliveries, and that C-section is not an option once shoulder dystocia occurs

## Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH)

**D**: blood loss >500mL after vaginal delivery.
- Primary PPH: within 24h of delivery
- Secondary PPH: >24h after delivery
- Massive obstetric haemorrhage: >1000–1500mL loss

> [!info] Causes of PPH — the 4 Ts
> - **T**one: uterine atony (90%)
> - **T**issue: retained products of conception (more associated with secondary PPH)
> - **T**rauma: genital tract trauma
> - **T**hrombin: clotting disorders

**R**: previous PPH or retained placenta, BMI >35, maternal Hb <85 before labour, antepartum haemorrhage, multiparity >4, maternal age >35, uterine malformations, large placenta/uterus, placental abruption, labour complications.

**Mx**
- Book mothers with risk factors for obstetric unit delivery
- Call for help — all hands on deck
- Resuscitate — ABC; intubate if ↓consciousness; crossmatch 4–6 units + transfuse 1 unit packed RBC to 1 unit FFP
- Deliver placenta; empty uterus of clots or retained tissue
- Massage uterus to generate contraction, or bimanual compression
- Drugs to contract uterus: **oxytocin 10 IU IM/IV is the first-line uterotonic in Australian practice** (per Safer Care Victoria), with **carbetocin 100mcg IM/IV** increasingly used as an alternative for all births (not just caesarean) where cost is comparable, given evidence of a reduced need for additional uterotonics and lower blood loss compared with oxytocin alone; ergometrine, misoprostol, and carboprost remain options for ongoing/refractory atony per escalating local protocol — check current local massive transfusion/PPH pathway for the specific escalation sequence used. See [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]] Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP) for the general AU-verified ratio-based product delivery framework this pathway draws on, not repeated here.
- Repair vaginal or cervical tears
- If bleeding continues: exam under anaesthesia ± laparotomy; insertion of intrauterine balloon tamponade
- May require subtotal or total hysterectomy

**P**: PPH remains a leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide — global estimates attribute roughly a quarter of all maternal deaths to obstetric haemorrhage (~70,000 deaths annually, disproportionately in low- and middle-income countries). Australian maternal mortality from PPH specifically is low in absolute terms given ready access to blood products and tertiary obstetric care, but PPH remains a leading preventable contributor to severe maternal morbidity here too — the priority is timely recognition and the escalating drug/procedural sequence above, not a specific mortality figure.

## Uterine rupture

**D**: complete division of all 3 layers of the uterus (endometrium, myometrium, perimetrium). Uterine dehiscence = incomplete division not penetrating all layers.

**R**: previous C-section (the leading risk factor internationally, via dehiscence of the caesarean scar — the original note's "~70%" figure was specifically UK-derived; the general principle that prior caesarean is the dominant risk factor holds internationally, but the exact proportion should not be assumed to transfer directly), previous uterine rupture, previous cervical/uterine surgery, induction of labour.

**S/smx**: abnormal fetal heart rate; sudden-onset abdominal pain (may be masked by epidural); ± vaginal bleeding; baby may be partially extruded through rupture; haematuria if rupture extends into bladder; haemodynamic instability (may not have much visible vaginal blood — mostly intraperitoneal); changes in contraction patterns; postpartum pain + persistent vaginal bleeding despite uterotonics ± haematuria.

**Mx**
- Stabilise patient (resuscitate)
- Notify anaesthesia, neonatology
- Crossmatch 6 units + prep for fast transfusion
- Laparotomy — hysterectomy if no choice, or attempt uterine repair; ± bladder repair if trauma present

**P**: maternal mortality 5%, fetal mortality 30%.

## DIC & coagulation defects

**D**: acquired syndrome characterised by activation of coagulation pathways, resulting in intravascular thrombi formation and depletion of platelets and coagulation factors.

**A**: disease states triggering systemic coagulation activation — e.g. obstetric disorders, sepsis.

**P**: continuous generation of intravascular fibrin and consumption of procoagulants/platelets; mutual potentiation between inflammatory and coagulation pathways.

**S/smx**: oliguria, ↓BP, ↑HR, purpura fulminans, gangrene, acral cyanosis, petechiae, ecchymosis, oozing, haematuria, delirium, coma.

> [!info] Diagnostic scoring/criteria
> - ↓platelets: moderate <100 x10⁹/L, severe <50 x10⁹/L
> - ↑fibrin degradation products (e.g. D-dimer)
> - ↑prothrombin time: moderate >3s but <6s, severe >6s
> - ↓fibrinogen <2.94 mmol/L
> - Blood film may show schistocytes (microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia)

**Mx**: treat underlying cause ASAP; manage shock (O2, IV fluids); blood products — tranexamic acid, vitamin K (especially if prolonged PT), platelets, fibrinogen concentrate.

**P**: <1% mortality if due to placental abruption; 50–80% mortality if due to infection.

## Amniotic fluid embolism

**D**: condition arising from entry of amniotic fluid or fetal cells into maternal circulation.

**R**: multiple pregnancy, maternal age >35, C-section, instrumentation, eclampsia, polyhydramnios, placental abruption, uterine rupture, induction of labour.

**A/P**: likely immune-mediated (over)reaction to fetal cells; likened to an anaphylactic-like response.

**S/smx**: dyspnoea, chest pain, hypoxia, respiratory arrest → ARDS (may be first sign); hypotension; fetal distress; seizures (20%); ↓consciousness; cardiac arrest. Almost all develop DIC within 48h.

**Mx**
- Prevent death from respiratory failure: high-flow 100% O2; call anaesthetist ASAP (intubation/ventilation may be necessary); transfer to ICU ASAP
- CPR if indicated; deliver baby via C-section (aids maternal resuscitation)
- Monitor for fetal distress
- If hypotensive: IV fluids, dobutamine; pulmonary artery catheterisation to guide Mx; after initial hypotension corrected, give only maintenance fluid required (avoid pulmonary oedema from ARDS)
- Manage DIC with fresh whole blood, packed RBC, or FFP

**P**: most mortality occurs within the first hour; 26–61% mortality. If mother survives, 85% have neurological damage. Request autopsy ASAP.
