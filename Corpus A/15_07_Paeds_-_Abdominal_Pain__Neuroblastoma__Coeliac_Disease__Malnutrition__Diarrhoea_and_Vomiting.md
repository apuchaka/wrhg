## Abdominal pain — differential diagnosis

> [!info] Acute abdominal pain
> Appendicitis; gastroenteritis; viral illness (e.g. URTI) associated with mesenteric adenitis; constipation; UTI; intussusception; torted testis in boys; in older girls — consider menses, ovarian cyst, ovarian torsion, **PID (pelvic inflammatory disease)**, ectopic pregnancy.

> [!info] Recurrent abdominal pain
> ≥10% of children >5 years old suffer recurrent abdominal pain, with no organic cause in 90%. Carries a 4× increased risk of psychological problems in adult life.
> - Functional abdominal pain: episodic or continuous pain ≥1×/week for ≥2 months, with no evidence of organic disorder.
> - GI disorders: coeliac disease, GORD, IBS, lactose intolerance.
> - In children with pica, check FBC, iron, ferritin, and lead levels.
> - Psychogenic: stress, depression, family problems, non-accidental injury.

> [!info] Rare but serious causes
> Volvulus; Meckel's diverticulum; pancreatitis; diabetes; PUD/*H. pylori* infection; IBD; abdominal TB; renal stones/gallstones; cholecystitis; Henoch-Schönlein purpura.

> [!danger] Red flags: weight loss, faltering growth; fever; GI bleeding; bilious vomiting, lots of vomiting; chronic severe diarrhoea; gross abdominal distention; palpable mass or organomegaly; specific tender area; oral ulcers, perianal fistulas, family history of IBD; back pain.

### GI obstruction, ascites, masses and organomegaly

> [!info] Causes of abdominal distention
> - **Air:** faecal impaction; air swallowing; malabsorption.
> - **Ascites:** nephrotic syndrome; hypoproteinaemia; cirrhosis; heart failure.
> - **Masses:** Wilms' tumour; neuroblastoma; adrenal tumour.
> - **Cysts:** polycystic kidney; hepatic cysts; dermoid cysts.

> [!info] Causes of hepatomegaly
> Infections (mono, CMV, malaria); malignancy (leukaemia, lymphoma, neuroblastoma); metabolic (Gaucher and Hurler diseases, cystinosis, galactosaemia); others (sickle cell disease, haemolytic anaemias, porphyria).

> [!info] Causes of splenomegaly
> As for hepatomegaly, except neuroblastoma.

### Investigation and management of abdominal pain/distention

- Ix: "dip the urine". Investigate red flags ± stool for occult blood, US, FBC + CRP/ESR, TTG+IgA, amylase/lipase, TFTs (if constipated), barium studies, and consider specialist referral.
- **Mx if not sinister:** trial of laxative or antacid if appropriate; simple analgesia and antispasmodics; avoid dietary interventions unless there is a positive history.

## Neuroblastoma

- **D:** malignant tumour arising from the embryological neural crest element of the peripheral sympathetic nervous system — most often at the adrenal medulla.
- Causes 7–8% of childhood cancer; median age of onset 20 months.
- **S/Smx:** abdominal swelling; pallor, weight loss, bone pain, limp, hepatomegaly, paraplegia, proptosis — these tend to result from metastasis to lymph nodes, scalp, or bone.
- **Ix:** ↑catecholamines in urine. Abdominal XR: calcification. Biopsy for confirmation.
- **Mx:** refer to a specialist centre. Excision if possible + chemotherapy.
- **P:** depends on genotype. Those <1 year old do best.

## Coeliac disease in children

- **D:** enteropathy induced by gluten (found in wheat, barley, rye).
- **S/Smx:** classically presents between 6–24 months, after introduction of gluten to the diet. Diarrhoea, anorexia, abdominal pain and distention, faltering growth ± anaemia. Signs are less obvious in older children — a higher level of suspicion is required.
- Associated with other autoimmune disorders, including T1DM, thyroid disease, JIA; also Down syndrome and Turner syndrome.
- **Ix:** as per [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Coeliac Disease — IgA-tTG plus total IgA, then duodenal biopsy; not repeated here.
  - **The one point that must not be lost in the hand-off: the child has to stay on a gluten-containing diet for ≥6 weeks before testing, or the serology is falsely negative.** This matters more in children than adults because parents frequently start a gluten-free diet before presenting, and the test then reassures wrongly. - **Mx:** dietary exclusion of gluten. Consider other DDx of malabsorption.

## Diarrhoea & vomiting

### Gastroenteritis

- Rotavirus is the most common cause, and can cause significant mortality. Also norovirus, astrovirus, adenovirus.
- Diarrhoea lasts ~5–7 days, stops within 2 weeks. Vomiting lasts ~1–2 days, stops within 3 days.

#### Initial management

- Weigh to monitor progress and quantify dehydration.
- Start oral rehydration salts (ORS): aim 1mL/kg every 5 minutes, continue breastfeeding. Encourage fluid intake if older; discourage fruit juices and carbonated drinks.

> [!info] Indications for stool culture: septicaemia suspected; blood/mucus in stool; immunocompromised child; recent travel abroad; diarrhoea not improved by day 7; uncertain about diagnosis.

- **Mx of severe dehydration or shock:** admit for fluids and antiemetics.

### Chronic diarrhoea

- Most common cause is cow's milk intolerance.
- Also: coeliac disease; post-gastroenteritis lactose intolerance (generally resolves after ~7 weeks).

## Malnutrition

> [!info] Marasmus: manifestation of severe protein-energy malnutrition occurring as a result of total calorie deficiency.

> [!info] Kwashiorkor: manifestation of severe protein-energy malnutrition associated with a poor-quality diet high in carbohydrates but low in protein content (the child may have sufficient total calorie intake).

- **S/Smx:** weight-for-height value ≤3× average deviation; mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) <115mm. Specific to Kwashiorkor: oedema, dermatosis, depigmentation of hair, cheilosis (swelling and fissuring of lips).

> [!info] Physiologic response to starvation
> 1. GI absorption of substrate (1–6h).
> 2. Glycogenolysis (1–2 days).
> 3. Gluconeogenesis (1 week).
> 4. Ketosis (3–4 days+).
> 5. ↑cerebral ketone use (2 weeks+).

### Management

1. **Resuscitation & stabilisation** — rehydrate + prevent infections, avoid re-feeding syndrome. Lasts ~1 week; patients can be very frail. Rehydrate with warmed IV fluids (isotonic). 60–80% of caloric requirement for age, continuous NG feeding at night or small meals at night time, phosphate + vitamin B1. Monitor closely for refeeding syndrome (arrhythmias, weakness, confusion, etc).
2. **Nutritional rehabilitation** — slow increase in caloric intake; vaccinations, ↑motor activity. Lasts 2–6 weeks.
3. **Follow up, prevent recurrence** — education, encourage breastfeeding and supplemental feeds; educate especially on high protein sources of food.

> [!info] DDx: HIV wasting syndrome (involuntary loss of >10% weight with no other explainable cause); chronic pancreatitis (secondary to Coxsackie B virus, mumps, trauma, cystic fibrosis, etc).
