## 0.1 Hypothyroidism

**D:** clinical disorder secondary to decreased T3 and T4.

**Causes of primary hypothyroidism:**
- Hashimoto's (autoimmune) — anti-thyroid peroxidase and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies; associated with other autoimmune disorders
- Subacute thyroiditis (de Quervain's) — thought to occur after viral infections; 4 phases: 1st phase (3–6 weeks) with ↑T, painful goitre, ↑ESR; 2nd phase euthyroid; 3rd phase ↓T; 4th phase normal. Scan shows ↓uptake of iodine-131. Usually self-limiting.
- Riedel thyroiditis — chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the thyroid gland
- Post-thyroidectomy or post-radioiodine treatment
- Post-partum thyroiditis
- Drug therapy (e.g. lithium, amiodarone, anti-thyroid drugs)
- Iodine deficiency (dietary)

**Causes of secondary hypothyroidism** (don't directly affect the thyroid gland): Down's syndrome, Turner's syndrome, coeliac disease

**S/Smx** (body slows down):
- General: weight gain, lethargy, cold intolerance
- Skin: dry, cold skin; non-pitting oedema (hands/face — can present as periorbital oedema); dry, coarse scalp hair; loss of the lateral aspect of the eyebrows

> [!info] Gap-filled — "non-pitting" appears three times across this file's hypothyroid/myxoedema content (here, in pretibial myxoedema below, and in Myxoedema Coma below) with no mechanism ever given for why this specific oedema doesn't pit, unlike the overwhelming majority of oedema causes covered elsewhere in this project (heart failure, renal, hepatic, venous). **Mechanism:** ordinary oedema (pitting) is simple excess *fluid* accumulating in the interstitial space, which can be temporarily displaced by finger pressure, leaving a transient indentation. **Myxoedema is fundamentally different** — hypothyroidism causes accumulation of **glycosaminoglycans (GAGs, particularly hyaluronic acid)** in the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, thought to result from reduced GAG clearance/breakdown in the hypothyroid state. These GAGs are hydrophilic and osmotically draw water into the tissue, but the water becomes bound within a thickened, gel-like GAG-rich matrix rather than existing as free interstitial fluid — this matrix doesn't displace under pressure the way free fluid does, so **finger pressure leaves no persistent indentation**, distinguishing it clinically from ordinary fluid-based oedema. This is the same underlying process (just occurring in different locations and different underlying thyroid states) as the pretibial myxoedema associated with Graves' disease below, not repeated here.

- GI: constipation
- Gynae: menorrhagia
- Neuro: carpal tunnel syndrome

> [!info] Gap-filled — **Woltman's sign** (delayed relaxation phase of a deep tendon reflex, classically the ankle/Achilles reflex — a "hung-up" or "slow-relaxing" reflex) is a genuinely classic hypothyroidism finding that was completely absent above. **Mechanism:** thyroid hormone normally supports rapid calcium reuptake into the sarcoplasmic reticulum after a muscle contraction, along with adequate myosin ATPase activity and ATP turnover — hypothyroidism slows both of these processes, so the *contraction* phase of the reflex remains relatively preserved while the **relaxation phase is specifically and disproportionately prolonged**, producing the "hung-up" quality on examination (the foot returns to resting position noticeably slower than normal, rather than a simple diminished/absent reflex). This is genuinely a useful examination finding to actively test for, not just a historical curiosity — though it isn't specific to hypothyroidism alone, given a similar delayed-relaxation pattern is also described in anorexia nervosa, advanced age, and hypothermia, so it should be interpreted alongside the other findings above rather than in isolation.

**Ix:** TFTs (*why:* the diagnostic test — establishes both the diagnosis and whether it's primary or secondary based on the TSH/T4 pattern; *what:* primary hypothyroidism shows ↑TSH with ↓free T4; secondary (pituitary/hypothalamic) hypothyroidism shows inappropriately low/normal TSH despite ↓free T4 — this pattern distinction is important, since secondary hypothyroidism warrants pituitary/hypothalamic investigation rather than simply starting levothyroxine and monitoring TSH the way primary disease is managed). Anti-thyroid peroxidase and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies if Hashimoto's suspected (*why:* confirms the autoimmune aetiology, the most common cause; *what:* positive in the majority of Hashimoto's cases).

> [!info] Verified — the levothyroxine starting doses, cautious titration in IHD, 8–12 week TFT recheck interval, and TSH target (0.5–2.5 mU/L) reflect standard international endocrine practice rather than a UK-specific protocol; carbimazole (not methimazole) is confirmed as the standard first-line antithyroid drug in Australia too, consistent with the Graves' Mx below. No material correction needed to the figures in this section.
> **Mx:** levothyroxine 50–100 mcg OD (if >50yo with IHD, start with 25 mcg OD and slowly titrate up). Check TFTs 8–12 weeks after any dose change. Goal is to normalise TSH — aim for 0.5–2.5 mU/L. In pregnancy, increase dose by ≥25–50 mcg, monitor TSH. **Practical administration point (an Australian endocrinology source specifically emphasises this):** take on an empty stomach with water only, at least 30 minutes before food, separately from other medications — absorption is significantly affected by food/other drugs, which is a common cause of apparently "resistant" hypothyroidism that's actually an absorption/adherence issue.

**Levothyroxine SE:** hyperthyroidism, ↓bone mineral density (may need calcium + vitamin D supplementation), worsening angina, AF.

> [!warning] In amiodarone-induced hypothyroidism, continue amiodarone + give levothyroxine.

> [!danger] Interactions: iron and calcium carbonate decrease levothyroxine absorption — give ≥4h apart. Levothyroxine is safe in breastfeeding.

### 0.1.1 Subclinical Hypothyroidism
= ↑TSH but normal T3, T4 + no obvious symptoms. Possible in patients non-compliant with levothyroxine.

> [!info] Verified — the TSH thresholds below (treat if >10, individualise 5.5–10 based on symptoms/age) reflect broad international consensus rather than a UK-specific set of numbers; multiple international sources converge on similar thresholds (treatment more clearly indicated >10 mIU/L, individualised judgment below that based on symptoms, age, and risk factors). No material correction needed.
> **Mx:**
> - TSH >10 mU/L on 2 occasions 3 months apart (T3/T4 normal) → consider offering levothyroxine
> - TSH 5.5–10 mU/L on 2 occasions 3 months apart + symptomatic + <65yo → consider a 6-month trial of levothyroxine
> - TSH 5.5–10 mU/L in older people → watch and wait
> - TSH 5.5–10 mU/L + asymptomatic → observe, repeat TFT in 6 months

### 0.1.2 Sick Euthyroid Syndrome
= non-thyroidal illness with ↓TSH, ↓T3, ↓T4. Most of the time TSH is roughly in the normal range ("inappropriate", since TSH should be high if T3/T4 are low). Usually a result of systemic illness. No treatment needed if it reverses upon recovery.

### 0.1.3 Myxoedema Coma
= extremely decompensated hypothyroidism.

**S/Smx:** confusion, hypothermia.

**Ix:** TFTs (*why:* confirms severe hypothyroidism as the cause; *what:* markedly deranged, often with very low T4); cortisol (*why:* screens for coexisting adrenal insufficiency, since giving thyroid hormone alone without covering a coexisting cortisol deficiency can precipitate an adrenal crisis — this is why the Mx below always pairs thyroid replacement with empirical corticosteroid cover; *what:* may be low if coexisting adrenal insufficiency present); U&Es, glucose, blood cultures/septic screen (*why:* myxoedema coma is often precipitated by an intercurrent illness/infection, which itself needs identifying and treating; *what:* may reveal the precipitant and associated metabolic derangements — e.g. hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** IV thyroid replacement; IV corticosteroids given empirically alongside (possibility of coexisting adrenal insufficiency — don't wait for cortisol results, given how dangerous unopposed thyroid replacement can be in this scenario); IV fluids, correct electrolytes; identify and treat any precipitating illness.
- **Chronic/long-term:** transition to oral levothyroxine replacement once stabilised, with ongoing monitoring as for standard hypothyroidism Mx above.

## 0.2 Hyperthyroidism

**D:** clinical state secondary to increased T3 and T4. "Thyrotoxicosis" is an interchangeable term.

**Causes:**
- Graves' disease (most common)
- Toxic nodular goitre
- Postpartum thyroiditis
- Gestational hyperthyroidism
- TSH-producing pituitary adenoma
- Thyroid cancers (don't usually cause hyper- or hypothyroidism)
- Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism (diet, contrast, drugs e.g. amiodarone)

**S/Smx:** weight loss, sweating, heat intolerance, tremor, irritability; palpitations ± AF; diarrhoea; oligomenorrhoea.

> [!info] Gap-filled — lid lag/lid retraction was mentioned only as a bare term elsewhere in this project (in the Ocular Manifestations reference), never explained, and never distinguished from exophthalmos despite the two being genuinely different processes that are easy to conflate as "the same thyroid eye thing." **Lid lag/retraction: sympathetic overactivity, not autoimmune.** Hyperthyroidism's generalised increase in sympathetic/adrenergic tone causes overactivity of **Müller's muscle** — a smooth muscle assisting upper eyelid elevation, under sympathetic control — producing lid retraction at rest and a visible lag of the upper lid behind the globe during downward eye movement. **This occurs in *any* cause of thyrotoxicosis** (Graves', toxic nodular goitre, thyroiditis, exogenous excess), given it simply reflects generalised sympathetic overactivity, not an eye-specific disease process — it resolves once the hyperthyroid state itself is corrected. This is genuinely distinct from **exophthalmos/proptosis**, which is specifically an autoimmune process (retro-orbital glycosaminoglycan and collagen deposition — see Thyroid Eye Disease in [[05_Ophthalmology]], not repeated here) occurring specifically in Graves' disease, and which — importantly — **doesn't reliably correlate with or resolve alongside thyroid hormone status**, unlike lid lag. Confusing the two matters clinically: lid lag in a hyperthyroid patient of any cause is expected and non-specific, while exophthalmos should prompt specific consideration of Graves'-associated thyroid eye disease and its own distinct, independently-progressing management pathway.

> [!note] Amiodarone can cause both hyper- and hypothyroidism.

### 0.2.1 Graves' Disease

**D:** autoimmune thyroid condition associated with hyperthyroidism — antibodies to the TSH receptor (90%) and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (75%).

**R:** family history, F>M (6:1), tobacco use

**Features specific to Graves':**
- Eyes (30%): exophthalmos and ophthalmoplegia (see Ophthalmology section) — smoking increases risk of eye disease
- Pretibial myxoedema — scaly, lumpy, non-pitting skin on the shins
- Thyroid acropachy — digital clubbing, soft tissue swelling of hands and feet, periosteal new bone formation

**Ix:** thyroid scintigraphy scan (*why:* distinguishes Graves' disease from other causes of hyperthyroidism by its characteristic uptake pattern, particularly useful when clinical features alone don't clearly indicate Graves'; *what:* diffuse, homogeneous, increased uptake of iodine-131 — contrast with the patchy uptake of toxic multinodular goitre below). TSH receptor antibodies (*why:* a specific serological test supporting the diagnosis, particularly useful when scintigraphy is unavailable or contraindicated e.g. in pregnancy; *what:* positive in ~90% of Graves' patients).

> [!info] Verified — carbimazole (not methimazole) is confirmed as the standard first-line antithyroid drug in Australia, and the propranolol-for-symptoms → carbimazole → radioiodine stepwise sequence reflects standard international practice, not a UK-specific protocol. No material correction needed.
> **Mx:**
> - **Immediate/acute (symptom control):** propranolol for symptom control while awaiting specialist review/definitive Mx.
> - **Definitive:** refer to endocrine; likely to start carbimazole if symptoms not controlled with propranolol; if still refractory, radioiodine therapy may be considered (contraindicated if pregnant or <16yo); surgery (thyroidectomy) is a further definitive option for select patients (e.g. large goitre, patient preference, or when antithyroid drugs/radioiodine are contraindicated/declined).
> - **Chronic/long-term:** patients who undergo radioiodine or surgery will typically become hypothyroid and require lifelong levothyroxine replacement (see Hypothyroidism above) — this is an expected outcome of definitive treatment, not a complication, and should be explained to patients as part of informed consent for these options.

**Anti-thyroid drugs (e.g. carbimazole):** MOA — inhibits thyroid peroxidase.

> [!danger] SE: agranulocytosis — warn patients to watch for signs of infection.

### 0.2.2 Toxic Multinodular Goitre
= multiple autonomously functioning thyroid nodules.

**Ix:** thyroid scintigraphy scan (*why:* distinguishes this from Graves' disease by the uptake pattern; *what:* patchy, heterogeneous uptake — contrast with the diffuse uptake of Graves' above).

**Mx:** radioiodine therapy is the mainstay definitive treatment — carbimazole can be used for interim symptom control but autonomous nodules don't remit the way Graves' disease can, so long-term antithyroid drug therapy alone isn't a definitive strategy the way it can be trialled in Graves'.

### 0.2.3 Thyroid Storm (Thyrotoxic Crisis)
Life-threatening hyperthyroidism, precipitated by surgery, trauma, infection or acute illness, acute iodine overload (e.g. contrast).

**S/Smx:** fever (>38.5°C); CNS dysfunction (agitation, delirium, psychosis, lethargy, seizure, coma); ↑HR ± AF, heart failure; GI dysfunction (nausea/vomiting/diarrhoea, abdominal pain, unexplained jaundice).

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (often using a scoring system such as the **Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale**, which weights thermoregulatory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal-hepatic and central nervous system dysfunction plus the presence of a precipitant — **the intern-level point is that it supports a clinical decision already made on the bedside picture, and a low score does not exclude thyroid storm in a patient who looks like one**; the full weighted scoring is specialist territory and is not reproduced here) supported by TFTs (*why:* thyroid storm is primarily a clinical diagnosis given the need for immediate treatment — waiting for TFT confirmation shouldn't delay Mx if the clinical picture is convincing; *what:* markedly elevated T3/T4 with suppressed TSH, though the degree of biochemical derangement doesn't necessarily correlate with clinical severity).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** IV propranolol (β-blockade) for symptomatic/haemodynamic control; antithyroid drugs — **carbimazole (or propylthiouracil, particularly in pregnancy given carbimazole's teratogenicity, or if hepatotoxicity is a specific concern)** — corrected here from the source's "methimazole," which is the equivalent US drug name for the same active moiety carbimazole is converted to in the body, but carbimazole is the actual product prescribed in Australia and the UK; dexamethasone (blocks conversion of T4 to T3, and also covers possible coexisting adrenal insufficiency, similar to the rationale in myxoedema coma above); Lugol's iodine (given at least 1 hour after starting antithyroid drugs, to avoid providing substrate for new hormone synthesis before the antithyroid drug has blocked the synthetic pathway); symptomatic treatment (antipyretics avoiding aspirin, which displaces thyroid hormone from binding proteins and can worsen the picture — paracetamol preferred); treat the precipitating event.
- **Chronic/long-term:** once stabilised, transition to standard Graves'/hyperthyroidism Mx pathway above, including consideration of definitive therapy to prevent recurrence.

## 0.3 Thyroid Cancers

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Papillary carcinoma (70%)** | F>M (3:1), young patients, good prognosis. Cancer of epithelial follicular cells, seldom encapsulated. Metastasises usually via lymph nodes, rarely haematogenously. |
| **Follicular adenoma** | Benign encapsulated tumour, usually a solitary thyroid nodule; requires histology to exclude malignancy. |
| **Follicular carcinoma (20%)** | Malignant transformation of follicular adenoma; capsular invasion (usually vascular); multifocal disease rare. |
| **Medullary carcinoma (5%)** | Malignancy of parafollicular C cells (neural crest derived) — secretes calcitonin. Associated with MEN2 (~20% familial). Nodal disease is associated with very poor prognosis. |
| **Anaplastic carcinoma (1%)** | Aka undifferentiated thyroid carcinoma. Highly aggressive, very poor prognosis, most common in elderly females. Mx: resection where possible, palliation via isthmusectomy and radiotherapy (chemo not effective). |
| **Thyroid lymphoma (rare)** | Associated with Hashimoto's. Primary — affects thyroid first, then LN, then other organs (B-cell lymphoma most common). Secondary — affects LN then other organs then thyroid. |

## 0.4 Goitre

**D:** neck swelling due to a thyroid issue. 5–12% malignant; usually benign and asymptomatic. Both hyper- and hypothyroidism can cause goitre.

**Dx:** establish whether the mass is intra- or extra-thyroidal — US + examination. Evaluate lymph nodes also. The thyroid moves with swallowing.

**Ix:** TFTs (*why:* establishes whether the goitre is associated with hyper-, hypo-, or euthyroid status, which narrows the underlying cause; *what:* may be deranged in either direction depending on cause, or normal in a simple/multinodular goitre). Thyroid US (*why:* characterises the goitre's structure — diffuse vs multinodular vs single dominant nodule — and identifies any nodule features raising malignancy concern warranting biopsy; *what:* diffuse enlargement suggests Graves'/Hashimoto's, multiple nodules suggest multinodular goitre, a single dominant or suspicious nodule warrants fine-needle aspiration). Fine-needle aspiration if a suspicious nodule is identified (*why:* the key test to exclude malignancy in a dominant/suspicious nodule, given the 5–12% malignancy rate above; *what:* cytology guides further Mx — see Thyroid Cancers above for the malignant subtypes this may reveal).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause (see Hypothyroidism, Hyperthyroidism, and Thyroid Cancers above/below for the specific disease-level Mx this feeds into) — goitre itself is a sign, not a standalone disease, so Mx is directed at the cause once identified.
- **Chronic/long-term:** large goitres causing compressive symptoms (dysphagia, dyspnoea, stridor) may need surgical referral for thyroidectomy regardless of the underlying functional status, given the mechanical rather than purely hormonal problem this represents.

## 0.5 Cushing's Syndrome/Disease

**Cushing's syndrome** = clinical state secondary to increased cortisol from any cause.
**Cushing's disease** = secondary to an ACTH-secreting pituitary tumour.

> [!note] Cushing's disease is usually secondary to a pituitary adenoma; carcinomas are rare.

**A:** exogenous (e.g. steroid exposure), ACTH-secreting pituitary tumours, ectopic ACTH production (e.g. SCLC), adrenal adenoma/carcinoma, micronodular adrenal dysplasia (very rare), Carney complex

**P:** ↑cortisol → symptoms

**S/Smx:** facial plethora (moon face); central obesity and violaceous striae; buffalo hump (enlarged fat pad on upper back); proximal limb muscle wasting (proximal myopathy); menstrual irregularities + hirsutism; thin skin (easy bruising, poor healing); mood changes (e.g. irritability). In Cushing's disease specifically: hyperpigmentation of skin.

> [!info] The mechanism behind the fat redistribution pattern — genuinely worth understanding, given it resolves an apparent paradox: cortisol is classically taught as **lipolytic** (fat-breaking-down), so why does Cushing's cause fat *gain* centrally rather than uniform fat loss? The answer is that cortisol's effect is genuinely **depot-specific, not uniform** — it simultaneously promotes **lipolysis in peripheral/subcutaneous fat** (contributing to the thin limbs) while promoting **fat storage (adipogenesis) in central/visceral fat** (driving the central obesity, moon face, and buffalo hump) — two opposite effects happening in different tissue compartments at the same time, not a contradiction. This depot-specific difference is thought to relate to differences in local cortisol-activating enzyme activity (11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1) and glucocorticoid receptor characteristics between visceral and subcutaneous fat, though the full molecular detail remains an active area of research rather than fully settled. **The proximal myopathy** reflects a separate but related catabolic effect: cortisol promotes skeletal muscle protein breakdown, preferentially affecting proximal muscle groups — the same catabolic, tissue-wasting theme that also explains the thin, easily-bruised skin (reduced collagen synthesis) and the violaceous striae (stretched, weakened skin over the expanding central fat).

**Associated medical conditions:** HTN, cardiac hypertrophy, T2DM/hyperglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, osteoporosis

**Ix:**
- VBG/ABG: metabolic alkalosis + ↓K ± hyperglycaemia (ectopic ACTH → markedly ↓K)
- Low-dose dexamethasone suppression test: in normal patients, dexamethasone suppresses the morning cortisol spike; in Cushing's, no suppression occurs
- 9am and midnight ACTH + cortisol: ACTH-dependent cause shows non-suppressed cortisol
- High-dose dexamethasone suppression test: cortisol and ACTH suppressed = Cushing's disease; cortisol and ACTH not suppressed = ectopic ACTH syndrome; cortisol not suppressed but ACTH suppressed = Cushing's syndrome from other causes (e.g. adrenal adenoma)
- 24h urinary free cortisol (2 measurements); bedtime salivary cortisol (2 measurements); CRH stimulation test (pituitary source → cortisol rise; ectopic/adrenal source → no rise); petrosal sinus sampling (differentiates pituitary vs ectopic ACTH); insulin stress test (excludes pseudo-Cushing's)
- Imaging: MRI (pituitary tumours), CT-TAP (adrenal or ectopic source)

**Mx:**
- **Definitive:** resection of the tumour where possible (transsphenoidal surgery for pituitary source, adrenalectomy for adrenal source, resection of the ectopic ACTH-secreting tumour where feasible), or taper down exogenous steroids gradually (not abruptly — see the general principle of steroid withdrawal risking adrenal insufficiency) if iatrogenic.
- **Medical Mx (bridging therapy pending surgery, or for inoperable/persistent disease):** somatostatin analogue (pasireotide); dopamine agonist (cabergoline); steroidogenesis inhibitor (e.g. osilodrostat, ketoconazole); glucocorticoid receptor antagonist (mifepristone).
- **Chronic/long-term:** patients may require post-surgical corticosteroid ± pituitary hormone replacement therapy — clinically guided (monitor BP, check for orthostatic symptoms) — since the chronically suppressed HPA axis from prolonged hypercortisolism doesn't recover instantly after the source is removed, patients can paradoxically develop adrenal insufficiency post-operatively and need covering/monitoring for this.

## 0.6 Addison's Disease (Primary Adrenal Insufficiency)

**D:** decreased cortisol, aldosterone, and DHEA (adrenal androgen) due to disease affecting the adrenal cortex.

**R:** F>>M, thromboembolic or hypercoagulable states (e.g. antiphospholipid syndrome), other autoimmune disease (especially coeliac disease)

**A:** autoimmune (majority), infections (e.g. TB), metastases, infiltrative disease (e.g. amyloidosis), haemorrhagic infarction of the adrenal glands (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome), adrenalectomy. Secondary Addison's (↓ACTH) — pituitary disorders.

**S/Smx:** can be quite non-specific — lethargy, weakness, ↓appetite, nausea/vomiting, weight loss, 'salt-craving'; vitiligo, loss of pubic hair in women, hypotension, hypoglycaemia; hyperpigmentation (primary Addison's only); ± ↓Na, ↑K; Addisonian crisis.

> [!info] Gap-filled — "hyperpigmentation (primary Addison's only)" was stated without explaining the mechanism, even though it's exactly the kind of detail a reader would look for here first. **Mechanism:** in primary adrenal failure, low cortisol removes negative feedback on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, driving markedly elevated **ACTH**. ACTH is cleaved from a larger precursor protein, **pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)** — and POMC also gives rise to **melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH)**, meaning the same precursor molecule that's overproduced to drive ACTH also generates excess MSH-family peptides, which directly stimulate melanocytes and cause hyperpigmentation (classically most visible in sun-exposed areas, skin creases, scars, and the buccal mucosa). **This is precisely why hyperpigmentation is specific to primary Addison's and absent in secondary adrenal insufficiency** (pituitary/ACTH-deficient causes) — with a pituitary cause, ACTH (and its shared MSH-generating precursor) is *low*, not elevated, so this pigmentation-driving mechanism simply isn't present.

**Ix:** ACTH stimulation test (short synacthen test) (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test — directly assesses adrenal cortisol reserve by measuring the response to exogenous ACTH stimulation; *what:* plasma cortisol measured 30 min before and after giving synacthen 150 µg IM — an inadequate rise confirms adrenal insufficiency). Alternatively, 9am cortisol (*why:* a simpler screening test using the physiological morning cortisol peak, useful when synacthen testing isn't immediately available; *what:* >500 nmol/L — Addison's unlikely; <100 nmol/L — Addison's likely; 100–500 nmol/L — equivocal, refer for synacthen test). U&Es (*why:* screens for the characteristic electrolyte/metabolic pattern from combined cortisol and aldosterone deficiency; *what:* ↑K, ↓Na, ↓glucose, metabolic acidosis).

**Mx:** replace adrenal hormones.
- Hydrocortisone, divided into 2–3 doses/day (majority given in the first half of the day)
- Fludrocortisone

> [!info] Verified against an Australian endocrinology source (Medicine Today, authored by Royal Adelaide Hospital endocrinologists) and general international consensus, Aug 2026 — the core "double the dose during illness" sick day principle is universal and matches Australian practice; adding AU-relevant specifics not in the original UK-style note.
> **Sick day rules:** double to triple the usual hydrocortisone dose during illness (specific multiplier depends on illness severity — minor illness/fever typically 2–3x, more severe illness/inability to tolerate oral intake warrants escalation to injectable hydrocortisone), fludrocortisone dose stays the same (its mineralocorticoid effect is adequately covered by the increased hydrocortisone dose during stress dosing); drink plenty of fluids. Patients should have (and be trained to self-administer) an emergency hydrocortisone injection kit for use if vomiting prevents oral absorption — this is standard practice in Australia as internationally, not a UK-specific addition.

> [!danger] DO NOT MISS DOSES. Patients may need hydrocortisone for injection in case of adrenal crisis.

### 0.6.1 Addisonian Crisis
= acute exacerbation of adrenal insufficiency, usually resulting from stress on the body (e.g. infection) where cortisol ± aldosterone requirements exceed supply. Can also result from abrupt steroid withdrawal after long-term therapy.

**S/Smx:** shock, collapse, fever.

> [!info] Verified — the core drug doses below (100mg hydrocortisone stat, ongoing 50mg qds or equivalent continuous infusion, fluid resuscitation) reflect standard international practice and are consistent with what's cited in Australian sources (e.g. the Medicine Today Royal Adelaide Hospital reference, which notes a similar ~50mg loading-dose order of magnitude) — this is not meaningfully a UK-specific protocol, and no material correction is needed to the figures below.
> **Mx:**
> - Hydrocortisone 100mg IM/IV stat
> - Then hydrocortisone 50mg qds or 200mg/24h IV until stable
> - 0.9% NaCl infusion (continuous, monitor BP, urine output)
> - Monitor and treat hypoglycaemia (dextrose IV if needed)
> - PO hydrocortisone replacement 24h after, taper to maintenance dose over 3–4 days

**Prevention:** carry a steroid card, ensure doctors/nurses know the patient is on steroids and needs replacement doses if unwell.

## 0.7 Hyperaldosteronism

**A:** bilateral idiopathic adrenal hyperplasia (60–70% of cases), Conn syndrome (adrenal adenoma), unilateral hyperplasia, familial hyperaldosteronism, adrenal carcinoma (rare)

**P:** ↑aldosterone → increased Na reabsorption via kidneys, decreased K and H.

> [!info] Gap-filled — a genuinely counterintuitive point worth addressing explicitly: if aldosterone drives sodium and water retention, why does primary hyperaldosteronism cause **hypertension without the peripheral oedema** seen in other sodium-retaining states (heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis)? **This is the "aldosterone escape" phenomenon**: aldosterone does cause an initial phase of sodium and water retention and volume expansion — but as blood volume and blood pressure rise, this triggers counter-regulatory mechanisms (increased renal perfusion pressure driving pressure natriuresis, and release of atrial natriuretic peptide) that promote renal sodium excretion **despite ongoing aldosterone excess**. Sodium balance re-establishes at a new, expanded steady state within days, rather than progressively accumulating — hypertension persists (reflecting this sustained higher-volume baseline plus aldosterone's direct vascular effects), but oedema doesn't develop, because the "escape" mechanism prevents the runaway volume accumulation that produces oedema in other conditions. This contrasts with states like heart failure, where persistently reduced *effective* circulating volume (despite total-body volume overload) keeps driving RAAS activation without a comparable escape, allowing oedema to develop and progress.

**S/Smx:** treatment-resistant HTN; ↓K ± metabolic alkalosis (muscle weakness, ECG changes — more common with Conn's).

**Ix:** plasma aldosterone-renin ratio (*why:* the first-line screening test, since it's a ratio rather than an absolute level it's more robust to some confounders and directly reflects the pathophysiology (autonomous aldosterone production suppressing renin); *what:* ↑aldosterone + ↓renin). CT abdomen + adrenal vein sampling (*why:* CT identifies a structural lesion (adenoma) but cannot reliably distinguish functional unilateral from bilateral disease — adrenal vein sampling is often needed to confirm lateralisation before committing a patient to unilateral adrenalectomy, since imaging alone can be misleading (e.g. a non-functioning incidentaloma on the "wrong" side); *what:* confirms whether the source is unilateral (surgical candidate) or bilateral (medical Mx candidate) — this distinction is the single most important determinant of Mx below.

**Mx:**
- **Definitive:** tumour/unilateral disease (confirmed by adrenal vein sampling) — adrenalectomy.
- **Chronic/long-term:** bilateral hyperplasia — aldosterone antagonist (e.g. spironolactone) as definitive medical Mx, since surgery isn't curative for bilateral disease; ongoing BP and electrolyte monitoring.

## 0.8 Acromegaly

**D:** disease resulting from excess growth hormone. Gigantism = onset in childhood, before epiphyseal closure.

> [!info] Gap-filled — the timing rule ("before epiphyseal closure") was stated without explaining why it produces such different presentations from the same underlying excess. **Mechanism:** growth hormone (via IGF-1) acts on the epiphyseal growth plates — cartilage that permits longitudinal bone growth — but these plates **fuse and are replaced by solid bone once puberty completes**, permanently removing this growth pathway. Before fusion (childhood/adolescence), excess GH drives the growth plates to keep proliferating, producing genuinely increased **linear/longitudinal growth — gigantism** (excessive height). After fusion (adulthood), the growth plates are gone and can't respond to GH this way — but GH/IGF-1 can still act on tissues that retain growth capacity throughout life: cartilage, connective tissue, and the soft tissues of the hands, feet, jaw, and face, producing the **appositional (width-wise) bone growth and soft tissue overgrowth of acromegaly** (coarsening features, enlarging hands/feet/jaw) rather than increased height. Same hormone, same excess — the presentation differs entirely based on whether the growth plates are still a viable target.

**A:** pituitary somatotroph adenoma (95%), with ↑prolactin in 25%; associated with MEN-1 in 6% of patients.

> [!info] S/Smx — mnemonic "ABCDEFGHIJ"
> **A**rthropathy (painful joints), **B**ig spade-like hands, **C**oarse features & carpal tunnel syndrome, **D**iabetes, **E**nlarged tongue/heart/throat, **F**ield defects (bitemporal hemianopia), **G**ynaecomastia/galactorrhoea (secondary to ↑prolactin) & greasy skin, **H**ypertension, **I**ncreasing shoe/hat size, **J**aw enlargement ± prognathism

**Ix:** serum IGF-1 levels (*why:* the first-line screening test — IGF-1 reflects integrated GH secretion over time and is more stable than a single GH measurement, which fluctuates significantly through the day; *what:* elevated in acromegaly). OGTT to confirm diagnosis if ↑IGF-1 (*why:* the definitive confirmatory test, exploiting the normal physiological suppression of GH by glucose — a response lost in autonomous GH-secreting tumours; *what:* in normal patients OGTT suppresses GH (<2 mU/L), in acromegaly no suppression occurs ± impaired glucose tolerance is often revealed as a secondary finding, consistent with the "D" in the mnemonic above). MRI brain (*why:* localises the pituitary tumour once the biochemical diagnosis is confirmed, essential for surgical planning; *what:* identifies the somatotroph adenoma, often also revealing its size/relationship to the optic chiasm — relevant to the bitemporal hemianopia risk in the mnemonic above).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive:** tumour resection (transsphenoidal surgery) first-line.
- **Medical Mx (if inoperable, surgery contraindicated, or persistent disease post-surgery):** somatostatin analogue (↓GH, e.g. octreotide); GH receptor antagonist (e.g. pegvisomant subcutaneous); dopamine agonists (e.g. bromocriptine — not particularly effective).
- **Chronic/long-term:** rarely, external irradiation for persistent/recurrent disease; ongoing monitoring of IGF-1 and screening for the associated complications the mnemonic above implies (diabetes, hypertension, cardiac effects, joint disease) given these often persist or only partially improve even after biochemical control is achieved.

## 0.9 Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia (MEN)

Autosomal dominant disorder affecting various endocrine organs.

> [!info] MEN-1 ("3 P's") — secondary to MEN1 gene mutation
> Hyperparathyroidism (90% of cases), Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (insulinoma, gastrinoma, etc.), Pituitary adenoma (anterior). Others: other NETs, adrenal cortical tumours, CNS tumours, thyroid tumours. Most likely to present with hypercalcaemia.

> [!info] MEN-2A ("2 P's") — secondary to RET oncogene mutation
> Hyperparathyroidism, Phaeochromocytoma, Medullary thyroid cancer. Associated with Hirschsprung disease, cutaneous lichen amyloidosis.

> [!info] MEN-2B ("1 P") — secondary to RET oncogene mutation
> Phaeochromocytoma, Medullary thyroid cancer, Marfanoid body habitus, Mucosal intestinal ganglioneuromatosis.

**Other MEN types:** MEN3 (aka familial medullary thyroid cancer); MEN4 (rare) — parathyroid adenomas, pituitary adenomas, adrenal/renal/reproductive tumours, GI NETs.

## 0.10 Hyperparathyroidism

**D:** increased PTH, divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary.

### 0.10.1 Primary Hyperparathyroidism
Main problem is ↑PTH. ↑PTH → ↑Ca, ↓PO4.
**Causes:** 80% solitary adenoma, 4% multifocal disease, 10% hyperplasia, ≤1% PTH carcinoma.
**S/Smx:** asymptomatic if mild; recurrent abdominal pain; emotional/cognitive changes; XR — pepperpot skull, osteitis fibrosa cystica (OFC — bone disease specifically secondary to ↑PTH, shows subperiosteal bone resorption).

### 0.10.2 Secondary Hyperparathyroidism
Main problem is ↓Ca. ↓Ca ± ↓Vitamin D → ↑PTH, ↑PO4.
**Causes:** usually secondary to chronic renal failure (↓vitamin D conversion → ↓Ca absorption).

> [!info] Why CKD specifically causes this — the kidney plays two distinct roles in calcium/phosphate homeostasis, and CKD disrupts both simultaneously, which is why renal failure is such a common and severe cause of secondary hyperparathyroidism (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), not repeated here, for the fuller renal-specific context). **(1) Vitamin D activation:** the final activation step of vitamin D (1-alpha-hydroxylation of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to the active hormone, calcitriol) occurs in the kidney — as nephron mass declines in CKD, this activation capacity progressively fails, directly reducing intestinal calcium absorption and contributing to hypocalcaemia. **(2) Phosphate excretion:** the kidney is the primary route of phosphate excretion — as GFR falls, phosphate is retained, and this retained phosphate has a **direct stimulatory effect on PTH secretion**, independent of and in addition to the hypocalcaemia-driven PTH rise; retained phosphate also directly suppresses calcitriol production further, compounding the vitamin D deficiency. The net result is PTH being driven up by two separate, mutually reinforcing renal failures at once — impaired vitamin D activation and phosphate retention — not just a single simple calcium-deficiency feedback loop.

**S/Smx:** few symptoms; eventually can develop bone disease, OFC, and soft tissue calcification.

### 0.10.3 Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism
Main problem is ↑PTH that persists despite correction of Ca levels. Parathyroid hyperplasia → ↑PTH. Labs: ↑PTH, normal/↑Ca, ↓/normal PO4, ↓/normal vitamin D, ↑ALP.
**Causes:** parathyroid hyperplasia secondary to chronic renal disease, non-responsive to Ca correction.
**S/Smx:** metastatic calcification, bone pain ± fractures, renal stones, pancreatitis.

**Ix:** PTH (*why:* the key test distinguishing the three subtypes above, interpreted alongside calcium — the pattern of PTH relative to calcium is the core diagnostic logic (elevated PTH with elevated Ca = primary; elevated PTH with low/normal Ca = secondary; elevated PTH with normal/elevated Ca despite chronic secondary disease = tertiary); *what:* the specific pattern for each subtype is detailed above). Bone profile (Ca, PO4, etc.) (*why:* completes the biochemical picture distinguishing the subtypes, and screens for the metabolic consequences described above; *what:* patterns as detailed per subtype above). XR (*why:* screens for the skeletal complications of chronic PTH excess; *what:* pepperpot skull, osteitis fibrosa cystica as described above). CT/MRI neck (*why:* localises a parathyroid adenoma/hyperplasia for surgical planning once the biochemical diagnosis is established; *what:* identifies the causative lesion(s), guiding whether single-gland or multi-gland surgery is needed).

**Mx:** treat underlying cause.
- Primary: surgical resection of tumours; medical — calcimimetic (e.g. cinacalcet)
- Secondary: correct renal failure, supplement vitamin D and calcium; may require surgery
- Tertiary: if developing post-renal transplant, wait 12 months as ↑PTH may resolve spontaneously; may require surgery

## 0.11 Hypoparathyroidism

**D:** decreased PTH → decreased Ca, increased PO4.

**A:** post-surgery (75% of cases), genetics (e.g. DiGeorge syndrome), ↑ or ↓ Mg

**S/Smx (hypocalcaemia):** tetany (muscle twitching, cramps, spasm); Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm); Chvostek's sign (tapping the facial nerve causes muscle spasm); ECG — prolonged QT interval.

> [!info] The mechanism behind hypocalcaemia's neuromuscular signs — worth understanding as one connected picture, not separate unrelated facts. Extracellular calcium normally **stabilises the neuronal cell membrane** by blocking sodium channels, raising the threshold needed for depolarisation; when calcium falls, this stabilising effect is lost, sodium channels become easier to open, and nerves/muscles become **hyperexcitable** — firing spontaneously or with minimal provocation. This single mechanism explains all three findings above: **tetany** is this hyperexcitability manifesting as spontaneous or exaggerated muscle contraction; **Trousseau's sign** provokes it by inflating a BP cuff above systolic pressure for ~3 minutes, inducing localised ischaemia that further increases nerve excitability, triggering visible carpal spasm (the "obstetrician's hand" posture); **Chvostek's sign** provokes it by directly tapping the facial nerve, triggering visible twitching of the facial muscles it supplies. The same membrane-excitability principle extends to cardiac tissue, explaining the **prolonged QT** — calcium affects the plateau phase of the cardiac action potential, and its loss prolongs ventricular repolarisation.

**Ix:** PTH (*why:* confirms the diagnosis — low/inappropriately normal PTH in the context of hypocalcaemia distinguishes this from the many other causes of hypocalcaemia where PTH would be appropriately elevated (see Hypocalcaemia in the Electrolyte Disorders section below); *what:* low or inappropriately normal despite low calcium). Ca, PO4, Mg (*why:* confirms the biochemical pattern and screens for magnesium derangement as a reversible cause/contributor, since both very high and very low Mg can impair PTH secretion/action; *what:* ↓Ca, ↑PO4 as above; Mg may be deranged as a contributing cause).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (symptomatic hypocalcaemia):** see Hypocalcaemia in the Electrolyte Disorders section below for the acute IV calcium gluconate regimen — not repeated here.
- **Definitive/chronic:** alfacalcidol (vitamin D analogue — preferred over native vitamin D since it doesn't require renal 1-alpha-hydroxylation, bypassing the PTH-dependent step that's deficient in this condition) ± calcium supplementation; regular monitoring of calcium levels to titrate therapy and avoid both under- and over-replacement (over-replacement risks hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis given the absence of PTH's normal renal calcium-conserving effect).

### 0.11.1 Pseudohypoparathyroidism
Normal PTH levels, but target cells do not respond to PTH → ↑PTH, ↓Ca, ↑PO4. Genetic problem associated with low IQ, short stature, shortened fingers.

### 0.11.2 Pseudopseudohypoparathyroidism
Similar phenotype to pseudohypoparathyroidism, but normal biochemistry.

## 0.12 Vitamin D Deficiency

> [!note] Gap-filled — genuinely absent as its own entry anywhere in this project despite being extremely commonly tested and mentioned only in passing as a risk factor elsewhere (Osteoporosis, Miscarriage). Verified against RACGP guidance and Australian Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander-specific epidemiological literature, Aug 2026.

- **D:** insufficient vitamin D to maintain normal bone and mineral metabolism — most vitamin D is synthesised cutaneously from UVB sun exposure, with a smaller contribution from diet (fortified foods, oily fish) and supplementation.
- **R:** reduced sun exposure (indoor lifestyle, covering clothing for cultural/religious reasons, sun avoidance behaviour), **darker skin pigmentation** (a physiologically genuine risk factor, not just a demographic association — melanin competes with the skin's vitamin D synthesis pathway for UVB photons, meaning darker skin requires substantially more UVB exposure to synthesise an equivalent amount of vitamin D compared with paler skin), obesity (vitamin D is sequestered in adipose tissue), malabsorption (e.g. coeliac disease, IBD, prior bariatric surgery), CKD (impaired renal 1-alpha-hydroxylation to the active form), older age (reduced cutaneous synthetic capacity), and certain medications (anticonvulsants, glucocorticoids).
- **S/Smx:** often asymptomatic and picked up incidentally; when symptomatic — bone pain, **muscle pain/myalgia** (a genuinely useful and easily overlooked clinical clue, not just a vague nonspecific symptom, given a specific Australian study identified muscle pain as an indicator of vitamin D deficiency in an urban Aboriginal population), proximal muscle weakness, and in severe/prolonged deficiency, osteomalacia (adults) or rickets (children — see the relevant paediatric entry elsewhere in this project, not repeated here).
- **Ix:** serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OHD) — the standard test, reflecting total body vitamin D stores (both cutaneous and dietary sources) rather than active hormone levels; corrected calcium, phosphate, ALP, and PTH if deficiency is confirmed, to assess for secondary hyperparathyroidism and screen for consequences on bone metabolism.
- **Mx:** vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) supplementation, dose and duration depending on severity of deficiency and underlying cause — check current local/eTG dosing given regimens vary by deficiency severity; sun exposure advice (balanced against skin cancer risk, given Australia's separate and significant skin cancer burden — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] and [[09_03a_Dermatology_-_Non-Melanoma_Skin_Cancer]], not repeated here); treat any underlying malabsorptive or renal cause where identified.

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with a genuinely counter-intuitive point — not a generic epidemiological footnote, and directly contrary to the assumption that an outdoor, sunny-climate lifestyle protects against vitamin D deficiency. **Vitamin D deficiency affects roughly 27% of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults nationally** — and, genuinely counter-intuitively, **prevalence is higher in remote areas (39%) than non-remote areas (23%)**, despite the assumption that a remote, outdoor lifestyle would mean more sun exposure and therefore better vitamin D status. The core mechanism is the skin-pigmentation risk factor described above, compounded by reduced physical activity in some communities following the shift from traditional outdoor lifestyles, and dietary changes away from vitamin D-rich traditional foods (offal, marine species) toward a Western dietary pattern lower in vitamin D.
> - **Practical clinical implication**: given how common this is and how nonspecific/easily-missed the presentation can be (muscle pain being a genuinely useful clue, as above), a lower threshold for testing 25-OHD in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients presenting with musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, or falls/fracture risk is genuinely warranted, rather than assuming a sunny climate and outdoor lifestyle rules out deficiency.

## 0.13 Prolactinoma

**D:** benign pituitary adenoma of prolactin-secreting cells.
- By size: microadenoma (<1cm) or macroadenoma (>1cm)
- By hormonal status: secreting/functioning (↑prolactin) or non-secreting/non-functioning

**S/Smx:**
- Females: amenorrhoea, infertility, galactorrhoea, osteoporosis
- Males: impotence, galactorrhoea, loss of libido
- Mass effect: headache, visual disturbances (bitemporal hemianopia most common)
- Hypopituitarism

**Ix:** MRI brain (*why:* the key diagnostic and localising test, confirming the pituitary lesion and its size/relationship to surrounding structures (particularly the optic chiasm, relevant to the bitemporal hemianopia risk above); *what:* identifies a micro- or macroadenoma). Serum prolactin (*why:* confirms the functional/secretory status and correlates roughly with tumour size, useful both diagnostically and for monitoring treatment response; *what:* elevated in functioning prolactinomas — very high levels are fairly specific for prolactinoma over other causes of hyperprolactinaemia, e.g. stalk compression from a non-functioning adenoma, discussed below).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (first-line, unusually for a pituitary tumour — most other pituitary adenomas go to surgery first):** dopamine agonists (e.g. cabergoline, bromocriptine; MOA: inhibit prolactin release from the pituitary gland) — genuinely first-line medical therapy rather than a bridging measure, since prolactinomas are unusually responsive to medical therapy compared to other pituitary tumour types, often shrinking substantially and normalising prolactin without surgery.
- **Surgical (second-line):** transsphenoidal surgery — reserved for medical therapy failure/intolerance, or apoplexy/acute mass effect requiring urgent decompression.
- **Chronic/long-term:** regular prolactin monitoring and visual field assessment (given the mass effect risk above) to guide dose titration and monitor for recurrence/regrowth if dopamine agonist therapy is ever weaned.

> [!note] Non-functioning pituitary adenoma can cause increased prolactin (via stalk compression), secondary hypothyroidism, hypogonadism.

> [!note] HbA1c may be inaccurate in: haemoglobinopathies (e.g. sickle cell), haemolytic anaemia, untreated iron deficiency anaemia, suspected gestational diabetes, children, HIV, CKD, and patients on medications causing hyperglycaemia (e.g. steroids).

## 0.14 Hypopituitarism

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Pituitary disorders," High yield) — not covered as its own entity in the source notes, despite Prolactinoma and Acromegaly above both being pituitary conditions; this entry covers the broader syndrome of anterior/posterior pituitary hormone deficiency, plus the acute emergency (pituitary apoplexy) that can precipitate it.

**D:** deficiency of one or more anterior pituitary hormones (partial or panhypopituitarism), ± posterior pituitary (ADH) deficiency causing central diabetes insipidus (see Diabetes Insipidus below).

**A:** pituitary macroadenoma (most common — via direct destruction or compression of normal pituitary tissue, including non-functioning adenomas causing mass effect as noted above), pituitary apoplexy (see below), Sheehan syndrome (postpartum pituitary infarction following major obstetric haemorrhage), radiotherapy, traumatic brain injury, infiltrative disease (sarcoidosis, haemochromatosis, lymphocytic hypophysitis — the latter particularly associated with pregnancy/postpartum and with immune checkpoint inhibitor cancer therapy), surgery, congenital causes.

**S/Smx:** depends on which axis/axes are affected, and typically follows a fairly predictable order of loss (GH and gonadotrophins usually affected earliest, ACTH and TSH deficiency later, though this is not absolute):
- **Gonadotrophin (LH/FSH) deficiency:** oligo-/amenorrhoea, loss of libido, infertility, reduced secondary sexual characteristics
- **GH deficiency:** fatigue, reduced muscle mass, central adiposity (adults) — often subtle/non-specific in adults, more obviously growth failure in children
- **TSH deficiency (secondary hypothyroidism):** as for primary hypothyroidism above, but note **TSH itself may be normal or even mildly elevated** despite true deficiency, since the defect is central — free T4 is the more reliable test (see Ix below)
- **ACTH deficiency (secondary adrenal insufficiency):** similar to Addison's disease above, but **critically, without hyperpigmentation** (since ACTH itself, not just cortisol, drives skin pigmentation via its melanocyte-stimulating shared precursor — the absence of this sign is a useful distinguishing feature from primary adrenal insufficiency) and **without the mineralocorticoid deficiency** seen in primary Addison's (aldosterone regulation is largely independent of ACTH, being primarily driven by the renin-angiotensin system) — so hyperkalaemia is much less prominent than in primary adrenal insufficiency
- **ADH deficiency:** central diabetes insipidus (see Diabetes Insipidus below)
- **Mass effect (if due to a macroadenoma):** headache, visual field defects (classically bitemporal hemianopia — see [[04_Neurology]] and [[05_Ophthalmology]] for the fuller localising detail on this finding, not repeated here)

**Ix:** anterior pituitary hormone panel — LH, FSH, GH/IGF-1, TSH + free T4, ACTH + cortisol (9am cortisol or short synacthen test, as for Addison's above) (*why:* systematically screens each axis, since deficiencies can be partial and axis-specific rather than uniformly panhypopituitary; *what:* low peripheral hormone levels with an inappropriately low/normal (rather than elevated) central pituitary hormone — this "inappropriately normal" pattern is the hallmark of a central/secondary problem, distinguishing it from primary end-organ failure where the pituitary hormone would be appropriately elevated). MRI pituitary (*why:* identifies a structural cause — macroadenoma, apoplexy, infiltrative changes — once the biochemical pattern confirms hypopituitarism; *what:* may show a mass lesion, pituitary infarction/haemorrhage, or an empty/small sella).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic:** hormone replacement for each deficient axis — hydrocortisone for ACTH deficiency (see Addison's Disease above for the general principles, noting the mineralocorticoid point above means fludrocortisone typically isn't needed here), levothyroxine for TSH deficiency, sex hormone replacement for gonadotrophin deficiency, desmopressin for ADH deficiency, GH replacement in selected cases (particularly children, for growth).
- **Important sequencing point:** if both ACTH and TSH deficiency are present, **glucocorticoid replacement must be started before or alongside levothyroxine, never after** — starting thyroid hormone first accelerates cortisol metabolism and can precipitate an adrenal crisis in a patient with unrecognised/untreated coexisting ACTH deficiency.
- **Definitive (structural cause):** treat the underlying cause where identifiable — surgery for a mass lesion causing compression, per the Prolactinoma/Acromegaly Mx above depending on the specific adenoma type.

### 0.14.1 Pituitary Apoplexy

**D:** sudden haemorrhage or infarction into a pituitary adenoma (usually a pre-existing, sometimes previously undiagnosed, macroadenoma) — a genuine endocrine and neurosurgical emergency.

**S/Smx:** sudden severe headache (classically "thunderclap," overlapping in presentation with subarachnoid haemorrhage — see [[04_Neurology]] Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH) for the differential this needs to be distinguished from), visual disturbance (acute visual field loss or ophthalmoplegia from cavernous sinus involvement), nausea/vomiting, acute hypopituitarism (particularly acute ACTH deficiency, which is the immediately life-threatening component given the risk of adrenal crisis).

**Ix:** urgent MRI (or CT if MRI unavailable) pituitary (*why:* confirms haemorrhage/infarction within the pituitary and distinguishes this from other causes of thunderclap headache like SAH; *what:* haemorrhagic/infarcted pituitary mass). Urgent cortisol and full pituitary hormone panel (*why:* acute ACTH deficiency is the most immediately dangerous consequence and needs urgent identification/treatment; *what:* may show acute hypopituitarism as above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** empirical high-dose hydrocortisone started on clinical suspicion — do not wait for cortisol results, given the life-threatening risk of unrecognised acute adrenal insufficiency in this context (the same principle as the GCA/temporal arteritis and myxoedema coma "don't wait for confirmation" pattern seen elsewhere in this file and in [[04_Neurology]]); urgent neurosurgical referral for consideration of surgical decompression, particularly if there's significant visual compromise.
- **Definitive:** surgical decompression (transsphenoidal) for patients with significant/progressive visual loss or reduced consciousness; conservative management with close monitoring may be appropriate for milder cases without significant visual or consciousness compromise.
- **Chronic/long-term:** most patients require long-term hormone replacement following apoplexy, per the Hypopituitarism Mx above, given the pituitary damage is often permanent.

## 0.15 Diabetes Mellitus

### 0.15.1 Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM)

**D:** metabolic disorder characterised by hyperglycaemia due to absolute insulin deficiency.

**A/P:** autoimmune or idiopathic destruction of pancreatic β cells.

**S/Smx:** peak diagnosis age 10–14; hyperglycaemia, polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, increased fatigue. In adults: hyperglycaemia, ketosis, rapid weight loss, BMI <25, often with family history.

> [!danger] Some patients present with DKA as their first presentation.

**Dx criteria for diabetes mellitus:**
- Symptomatic patients: random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L
- Fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L
- Plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L 2h after 75g OGTT
- HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (≥6.5%)

**Ix:** glucose levels (*why:* confirms hyperglycaemia against the diagnostic criteria above; *what:* elevated per the criteria). Plasma or urine ketones (*why:* screens for ketosis, both supporting the T1DM diagnosis (absolute insulin deficiency drives ketogenesis) and screening for DKA at presentation, given how commonly T1DM first presents this way; *what:* positive/elevated ketones support T1DM over T2DM and may indicate concurrent DKA). C-peptide (*why:* directly reflects endogenous insulin production, distinguishing T1DM (absolute deficiency) from T2DM (relative deficiency with preserved/excess endogenous production) — particularly useful in atypical presentations where the clinical picture alone doesn't clearly distinguish the two; *what:* low, reflecting minimal endogenous insulin production). Diabetes-specific autoantibodies (*why:* confirms the autoimmune subtype specifically, supporting T1DM diagnosis and distinguishing from other causes of absolute insulin deficiency; *what:* e.g. anti-GAD, anti-IA2, anti-insulin antibodies — positive in the majority of autoimmune T1DM).

**Mx:** basal-bolus insulin (long-acting for basal, rapid-acting for bolus) in a multiple daily injection regimen. Metformin if BMI ≥25. Monitor glucose before each meal and before bed (≥4x daily); more frequent monitoring if unwell, exercising more, or pregnant.

> [!info] Verified — Australian obstetric practice confirms low-dose aspirin for pre-eclampsia prevention in high-risk pregnancies (including pre-existing diabetes), starting 12–16 weeks and continuing until 36 weeks, consistent with the general principle in the UK-sourced note — this is genuinely international-consensus practice (WHO and multiple countries including Australia recommend it), not a UK-only protocol. Two AU-relevant refinements: **RANZCOG guidance and Australian obstetric units generally use 100–150mg (rather than 75mg)**, and **night-time dosing is now preferred** over morning dosing, since it aligns better with circadian platelet activity/BP patterns and shows greater BP reduction/endothelial benefit in trial evidence — a specific, practical point not in the original note.
> Pregnant women with T1DM — low-dose aspirin (100–150mg, taken at night) from 12–16 weeks until 36 weeks.

### 0.15.2 Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM)

**D:** progressive disorder defined by deficits in insulin secretion and increased insulin resistance, leading to abnormal glucose metabolism and related metabolic derangements.

**A/P:** relative insulin deficiency due to excess adipose tissue — not enough insulin to "go around" the excess fatty tissue.

**S/Smx:** polyuria, polydipsia ± weight loss, paraesthesias, acanthosis nigricans. Usually picked up incidentally.

**R:** ↑age, obesity, PMH of GDM, prediabetes, family history of T2DM, non-white ancestry, PCOS, HTN, dyslipidaemia, CVD, stress.

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with a specific, actionable screening-tool exception — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **T2DM in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians is roughly 4 times more prevalent than in non-Indigenous Australians, with a substantially earlier age of onset** (T2DM was traditionally considered an adult-onset disease, but is now diagnosed in Indigenous children as young as 10–14) and diabetes-related death occurring at a median age around **20 years younger** than non-Indigenous Australians — diabetes was the leading cause of death for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women in 2023.
> - **The critical, actionable point: AUSDRISK (Australia's standard diabetes risk-assessment tool) is not recommended for use in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people**, given the significantly higher baseline prevalence in these communities means the tool can genuinely underestimate individual risk — directly analogous to how Centor/FeverPAIN don't capture rheumatic fever risk (see [[13_05a_ENT_-_Sore_Throat_and_Tonsillitis]], not repeated here) — a general-population risk-stratification tool doesn't transfer safely to a population with a fundamentally different baseline risk.
> - **Current RACGP guidance recommends bypassing the risk-calculator approach entirely** and instead performing **annual screening** (fasting glucose, random glucose, or HbA1c) directly — from age 15 as part of the MBS-funded Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health check, or from age 10 in remote settings if additional risk factors (overweight, family history, maternal GDM, PCOS, acanthosis nigricans) are present. This is a genuinely earlier and more proactive screening threshold than general population practice, not just the same test done slightly more often.

**Ix:** fasting lipid profile (*why:* T2DM carries significant cardiovascular risk, so lipid assessment is part of the baseline cardiovascular risk stratification driving Mx decisions — see the Hypercholesterolaemia section above and the T2DM Stepwise Drug Management section below for how this feeds into treatment choice; *what:* often shows the characteristic diabetic dyslipidaemia pattern — ↑triglycerides, ↓HDL). Urine ACR (*why:* screens for diabetic nephropathy, one of the key microvascular complications, and its presence/severity influences both cardiorenal drug choice (e.g. SGLT2i) and monitoring intensity; *what:* elevated ACR indicates albuminuria/nephropathy). eGFR (*why:* assesses renal function, relevant both to nephropathy screening and to drug dosing/choice (e.g. metformin contraindications, SGLT2i eligibility); *what:* may show reduced function in established nephropathy). ECG (*why:* baseline cardiovascular assessment given the elevated CV risk in T2DM; *what:* may reveal silent ischaemia or baseline abnormalities relevant to future risk assessment). ABPI (*why:* screens for peripheral vascular disease, a macrovascular complication with implications for foot care and vascular risk; *what:* reduced ABPI indicates PVD). LFT (*why:* baseline before starting medications metabolised hepatically or with hepatotoxic potential, and screens for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, which is strongly associated with T2DM; *what:* may show evidence of NAFLD/steatohepatitis).

**Mx:** lifestyle modification (diet, exercise, stop smoking), management of comorbidities, plus pharmacological agents (see T2DM Stepwise Drug Management below for the full AU-verified approach, not repeated here).

### 0.15.3 Complications of Diabetes Mellitus

**Macrovascular:**
- Brain — stroke/TIA, cognitive impairment
- Heart — HTN, atherosclerosis, CHD, MI
- Extremities — peripheral vascular disease, increased risk of ulcers, gangrene, amputation

**Microvascular:**
- Eye — diabetic retinopathy, cataracts, glaucoma (see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Diabetic Retinopathy, Cataracts, and Open-Angle Glaucoma for the full AU-verified disease-level detail on each, not repeated here)
- Kidney — diabetic nephropathy → CKD
- Neuropathy — peripheral neuropathy (glove and stocking pattern), contributes to extremity issues (see Diabetic Foot below for how this and the macrovascular extremity complications combine into a distinct clinical entity)

### 0.15.4 Diabetic Foot

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Diabetic foot," High yield) — not covered in the source notes despite being one of the highest-yield diabetes complications topics, combining elements of the neuropathy and peripheral vascular disease already listed above into a distinct clinical entity with its own assessment and management approach. See [[04_Neurology]] Diabetic Neuropathy for the fuller classification and Mx of the neuropathic component specifically (including the pharmacological approach to painful neuropathy), not repeated here.

**D:** a spectrum of foot pathology in people with diabetes arising from the combination of peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, and increased infection susceptibility — the leading cause of non-traumatic lower limb amputation.

**A/P:** three interacting mechanisms, often present together in the same patient:
- **Peripheral neuropathy** (sensory, motor, and autonomic) — sensory loss means injury goes unnoticed; motor neuropathy causes small muscle wasting and altered foot biomechanics (clawing of toes, altered pressure points); autonomic neuropathy causes reduced sweating (dry, cracked skin prone to fissuring and infection) and arteriovenous shunting.
- **Peripheral arterial disease** — reduces tissue perfusion, impairing wound healing and increasing infection/ischaemic risk once a wound occurs.
- **Increased infection susceptibility** — hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophil function and wound healing generally, so infections that would be trivial in a non-diabetic patient can progress rapidly.

**S/Smx:**
- **Neuropathic ulcer:** typically painless (given the sensory loss), located over pressure points (plantar surface, metatarsal heads, heel), surrounded by callus, in a foot that may otherwise feel warm with palpable pulses (neuropathy without significant ischaemia).
- **Ischaemic ulcer:** typically painful, located at the foot margins/toes, in a foot that is cool with reduced/absent pulses — often with other signs of PVD (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD), not repeated here).
- **Neuroischaemic ulcer:** features of both — increasingly the most common pattern given how often neuropathy and PVD coexist.
- **Charcot foot (neuroarthropathy):** a specific and easily-missed complication — acute presentation is a warm, swollen, sometimes painful foot (which can be mistaken for cellulitis or gout given active inflammation) in a patient with established neuropathy, progressing to bone/joint destruction and characteristic foot deformity ("rocker-bottom foot") if unrecognised and not offloaded — a genuine diagnostic pitfall given how easily it's mistaken for infection when in fact it's an inflammatory/destructive bone process requiring offloading rather than antibiotics as the primary intervention.
- **Infection:** cellulitis, abscess, osteomyelitis — signs of infection can be subtle in a neuropathic/ischaemic foot (reduced systemic inflammatory response), so a high index of suspicion is needed; probe-to-bone testing (a sterile probe reaching bone through an ulcer) is a useful bedside sign strongly suggestive of underlying osteomyelitis.

**Ix:** clinical foot examination — inspection (deformity, callus, ulceration, skin changes), palpation (temperature, pulses), 10g monofilament testing and vibration sense (*why:* the standard bedside screen for peripheral sensory neuropathy, identifying patients at high ulceration risk before an ulcer develops — this is the basis of the annual diabetic foot screening/risk stratification programs; *what:* loss of monofilament/vibration sensation indicates high-risk neuropathic feet). ABPI (*why:* screens for coexisting PVD, relevant both to healing potential and to revascularisation decisions; *what:* reduced ABPI indicates arterial insufficiency — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] for the fuller ABPI interpretation, not repeated here). Wound swab/deep tissue culture if infected (*why:* identifies the causative organism(s), often polymicrobial in diabetic foot infections, to guide targeted antibiotic therapy; *what:* guides antibiotic choice). X-ray ± MRI if osteomyelitis suspected (*why:* MRI is more sensitive for early osteomyelitis than X-ray, which may not show changes until bone destruction is more advanced; *what:* MRI shows bone marrow oedema/destruction; X-ray may show periosteal reaction or bone destruction in more established disease).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (infected ulcer):** antibiotics per local guidelines (often broad-spectrum given the polymicrobial nature of diabetic foot infections), urgent surgical debridement if significant infection/abscess/necrotic tissue, urgent vascular review if critical limb ischaemia is present given the time-sensitivity of revascularisation.
- **Definitive (uninfected ulcer):** offloading (the single most important intervention for a neuropathic ulcer — pressure removal from the affected area, e.g. via a total contact cast or offloading footwear, allows healing that won't otherwise occur despite good wound care alone), wound debridement and appropriate dressings, glycaemic optimisation, revascularisation if significant PVD is limiting healing.
- **Charcot foot specifically:** offloading/immobilisation (total contact casting) is the primary treatment — recognising this as a distinct entity from infection is critical, since inappropriately treating it as cellulitis with antibiotics alone, without offloading, allows ongoing joint destruction and deformity.
- **Chronic/long-term (prevention — arguably the most important tier for this condition):** regular podiatry review and foot screening (annual at minimum, more frequent if high-risk), patient education on daily foot inspection (particularly important given the sensory loss means patients cannot rely on pain to alert them to a problem), appropriate footwear, prompt review of any new lesion.

**P:** diabetic foot ulceration carries significant morbidity (recurrence is common even after healing) and is the leading cause of non-traumatic lower limb amputation — prevention through regular screening and patient education is disproportionately more effective than treatment once ulceration has occurred.


### 0.15.5 T2DM Stepwise Drug Management

> [!info] Verified against RACGP's 2024 "Management of Type 2 Diabetes" handbook and recent PBS changes (Medicine Today, June 2025), Aug 2026 — **the Australian approach has genuinely diverged from the sequential "metformin first, then add" UK-style model since PBS changes in June 2024, December 2024, and April 2025.** This is a substantive, currently-relevant difference, not just a drug-name or dose adjustment.
> **The key shift:** metformin is **no longer necessarily the mandatory default first-line agent**. As of April 2025, PBS rules allow **any SGLT2 inhibitor to be used as initial combination therapy WITH metformin from treatment start** (not added sequentially after a metformin trial) for patients who are at high cardiovascular risk (≥10% 5-year risk via the Australian CVD risk calculator, cvdcheck.org.au), have established atherosclerotic CVD, chronic kidney disease, or heart failure — and critically, **there is no longer an HbA1c threshold requirement** before this combination can be used (previously HbA1c needed to be >7% after a metformin trial; this restriction has been removed). This reflects a philosophical shift in Australian diabetes care from "glycaemic control first, cardiorenal risk second" toward upfront cardiorenal risk stratification driving initial drug choice.
> **What this means practically:** for a newly-diagnosed T2DM patient, the first clinical step is no longer simply "start metformin" — it now requires assessing cardiovascular/renal/heart-failure risk status *before* deciding whether metformin alone or metformin+SGLT2i combination is the appropriate starting point. A patient with high CV risk may reasonably start on combination therapy from diagnosis, even with a relatively modest HbA1c, given the drugs' cardiorenal protective benefits are now prioritised independent of glycaemic control alone.
> **The general escalation logic in the UK-sourced steps below** (add further agents if HbA1c remains above target; insulin if triple therapy fails) is still broadly reasonable as a fallback teaching framework, but should not be taught as the primary Australian first-line pathway given the above — the starting point itself is now risk-stratified, not purely metformin-then-escalate.
> 1. Metformin (if not tolerated or contraindicated: SGLT2 inhibitor alone, or DPP4 inhibitor + pioglitazone/sulfonylurea) — **or metformin + SGLT2i as initial combination if high CV/renal/HF risk, per the above.**
> 2. If HbA1c >58 mmol/mol (7.5%) — add sulfonylurea, gliptin (DPP4i), pioglitazone, or SGLT2 inhibitor
> 3. If HbA1c still >58 — add a 2nd or 3rd drug from above, or start insulin
> 4. If triple therapy not effective/tolerated/contraindicated AND BMI >35 — metformin + sulfonylurea + GLP1 mimetic
> 5. If HbA1c still >58 — insulin
>
> Target HbA1c ≤48 mmol/mol (≤53 if on a drug that may cause hypoglycaemia). Only add a new drug if HbA1c >58 mmol/mol. These specific numeric targets are broadly consistent with RACGP guidance (general target <7%, more flexible up to 8% in frail older patients), though the decision to escalate now also depends on cardiorenal risk status, not HbA1c alone.

> [!info] HbA1c reference ranges
> Normal: <42 mmol/mol (<6%) | Pre-diabetes: 42–47 mmol/mol (6–6.4%) | Diabetes: ≥48 mmol/mol (≥6.5%) | Goal for known diabetes: 58 mmol/mol (7.5%) | Impaired fasting glucose: 6.1–6.9 mmol/L

### 0.15.6 DM Sick Day Rules

> [!danger] Do NOT stop insulin — risk of DKA.

Increase monitoring ± check ketones; consume 3L of fluids/24h ± sugary drinks.

> [!info] Verified against RACGP's "Diabetes sick-day management" guidance, Aug 2026 — the drug-specific principles below are directionally confirmed as correct for Australia (RACGP's own document uses the same general logic), with one addition — GLP-1 receptor agonists specifically aggravate nausea/vomiting themselves (not just an AKI-dehydration concern), which is worth knowing as a distinct reason to stop them during GI illness.
> - Metformin: stop if risk of dehydration/GI illness (lactic acidosis risk; may also directly aggravate GI symptoms)
> - Sulfonylureas: caution — increased hypoglycaemia risk; hold only if blood glucose is low, not routinely
> - SGLT2 inhibitors: check ketones, stop if acutely unwell + risk of dehydration (euglycaemic DKA risk) — RACGP specifically advises having a blood ketone meter available for patients on SGLT2i given this risk
> - GLP-1 receptor agonists: stop if risk of dehydration (AKI risk) — **and independently, because they may themselves aggravate nausea/vomiting**, a direct symptomatic reason to stop distinct from the dehydration-risk rationale
> - Seek urgent medical review if blood glucose ≥15 mmol/L on two consecutive readings (2 hours apart) — a specific numeric trigger point from the RACGP guidance worth knowing

### 0.15.7 Perioperative Diabetes Management

> [!info] Resolved — verified against the ADS-ANZCA Perioperative Diabetes and Hyperglycaemia Guidelines (Adults), the actual Australian national guideline co-developed by the Australian Diabetes Society and ANZCA, Aug 2026. This is genuinely more detailed and in places different from the general international principles the file previously described — most notably, basal insulin is **not** routinely reduced the day before surgery (only reduced if there's a specific reason to), and SGLT2i cessation timing is longer than a single "day of" omission for major surgery.
> **Non-insulin medications:**
> - **SGLT2 inhibitors** — withhold for **two days prior to surgery AND the day of surgery** (major surgery/anything requiring bowel preparation); for **minor/day-stay procedures** (including gastroscopy), only the day of the procedure needs SGLT2i held — genuinely longer than a simple "day of" omission for anything beyond minor surgery, reflecting the euglycaemic DKA risk this class carries.
> - **All other non-insulin anti-hyperglycaemic medications** (metformin, DPP4i, sulfonylureas, GLP-1 agonists) — continue as usual up to and including the night before surgery, then **withhold on the day of surgery itself** (not "no adjustment" for metformin/DPP4i/GLP-1 as previously stated) — this is a genuine, deliberate simplification in current Australian practice: essentially all oral/non-insulin diabetes medications are held on the day of surgery regardless of class, rather than picking and choosing which classes need holding.
> **Insulin (except pumps):**
> - **Basal insulin** — continue at the **usual dose and time**, the night before and on the day of surgery — **do NOT routinely reduce by 20% the day before**, as previously stated; the 20% reduction is reserved specifically for patients with **recent overnight hypoglycaemia**, not applied as a blanket rule.
> - **Pre-mixed insulin** — continue the usual evening dose the night before surgery (with the same hypoglycaemia-triggered 20% reduction exception above); **reduce the morning dose by 50% and omit any lunchtime dose** on the day of surgery — this part of the original note was accurate.
> - **Co-formulated insulin** (e.g. Ryzodeg) — timing is more nuanced depending on morning vs afternoon surgery and whether the patient can eat by a given mealtime — see the full guideline's Appendix K for the specific decision pathway, not reproduced here given the complexity.
> **Metformin and contrast/renal function:** for **major surgery** with known impaired renal function (CKD stage 3B or worse, eGFR <45), check creatinine post-operatively and only restart metformin once renal function is confirmed adequate; for **minor surgery** where the patient can eat/drink immediately after, metformin can simply be restarted without needing to re-check renal function first — a practical distinction not in the original note. **SGLT2i should not restart until the patient has returned to a full normal diet**, and ideally not until discharge — genuinely more cautious than a fixed 48h-post-contrast rule, reflecting the ongoing euglycaemic DKA risk while intake remains reduced.
> **Additional AU-specific points worth knowing:** target perioperative BGL is 5–10mmol/L (7.5±2.5mmol/L), with a tighter target in pregnancy (5.0±1.0) and a higher target (10.0±2.5) for emergency surgery with poor prior control, known hypoglycaemia unawareness, or age >75; dexamethasone (a common perioperative antiemetic) causes clinically meaningful hyperglycaemia and other antiemetics are preferred in people with diabetes where practical; patients should not drive themselves to hospital on the day of surgery given hypoglycaemia risk while fasting.

> [!note] MODY 1/2/3 are autosomal dominant inherited forms of diabetes with onset between the 2nd and 5th decades. MODY 1 and 3 respond well to oral hypoglycaemic agents. Gestational diabetes and paediatric diabetes topics are covered elsewhere in this source (sections 16.04 and 15.16 respectively).

### 0.15.8 Austroads Driving Standards for Diabetes

> [!info] Verified against Austroads *Assessing Fitness to Drive* (2022 edition, current at Aug 2026), Aug 2026 — Australian standards are structured differently from the UK DVLA episode-counting model: rather than counting hypoglycaemic episodes over the preceding 12 months, the Australian framework is built around periodic review requirements (5-yearly for non-insulin, more frequent for insulin) plus a specific hypoglycaemia-awareness assessment tool (the Clarke Questionnaire) and a public safety message — "Don't Drive Under Five" — advising against driving with a BGL <5 mmol/L, rather than a retrospective episode count.
> **Private vehicle drivers:**
> - Diet/lifestyle-controlled: generally no licence restriction.
> - Treated with glucose-lowering agents other than insulin (e.g. metformin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists): may generally drive on an unconditional licence, but the driver licensing authority should require 5-yearly reviews.
> - Treated with insulin: a conditional licence, based on the opinion of an endocrinologist or consultant physician specialising in diabetes, with periodic (typically annual, per Diabetes Australia) review — assessed via intact hypoglycaemia awareness (Clarke Questionnaire), regular BGL monitoring, and absence of disqualifying diabetes-related complications (e.g. significant retinopathy, neuropathy, cardiovascular instability) that could impair driving safety, rather than via a specific episode count in the preceding 12 months.
>
> **Commercial vehicle drivers:** require at least an annual specialist review to monitor disease progression if treated with glucose-lowering agents other than insulin (exception: T2DM managed by metformin alone may be assessed by the treating GP by mutual agreement with the specialist). Insulin-treated commercial drivers require a conditional licence based on specialist opinion and the nature of the driving task, with periodic review — commercial standards are considerably more stringent than private standards throughout, consistent with the general Austroads pattern seen in other categories (e.g. cardiovascular, neurological conditions) in this project's other files.
>
> **HbA1c as a review trigger, not an absolute cutoff:** Austroads guidance references an HbA1c of ≤9.0% as an indicator of "satisfactory control" for licensing purposes — but this is explicitly meant to trigger further medical review, not to function as an automatic disqualifying threshold. Diabetes Australia has specifically advocated that this figure has been misapplied administratively in some cases to deny licence eligibility outright, when hypoglycaemia risk/awareness (not HbA1c per se) is the more directly safety-relevant factor — worth knowing as a point of ongoing advocacy tension in current Australian practice, not a settled, uncontroversial rule.

## 0.16 Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

**D:** acute metabolic complication of diabetes, characterised by absolute or relative insulin deficiency.

**R:** inadequate/inappropriate insulin therapy, infection, MI

**A/P:** uncontrolled lipolysis → increased free fatty acids converted to ketone bodies.

**S/Smx:** abdominal pain, polyuria, polydipsia, dehydration, deep hyperventilation (Kussmaul breathing), "pear drops" breath.

> [!info] Gap-filled — Kussmaul breathing was named without explaining why metabolic acidosis produces this specific pattern. **Mechanism:** the ketoacidosis described above (A/P) lowers blood pH — this is sensed by peripheral and central chemoreceptors, triggering **respiratory compensation**: deep, laboured breathing that increases alveolar ventilation and "blows off" CO2, lowering PaCO2 to partially offset the metabolic acidosis (per the Henderson-Hasselbalch relationship, reducing the carbonic acid side of the buffer equation helps limit the fall in pH). This is genuinely a compensatory mechanism, not a primary respiratory problem — **the depth of breathing (tidal volume) is what's characteristically increased, more than the rate**, distinguishing it from the fast, shallow breathing of primary respiratory distress. "Pear drops" breath reflects a separate but related process: acetone (a ketone body) is volatile and partly exhaled directly, giving breath a characteristic sweet smell.

**Ix:** blood glucose (*why:* confirms the "D" of the diagnostic criteria below; *what:* elevated, or history of DM). ABG/VBG (*why:* confirms the acidosis component and allows monitoring of resolution; *what:* pH <7.3, low bicarbonate). Urine dipstick (*why:* a rapid bedside screen for ketonuria and glycosuria; *what:* positive ketones and glucose). Bloods — U&Es especially (*why:* essential for potassium monitoring given the insulin-driven hypokalaemia risk emphasised in the Mx box below, and to assess renal function/AKI risk from dehydration; *what:* K+ may appear falsely normal/high initially despite total body depletion, since acidosis shifts potassium extracellularly — this is why K+ often falls sharply once insulin/fluid treatment begins, the key reason for close monitoring).

> [!info] Diagnostic criteria
> **D**iabetes — BGC >11 or history of DM | **K**etones — >3 mmol/L or urine ketones 2+ | **A**cidosis — pH <7.3 or bicarbonate <15

> [!info] Verified against a 2025 national survey of Australian DKA management protocols (Internal Medicine Journal), Aug 2026 — the core principles below are broadly consistent with Australian practice, but Australia does **not** have one single unified national protocol the way the UK has JBDS-IP — there is genuine, documented heterogeneity across Australian hospitals in exact insulin/fluid specifics, so treat the figures below as illustrative of the general approach rather than a fixed national standard, and check the specific institution's protocol.
> **Mx (general principles):**
> 1. 0.9% sodium chloride (isotonic saline) is confirmed as the predominant IV fluid choice in Australian practice (used by ~86% of surveyed protocols/70% by clinician preference) — consistent with the UK-sourced note; start a glucose-containing infusion (e.g. D5) once BGC falls to a level where the insulin infusion needs to continue without causing hypoglycaemia (~15 mmol/L is a reasonable general figure and consistent with international practice, though the exact threshold varies by protocol).
> 2. Fixed-rate IV insulin infusion is confirmed as the internationally and Australian-recommended approach (over variable-rate) — the ~0.1 U/kg/h figure is a reasonable general starting point, though note some more recent (2021+) UK guideline revisions have moved toward a lower 0.05 U/kg/h rate specifically to reduce hypoglycaemia/hypokalaemia complication rates, a change worth being aware of even though this session couldn't confirm whether Australian protocols have uniformly adopted this lower figure given the documented institutional variation. Stop short-acting insulin, continue long-acting/basal insulin throughout (this specific point — continuing basal insulin — is a well-established principle, not UK-specific, and reduces rebound hyperglycaemia/ketosis risk on stopping the infusion).
> 3. Monitor and correct electrolyte abnormalities, especially K (may require cardiac monitoring) — insulin-driven hypokalaemia is a recognised major complication across all protocols studied, reinforcing why proactive potassium monitoring/replacement is emphasised regardless of the specific institutional protocol.

**DKA resolution** (should occur within 24h): pH >7.3; blood ketones <0.6 mmol/L; bicarbonate >15 mmol/L.

**Continued Mx:** eat and drink as tolerated, switch to SC insulin, review by diabetes nurse.

**Complications:** ARDS, AKI, gastric stasis, VTE, arrhythmias (secondary to hyper/hypokalaemia), cerebral oedema and hypoglycaemia (iatrogenic — children/young adults especially vulnerable to cerebral oedema after fluid resuscitation, requiring 1:1 monitoring).

> [!warning] **This entry is the adult protocol. Do not apply it to a child.** [[15_16b_Paeds_-_Diabetes_Mellitus__MODY__DKA]] Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) owns the paediatric protocol, and it differs in the ways that matter for the cerebral oedema risk named just above: **deficit replaced over 48 hours**, **reduced maintenance rates** (lower than APLS), **insulin started only after 1–2 hours of fluids and never as a bolus**, and dextrose started at a **BG <14 with 10% dextrose** rather than the ~15 mmol/L and D5 above. Weight-banded fluid figures live there, not here.

## 0.17 Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS)

**D:** acute metabolic complication of DM, characterised by profound hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolarity, and volume depletion in the absence of ketoacidosis.

**R:** infection, inadequate insulin or oral antidiabetic therapy, acute illness in known diabetics, nursing home residents/elderly patients

**A/P:** hyperglycaemia → osmotic diuresis with loss of Na and K. Severe volume depletion → significantly raised serum osmolarity (>320 mOsm/kg) → hyperviscosity. Patients with HHS may not appear as dehydrated as they are, as hypertonicity preserves intravascular volume.

> [!info] Gap-filled — HHS's own diagnostic criteria state "without significant ketonaemia or acidosis," but never explain why T2DM specifically avoids the ketoacidosis seen in T1DM's DKA (see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA), not repeated here), despite both being severe hyperglycaemic emergencies. **The key mechanism is a genuine difference in insulin sensitivity between two separate processes**: suppressing lipolysis/ketogenesis requires only a **small** amount of circulating insulin — hormone-sensitive lipase (the enzyme driving fat breakdown into free fatty acids, which the liver then converts to ketones) is exquisitely insulin-sensitive and switched off by low insulin levels. Controlling blood glucose (driving cellular glucose uptake and suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis), by contrast, requires **much more** insulin action, especially against a background of insulin resistance. **T2DM patients in HHS still have some residual endogenous insulin secretion** — not enough to control glucose (hence the profound hyperglycaemia), but genuinely enough to keep suppressing lipolysis and ketogenesis. **T1DM patients in DKA have essentially zero residual insulin** — with neither process suppressed, unrestrained lipolysis proceeds and ketoacidosis develops on top of the hyperglycaemia. This differential sensitivity is precisely why the same triggering scenario (illness, missed medication) produces two genuinely different emergency presentations depending on diabetes type.

**S/Smx:** fatigue, lethargy, nausea/vomiting; neuro — altered consciousness, headache, papilloedema, weakness; haematological — hyperviscosity → MI, stroke, peripheral arterial thrombosis; CVS — hypotension, tachycardia.

**Dx:** (1) hypovolaemia, (2) BGC >30 mmol/L without significant ketonaemia or acidosis, (3) significantly raised serum osmolarity (>320 mosmol/kg)

**Mx principles:**
1. Gradually normalise osmolality — monitor serum osmolarity, or infer from Na, glucose, and urea; aim for a gradual decline (~3–8 mOsm/kg/h) rather than rapid correction, given the cerebral oedema/osmotic demyelination risk from over-rapid correction (see the Urgent Considerations in Hyponatraemia section above for the analogous principle in severe hyponatraemia)
2. Replace fluid and electrolyte losses — isotonic 0.9% NaCl first, may need to switch to 0.45% NaCl; aim for positive fluid balance of 3–6L by 12h, replace remaining fluid over the next 12h; monitor K levels (may need cautious replacement)
3. Gradually normalise blood glucose — fluid resuscitation alone lowers BGC; insulin only needed if ketosis is significant, otherwise monitor

> [!danger] Content correction — the detailed hour-by-hour protocol previously here (starting fixed-rate insulin at 0.1 U/kg/h from minute 0, following the standard DKA phase structure) was **actually the DKA protocol duplicated under the HHS heading**, and directly contradicted the correct HHS principles immediately above it (which correctly state insulin is only needed if ketosis is significant). This is a genuine and clinically important distinction, not just a UK/AU localisation issue: **HHS and DKA use different insulin strategies.**
> **Correct HHS-specific approach (verified against JBDS-IP HHS-specific guidance, which is structurally distinct from its own DKA guidance):**
> - **Fluid resuscitation alone is the primary initial therapy** — IV 0.9% NaCl to restore circulating volume; this alone often substantially lowers glucose and osmolality without insulin.
> - **Low-dose fixed-rate insulin (0.05 U/kg/h — HALF the DKA starting rate) is only started once glucose/osmolality stops falling with fluids alone, OR immediately if there is significant ketonaemia** (suggesting a mixed DKA/HHS picture) — starting insulin too early or too fast in pure HHS can cause a precipitous osmotic shift and precipitate cerebral oedema or osmotic demyelination, which is exactly why the DKA-style immediate/full-rate protocol is inappropriate here.
> - Glucose infusion (5% or 10%) started once glucose <14 mmol/L, as in DKA.
> - Potassium replacement per levels, as in DKA.
> - **Resolution criteria:** osmolality <300 mOsm/kg, hypovolaemia corrected (urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/h), cognitive status returned to baseline, blood glucose <15 mmol/L.
> - Consider ICU/HDU referral for the same red-flag features as DKA (young/elderly/pregnant, organ failure, severe metabolic derangement, GCS <12) — the general escalation principle is the same even though the underlying protocol differs.
> - Insulin infusion preparation is the same as for DKA: draw up 50U Actrapid insulin, add to 49.5mL 0.9% NaCl in a 50mL syringe → 1U insulin per 1mL solution — only the rate and timing of starting it differs.

## 0.18 Hypoglycaemia

**D:** blood glucose <3.3 mmol/L.

**A:** insulinoma; drugs (insulin, sulfonylureas); liver failure; Addison's disease (↓↓cortisol); alcohol (causes increased insulin secretion)

**S/Smx:** can be asymptomatic early on (patients need to monitor BMs). Early: sweating, shaking, hunger, anxiety, nausea. Later: weakness, vision changes, confusion, dizziness. Severe: seizures, coma.

**Ix:** capillary/plasma blood glucose (*why:* confirms the diagnosis and severity, guiding the Mx tier below; *what:* <3.3 mmol/L per the definition above). If cause unclear/recurrent — insulin, C-peptide, sulfonylurea screen, cortisol (*why:* distinguishes exogenous insulin/sulfonylurea use from an endogenous cause like insulinoma or adrenal insufficiency, since Mx of the underlying cause differs substantially; *what:* insulinoma shows inappropriately elevated insulin AND C-peptide during a hypoglycaemic episode — since exogenous insulin administration would suppress endogenous C-peptide, elevated C-peptide alongside elevated insulin points to an endogenous source like insulinoma, while suppressed C-peptide with elevated insulin suggests exogenous insulin administration).

**Mx:**
- Community, patient alert: oral glucose 10–20g in any form (≈4 jelly babies, 200mL pure fruit juice, 200mL sugary Coke), or quick-acting carb (e.g. Glucogel); some patients may have a Hypokit
- Hospital, patient alert: quick-acting carb (e.g. Glucogel)
- Hospital, unable to swallow or unconscious: **glucagon 1mg IM/SC**, or **IV 20% dextrose**

> [!note] **Paediatric hypoglycaemia is a different threshold and a different dose.** [[15_16b_Paeds_-_Diabetes_Mellitus__MODY__DKA]] Hypoglycaemia owns the child-with-diabetes management (**glucagon 500mcg if <8 years or <25kg**; **IV 10% glucose 5mL/kg** rather than 20% dextrose), and [[15_23a_Paeds_-_NEC__Neonatal_Hypoglycaemia__Hypotonia]] Neonatal hypoglycaemia owns the newborn, where there is **no fixed definition and the working figure is <2.6 mmol/L** rather than the <3.3 above.

**Prevention:** patient education on symptoms, encourage frequent monitoring (especially in T1DM), provide a Hypokit (with glucagon).

> [!note] β-blockers reduce hypoglycaemic awareness.

## 0.19 Diabetes Insipidus (DI)

**D:** metabolic disorder characterised by absolute or relative inability to concentrate urine, resulting in large quantities of dilute urine.
- **Cranial DI:** decreased ADH production
- **Nephrogenic DI:** decreased sensitivity to ADH

**Causes of cranial DI:** idiopathic, post head injury/TBI, pituitary surgery, craniopharyngioma, infiltrative disease (histiocytosis X, sarcoidosis), DIDMOAD/Wolfram syndrome (DI, DM, optic atrophy, deafness), haemochromatosis

**Causes of nephrogenic DI:** genetic (ADH receptor or aquaporin 2 channel), electrolytes (hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia), lithium (desensitises kidney to ADH), demeclocycline, tubulo-interstitial disease (obstruction, sickle cell, pyelonephritis)

**S/Smx:** polyuria, polydipsia, nocturia; other signs depend on causative factor (e.g. visual field defects if secondary to craniopharyngioma).

**Ix:** high plasma osmolality, low urine osmolality (*why:* the initial screening pattern supporting DI over other causes of polyuria; *what:* very dilute urine despite concentrated plasma — the mismatch is the key finding; urine osmolality >700 mOsm/kg excludes DI, since this shows the kidney can concentrate urine appropriately). Water deprivation test with DDAVP challenge (*why:* the definitive test distinguishing true DI (cranial or nephrogenic) from psychogenic polydipsia, and further distinguishing cranial from nephrogenic DI by the response to synthetic ADH; *what:* see the table below for the specific pattern each condition produces).

> [!info] Water deprivation test — method: patient stops drinking water and empties bladder, then hourly urine and plasma osmolalities are measured.

> [!info] **DDAVP is desmopressin — a synthetic analogue of ADH — and that is the entire logic of the test below.** The water deprivation test first asks whether the patient can concentrate urine at all; **giving DDAVP then asks *why not*.** If the kidney concentrates once external ADH is supplied, the problem was a **lack of ADH** (cranial DI). If it still cannot concentrate despite adequate ADH being present, the kidney is **not responding** (nephrogenic DI). The post-DDAVP column is not an extra data point — it is the step that separates the two diagnoses, and reading the table without knowing that makes the last column look arbitrary.

| | Starting plasma osm | Final urine osm | Post-DDAVP urine osm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | Normal | >600 | >600 |
| Psychogenic polydipsia (PP) | Low | >400 | >400 |
| Cranial DI (CDI) | High | <300 | >600 |
| Nephrogenic DI (NDI) | High | <300 | <300 |

*DDAVP = desmopressin (synthetic ADH)*

**Mx:**
- **Definitive:** nephrogenic DI — thiazides (paradoxically reduce urine output in nephrogenic DI via a volume-depletion-mediated increase in proximal tubular reabsorption), low salt/protein diet (reduces the osmotic load requiring excretion); central (cranial) DI — desmopressin, which directly replaces the deficient hormone and is generally highly effective given the problem is one of production, not receptor sensitivity.
- **Chronic/long-term:** treat/address the underlying cause where identifiable (e.g. the specific infiltrative, structural, or drug-related causes listed above); ongoing monitoring of fluid balance and sodium given the risk of both under- and over-treatment (the latter risking hyponatraemia from excessive desmopressin effect).

## 0.20 Acid-Base Balance

**D:** acidosis (pH <7.35), alkalosis (pH >7.45). Further divided into metabolic and respiratory components.

### 0.20.1 Metabolic Acidosis
↓pH, ↓HCO3 (↓PCO2 if compensated). Check anion gap: Na − (Cl + HCO3).

**Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis** (anion gap 6–12, aka hyperchloraemic MA — GI or renal causes common):
> [!tip] Mnemonic "HARDASS": Hyperalimentation, Addison's, Renal tubular acidosis, Diarrhoea, Acetazolamide, Spironolactone, Saline infusion

**Anion gap metabolic acidosis** (anion gap >12):
> [!tip] Mnemonic "MUDPILES": Methanol, Uraemia, Diabetic ketoacidosis (also alcoholic ketones), Propylene glycol, Iron tablets, Isoniazid, Lactate (shock, sepsis, hypoxia, burns), Ethylene glycol, Salicylates (late)

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (severe/symptomatic):** sodium bicarbonate (IV/PO) — used selectively rather than routinely, given evidence for bicarbonate correction in most causes of metabolic acidosis (particularly lactic acidosis) is weak and it doesn't address the underlying cause; ?haemodialysis for severe/refractory cases or specific toxic ingestions (see the SLIME mnemonic below).
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause identified via the anion gap classification above (e.g. insulin for DKA, fluid/perfusion optimisation for lactic acidosis, specific antidotes/dialysis for toxic ingestions).

### 0.20.2 Metabolic Alkalosis
↑pH, ↑HCO3 (or ↓H) (↑PCO2 if compensated).
> [!tip] Mnemonic "HALVE": Hyperaldosteronism, Antacids, Loop diuretics, Vomiting, Extremely low circulating volume (↑concentration of HCO3) — may also be a compensatory mechanism for chronic respiratory acidosis (e.g. COPD)

**S/Smx:** tingling, muscle cramps, weakness, cardiac arrhythmias, seizures.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** if severe (pH >7.6), controlled hypoventilation using sedation and mechanical ventilation — reserved for genuinely severe/dangerous alkalosis given the invasiveness of this approach.
- **Definitive:** correct volume depletion with NaCl (addresses the "contraction alkalosis" mechanism directly); manage electrolyte abnormalities (particularly hypokalaemia, which itself perpetuates alkalosis via renal H+ handling); if hypervolaemic, acetazolamide (promotes bicarbonate excretion); if reduced renal function, haemodialysis.

### 0.20.3 Respiratory Acidosis
↓pH, ↑PCO2 (>6kPa) (↑HCO3 >24 if compensated).
> [!tip] Mnemonic "AACOW": Airway obstruction, Acute lung disease, Chronic lung disease (e.g. COPD), Opioids/sedatives, Weakening of lung muscles (e.g. myasthenia gravis)

**S/Smx:** hypoventilation, obtundation, haemodynamic instability, respiratory muscle fatigue (accessory muscle use, SOB, ↑RR).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** O2 Venturi mask or CPAP (sats 88–92% for COPD, others >94%) — the COPD-specific lower target reflects the risk of hypercapnic respiratory drive suppression with over-oxygenation in chronic CO2 retainers (see [[02_Respiratory]] COPD for the fuller detail, not repeated here).
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause per the AACOW categories above (e.g. reverse opioid effect with naloxone, bronchodilators for airway obstruction, NIV for progressive hypercapnic failure).

### 0.20.4 Respiratory Alkalosis
↑pH, ↓PCO2 (<4.7kPa) (↓HCO3 <24 if compensated).
> [!tip] Mnemonic "HAPPENS": Hyperventilation, ARDS, Pneumonia, PE, Neoplasm, Sepsis

> [!note] Salicylates cause early respiratory alkalosis followed by metabolic acidosis.

**Mx:** treat the underlying cause per the HAPPENS categories above — respiratory alkalosis itself is rarely directly treated (e.g. simply asking an anxious hyperventilating patient to slow their breathing), since it's almost always a sign of an underlying process rather than a primary problem requiring its own correction.

### 0.20.5 Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA)
Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis + normal serum anion gap and hypokalaemia (except type IV).

| Type | Mechanism | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Type I (distal) | Cannot excrete H+, often genetic | ↑urine pH |
| Type II (proximal) | Cannot reabsorb HCO3, often genetic or Fanconi syndrome — due to decreased H+ secretion by NH3/H-ATPase, decreased NaHCO3 cotransporter, or decreased carbonic anhydrase activity | ↓urine pH; Fanconi — urine glucose, PO4 wasting |
| Type III (mixed) | Any condition causing carbonic anhydrase deficiency, including acetazolamide | ↑urine pH, ↑fractional excretion of HCO3 |
| Type IV (hyperkalaemic distal) | Aldosterone deficiency or resistance — inhibits Na reabsorption in distal nephron → decreased K excretion (∴ ↑K) → decreased ammonia production → decreased urine buffering capacity; also decreased distal H-ATPase function → ↑H | ↓urine pH + positive urine anion gap |

> [!note] Congenital RTA: growth retardation, muscle weakness, failure to thrive, fructose intolerance (hypoglycaemia after fructose ingestion), rickets, renal stones. Fanconi syndrome associated with Balkan ethnicity.

**Ix:** serum HCO3, Cl, Na, K, pH, anion gap (*why:* establishes the normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis pattern characteristic of RTA, distinguishing it from the high-anion-gap causes in MUDPILES above; *what:* the specific electrolyte pattern per type, as detailed in the table above). Urine pH (*why:* a key discriminating test between the RTA types, since the ability (or inability) to acidify urine appropriately is the core defect distinguishing them; *what:* the specific pattern per type as above). Further Ix to identify the underlying cause — e.g. serum aldosterone (*why:* screens for the aldosterone deficiency/resistance driving Type IV specifically; *what:* low or resistant to normal levels), US/CT (*why:* screens for structural renal causes or nephrocalcinosis, a recognised complication particularly of Type I RTA; *what:* may show nephrocalcinosis or an underlying structural cause).

**Mx:** alkali replacement (NaHCO3 or Shohl's solution) — the mainstay across most types, correcting the underlying acidosis; K replacement in Types I, II, III (given the hypokalaemia these types characteristically cause) — note Type IV is the exception, being hyperkalaemic, so K replacement isn't part of Type IV Mx.

## 0.21 Metabolic Syndrome

> [!note] Gap-filled — genuinely absent as its own diagnostic entity despite being mentioned once as a bare risk factor for hypertension elsewhere in this project. This is exactly the "assumed but never explained" pattern found repeatedly throughout this project (TNM, ECOG, DAS28, and others) — a term used as if already understood, never itself defined.

**D:** a clustering of cardiometabolic risk factors — central obesity, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, and insulin resistance/hyperglycaemia — that together confer substantially greater cardiovascular and type 2 diabetes risk than any single component alone; presence of metabolic syndrome roughly **quintuples the risk of developing T2DM** and **triples cardiovascular risk** compared with the general population.

> [!info] Diagnostic criteria — genuinely worth knowing that **multiple competing definitions exist**, and the key practical difference between them is whether central obesity (waist circumference) is treated as an *obligatory* criterion or just one of several equally-weighted factors:
> - **International Diabetes Federation (IDF) criteria:** central obesity (ethnicity-specific waist circumference threshold — ≥94cm men / ≥80cm women for Europid populations, lower thresholds for South Asian/Chinese populations) is **obligatory**, **plus any 2 of the following 4**: raised triglycerides (≥1.7 mmol/L, or specific treatment); reduced HDL (<1.0 mmol/L men / <1.3 mmol/L women, or specific treatment); raised BP (≥130/85, or treated hypertension); raised fasting glucose (≥5.6 mmol/L, or known T2DM).
> - **Harmonised (2009) criteria** (jointly reconciled by IDF, AHA/NHLBI, and other bodies): **any 3 of the 5 components above** (with waist circumference simply being one of the five, not obligatory) — this is now the more widely used definition, given it resolved much of the earlier disagreement between the IDF and the older ATP III/AHA-NHLBI approaches.
> - **Practical point:** given genuine definitional variation still exists in the literature, know the general clustering-of-risk-factors concept and the broad component list above as the core testable content, rather than memorising one single "correct" cutoff set as though it were universally agreed.

**Mx:** addressing the underlying components individually — weight loss/lifestyle modification (the single intervention capable of improving multiple components simultaneously, given the central role of obesity/insulin resistance), management of dyslipidaemia (see Hypercholesterolaemia below, not repeated here), hypertension, and hyperglycaemia (see Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus above, not repeated here) — there is no single unifying drug treatment for "metabolic syndrome" itself; management is inherently multi-component, targeting each contributing factor.

## 0.22 Hypercholesterolaemia

**D:** ↑total cholesterol (TC) and/or ↑LDL-cholesterol or ↑non-HDL-cholesterol ± ↑triglycerides, ↑apolipoprotein B, ↑lipoprotein(a).

**R:** insulin resistance, T2DM, BMI >25, hypothyroidism, cholestatic liver disease

**S/Smx:** risk factors, family history, history of CVD, poor diet, ↑BMI (especially abdominal obesity), xanthelasma ± corneal arcus.

> [!info] Dx criteria: TC >5.18 mmol/L; LDL-C >2.6 (very high >4.9); non-HDL <3.4; HDL <1.04 (M), <1.29 (F); TG >1.7; ± lipoprotein(a) >50 mg/dL
> **Optimal levels:** TC <4.4–5.2; LDL-C <1.8–2.6; TG <1.1–1.7

> [!info] Verified against the National Heart Foundation of Australia's 2023 Guideline for assessing and managing CVD risk and the 2025 Australian Clinical Guideline for ACS, Aug 2026 — the statin class/mechanism content is universal, but the risk-scoring tool and post-ACS target below need genuine correction, not just a dose tweak.
> **Mx:** lifestyle advice (diet, smoking, exercise, weight loss) — no evidence for omega-3 fatty acid supplementation.
> **Statins** (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors) — first-line; reduce LDL 20–55% (each 1mM reduction ≈ 10% reduction in mortality risk, 20% reduction in death due to heart disease, 27% reduction in MI, 21% reduction in stroke).
> - **Risk assessment tool:** Australia does not use QRISK2/3 — the current Australian tool is the **Australian CVD risk calculator** (cvdcheck.org.au), based on the New Zealand PREDICT equation and developed/validated on Australian data as part of the Heart Foundation's 2023 guideline update, replacing the older 2012 absolute-risk framework. QRISK2/3 is a UK-specific tool and shouldn't be taught as the Australian standard.
> - High-risk primary prevention (≥10% 5-year risk on the Australian CVD risk calculator, DM, or CKD): atorvastatin 20mg is a reasonable general starting dose, consistent with international practice, though the risk-stratification tool determining who qualifies is the Australian-specific point above, not the dose itself.
> - **Post-ACS specifically:** current Australian guidance (2025 ACS guideline) sets a target LDL-C **<1.4 mmol/L AND ≥50% reduction from baseline** (not simply "atorvastatin 80mg" as a flat rule) — highest tolerated statin dose is used to reach this target, with ezetimibe added if target isn't reached on statin alone. See [[01_Cardiovascular]] Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) for the fuller AU-verified detail on this specific target, not repeated here.
> - Main SE: myositis, LFT changes
>
> **Second-line/add-on options:**
> (a) Ezetimibe — inhibits GI cholesterol absorption; SE: headache
> (b) Fibrates (e.g. fenofibrate) — PPAR-α agonist, ↑lipoprotein lipase; SE: myositis, pruritus, cholestasis
> (c) Nicotinic acid — decreases hepatic VLDL secretion; SE: flushing, myositis
> (d) PCSK9 inhibitors — inhibit degradation of the LDL receptor; SE: arthralgia, back pain, nausea, infections
> (e) Bile acid sequestrants (e.g. cholestyramine) — decrease bile reabsorption in the small intestine, increase cholesterol conversion to bile; SE: GI side effects

**Monitoring:** bloods every 6 weeks until target LDL achieved, then every 6–12 months when stable; baseline LFTs before starting a statin (no need to check regularly unless myositis suspected).

**Risk scores:** the Australian CVD risk calculator (see above) is the current Australian standard — QRISK2/3 and Framingham are not routinely used in Australian practice, though Framingham-derived concepts historically informed the older (pre-2023) Australian absolute-risk framework.

**Complications:** IHD/ACS, stroke, PVD, erectile dysfunction.

**P:** overall good prognosis, especially with statins; monitor for other comorbidities (e.g. HTN, DM) to improve outcomes.

## 0.23 Principles of Fluid and Electrolyte Management in Surgical Patients

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Principles of fluid and electrolyte management in surgical patients," Low yield) — genuinely absent as a general principles entry despite the individual electrolyte disorders (hyponatraemia, hypernatraemia, acid-base) being thoroughly covered elsewhere in this file.

**The three indications for IV fluid therapy — a useful organising distinction, since they call for different fluid types and volumes:**
- **Maintenance:** replacing ongoing normal physiological losses (urine, insensible losses via skin/respiration, stool) in a patient who cannot meet these needs orally.
- **Resuscitation:** rapidly correcting an existing volume deficit (e.g. hypovolaemic shock, sepsis) — larger volumes given faster, typically as isotonic crystalloid boluses, with the response reassessed after each bolus rather than a fixed total volume prescribed upfront.
- **Replacement:** matching ongoing abnormal losses beyond normal maintenance — e.g. high-output stoma, nasogastric losses, fistula output, large surgical drain losses — ideally matched to the actual measured volume and, where possible, the electrolyte composition of what's being lost, rather than a generic fluid substituted for a very different one.

**Maintenance fluid requirements (per day, in a euvolaemic adult):** roughly 25–30mL/kg of water, 1mmol/kg of sodium, and 1mmol/kg of potassium, plus enough glucose to limit starvation ketosis — the widely used general estimate, though individual requirements genuinely vary and should be reassessed against the specific patient's fluid balance and electrolytes rather than applied as a fixed rule regardless of clinical context.

**Genuinely important surgery-specific principle: the surgical stress response drives sodium and water retention in the immediate postoperative period** — surgical trauma triggers increased aldosterone and cortisol release, promoting renal sodium and water retention independent of the patient's actual fluid status. This is precisely why **postoperative patients often need less maintenance fluid than the standard calculation suggests**, particularly in the first 24–48 hours — over-administering fluid on top of this physiological retention is a genuine, common cause of postoperative fluid overload and its complications (pulmonary oedema, delayed return of bowel function, impaired wound healing), not just a theoretical risk.

**Fluid choice:** **balanced crystalloids** (e.g. Hartmann's solution/compound sodium lactate, Plasma-Lyte) — with an electrolyte composition closer to plasma — are increasingly preferred over 0.9% sodium chloride for most indications, given large-volume 0.9% saline administration is specifically associated with **hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis**, which can itself impair cardiac contractility, adrenoceptor function, and coagulation; 0.9% saline remains appropriate in specific situations (e.g. hypochloraemic states, or where a balanced solution's potassium content is undesirable, such as significant hyperkalaemia).

**Composition of common GI fluid losses — a classic exam point given it directly informs replacement fluid choice:**
- **Gastric/nasogastric losses:** high in H⁺, Cl⁻, and K⁺, relatively low in Na⁺ — significant losses classically produce a hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis (see the acid-base section above for the physiology, not repeated here).
- **Biliary, pancreatic, and small bowel (high) fistula losses:** alkaline, high in Na⁺ and HCO₃⁻ — significant losses tend toward a metabolic acidosis given the bicarbonate loss.
- **Diarrhoea/lower GI losses:** high in K⁺ — significant losses risk hypokalaemia.

**Practical principle:** actively measure and chart fluid balance (input and output, including drains/stomas/NG losses) in any patient with significant ongoing losses or fluid shifts, and reassess the fluid prescription regularly against this data and current electrolytes — fluid therapy should be a continuously reassessed, individualised prescription, not a single fixed order written once and left unchanged through a variable clinical course.

## 0.24 Electrolyte Disorders

### 0.24.1 Hyperkalaemia

**D:** ERC classification — mild 5.5–5.9; moderate 6–6.4; severe ≥6.5 mmol/L.

**Causes:** AKI, rhabdomyolysis; drugs (ACEI, ARBs, K-sparing diuretics e.g. spironolactone, ciclosporin, heparin including LMWH); metabolic acidosis; Addison's disease; massive blood transfusion.

**S/Smx:** muscle weakness, palpitations, paraesthesias.

> [!danger] ECG changes: tall tented T waves → loss of P waves → broad QRS complexes → sinusoidal wave pattern → ventricular fibrillation.
>
> **The mechanism behind this specific progression — worth understanding rather than memorising as an arbitrary sequence:** hyperkalaemia raises the resting membrane potential of cardiac myocytes closer to the threshold for depolarisation. Initially, this **speeds repolarisation** (faster potassium efflux down a steeper gradient) — producing the earliest change, tall tented T waves. As potassium rises further, the persistently depolarised resting membrane potential causes voltage-gated **sodium channels to become progressively inactivated**, which **slows conduction velocity** through the heart — this explains the rest of the sequence in order: the atria (with their smaller myocardial mass and lower safety margin for conduction) are affected first, so **P waves flatten and disappear**; slowed ventricular conduction produces **QRS widening**; and at critically slowed conduction, the widened QRS eventually merges with the T wave into the pre-terminal **sinusoidal pattern**, immediately preceding **ventricular fibrillation** as conduction fails entirely. The progression isn't arbitrary — it reflects a single underlying process (progressively worsening sodium channel inactivation) manifesting first in repolarisation, then progressively in conduction.

**Ix:** ECG (*why:* the most urgent test, since ECG changes — not the absolute potassium level alone — determine treatment urgency; a patient with severe hyperkalaemia but a normal ECG is managed less urgently than one with a lower level but ECG changes; *what:* the specific progression described above, from tented T waves through to the pre-terminal sinusoidal pattern). VBG (K+, HCO3, pH) (*why:* provides a rapid potassium result and assesses for concurrent acidosis, which both causes and exacerbates hyperkalaemia (acidosis drives K+ out of cells) and independently guides Mx (see the bicarbonate step below); *what:* confirms the potassium level and any acid-base derangement).

> [!info] Verified — the core drug doses below (calcium gluconate, insulin+glucose regimen, salbutamol) are consistent with Australian paediatric and adult critical care sources (Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne guideline; general Australian emergency medicine practice) and are not meaningfully UK-specific — these reflect the underlying pharmacology (calcium stabilises the cardiac membrane, insulin/glucose and salbutamol shift potassium intracellularly) rather than a UK-only protocol. No material correction needed to the doses below.
> **Mx — mnemonic "C BIG K DROP":** Stop offending drugs first.
> - **C**alcium gluconate: 30mL of 10% CaGlu IV over 5 min; repeat every 10 min until ECG normalises — **adult dose**
> - **B**eta-agonists: 10mg nebulised salbutamol — **adult dose**
> - **I**nsulin & **G**lucose: 10U Actrapid insulin + 50mL 50% glucose (25g) over 15–30 min into a large vein (do NOT give 50% glucose if hyperglycaemic) — monitor VBG every 2h; monitor BGC at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90 min, then 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 12h (delayed hypoglycaemia is frequently reported)
> - If volume deplete: IV 0.9% NaCl 500–1000mL
> - If volume overloaded: IV furosemide 50mg
> - If pH <7.3, bicarbonate <20: 500mL 1.26% sodium bicarbonate over 1h
> - Monitor urine output; refer urgently to renal team — consider haemodialysis ± K-sequestering agents
>
> [!danger] **The box above states these doses are consistent with Australian *paediatric and adult* critical care sources. The figures in this box are absolute adult doses and do not scale.** A child needs a weight-based calcium gluconate dose and an age-based salbutamol dose; giving 30mL of 10% calcium gluconate to a small child is a large overdose of calcium. **Do not read these figures across to a child** — take paediatric hyperkalaemia doses from the RCH Melbourne guideline the box itself cites. ### 0.24.2 Hypokalaemia

**D:** mild 3–3.5; moderate 2.5–3; severe ≤2.5 mmol/L.

**Causes:**
- With alkalosis: vomiting, thiazides/loop diuretics, Cushing's, Conn's
- With acidosis: diarrhoea, acetazolamide, renal tubular acidosis, partially treated DKA
- Others: ↓Mg

**S/Smx:** muscle weakness, hypotonia, digoxin toxicity.

> [!danger] ECG: U waves, small/absent T waves, prolonged PR, prolonged QT, ST depression.

**Ix/Dx:** U&Es including Mg (*why:* confirms the potassium level and screens for coexisting magnesium deficiency, which is important since hypomagnesaemia both causes and perpetuates hypokalaemia (renal potassium wasting persists until magnesium is corrected) — this is why correcting Mg first is explicitly emphasised in the Mx below; *what:* confirms severity per the classification above; may reveal coexisting ↓Mg). Glucose (*why:* insulin drives potassium intracellularly, so hyperglycaemia/insulin therapy is a relevant precipitant/contributor worth knowing about, particularly relevant given how often hypokalaemia and diabetes Mx intersect (e.g. DKA treatment-induced hypokalaemia); *what:* may be relevant to the underlying cause). ECG (*why:* assesses for the specific rhythm-risk changes below, guiding Mx urgency similarly to hyperkalaemia; *what:* the specific pattern described below). FBC (*why:* a general baseline test, not specifically diagnostic for hypokalaemia itself but often included as part of routine bloods).

> [!info] Verified — **"SandoK" is a UK-specific brand not available in Australia.** The Australian equivalent oral potassium chloride products are **Span-K (or Duro-K)** — slow-release 600mg tablets (8mmol potassium each) — or **Chlorvescent** — effervescent tablets (14mmol potassium each). The general dosing structure and IV replacement rate below (KCl 40mmol/L over 4h with ECG monitoring) reflects standard, non-jurisdiction-specific practice and doesn't need correction beyond the brand substitution.
> **Mx:** stop offending drugs; correct hypomagnesaemia first; IV or PO replacement.
> - K 3.0–3.5 mmol/L: Span-K 2 tablets (16mmol) 8-hourly, or if ECG changes/on digoxin, 0.9% NaCl 1L with KCl 40 mmol/L over 4h with continuous ECG monitoring
> - K 2.5–3.0 mmol/L: Span-K 2 tablets (16mmol) 6-hourly, or as above with ECG monitoring if indicated
> - K <2.5 mmol/L: inform senior doctor, give 0.9% NaCl 1L with KCl 40 mmol/L over 4h with continuous ECG monitoring

> [!danger] Do not replace K at a rate >10 mmol/L/h.

### 0.24.3 Hypercalcaemia

**D:** normal Ca 2.1–2.6 mmol/L. Mild 2.6–2.9; moderate 3.0–3.4; severe >3.4 mmol/L.

> [!tip] Causes — mnemonic "CHIMPANZEES"
> **C**alcium supplementation, **H**yperparathyroidism, **I**atrogenic (thiazides), **M**ilk-alkali syndrome, **P**aget's disease of bone, **A**cromegaly & Addison's, **N**eoplasia, **Z**ollinger-Ellison syndrome (MEN Type I), **E**xcessive vitamin D, **E**xcessive vitamin A, **S**arcoidosis

**S/Smx:** unusual until Ca >3.0. Mnemonic "moans, stones, bones, groans": GI — nausea/vomiting, constipation, abdominal pain; renal — polyuria, polydipsia; CVS — HTN, ECG changes (↑QT, ↑PR, wide QRS, arrhythmias); CNS — depression, altered mental status, headache, ↓GCS, psychosis.

**Ix:** U&Es, bone profile (*why:* confirms the calcium level and screens for associated renal impairment, both a potential cause and a consequence of significant hypercalcaemia; *what:* elevated calcium, may show deranged renal function). CXR (*why:* screens for malignancy (a common cause via bony metastases or PTHrP-secreting tumours — see [[04_Neurology]] Malignant Spinal Cord Compression (MSCC) for a related oncological-emergency context) and sarcoidosis, both entries in the CHIMPANZEES mnemonic above; *what:* may reveal a lung mass, hilar lymphadenopathy, or bony lesions). ECG (*why:* assesses for the cardiac conduction changes associated with hypercalcaemia, relevant to Mx urgency; *what:* the changes described in the S/Smx above — shortened QT is actually the more classic ECG finding in hypercalcaemia, worth noting alongside the broader arrhythmia risk described). PTH (*why:* the key test distinguishing PTH-dependent (hyperparathyroidism) from PTH-independent (malignancy, vitamin D excess, etc.) causes, fundamentally directing which branch of the CHIMPANZEES differential to pursue further; *what:* elevated/inappropriately normal PTH suggests hyperparathyroidism; suppressed PTH suggests a non-parathyroid cause). FBC, ESR, myeloma screen (*why:* screens for haematological malignancy, particularly myeloma, a recognised cause of hypercalcaemia; *what:* may reveal anaemia, elevated ESR, or a paraprotein). 25-OH-vitamin D (*why:* screens for vitamin D excess as a cause, and is a relatively simple test to complete the biochemical work-up; *what:* elevated in vitamin D toxicity).

> [!info] Verified — the fluid/drug doses below (0.9% NaCl volumes, zoledronic acid 4mg over 15 min) reflect internationally standard practice (confirmed against US/UK/general critical care sources) rather than a UK-specific protocol; no material correction needed to the figures themselves.
> **Mx:**
> - Ca 2.6–2.9: stop thiazides, calcium supplements, vitamin A/D; keep hydrated
> - Ca 3.0–3.4 but asymptomatic: 2–3L water/day, or 2–3L IV 0.9% NaCl
> - Ca 3.0–3.4 + symptomatic, OR >3.4 mmol/L: admit + IV 0.9% NaCl 4–6L over 24h as tolerated; monitor fluid balance; measure U&Es and Ca every 12h; IV zoledronic acid 4mg over 15 min if hypercalcaemia persists after hydration (reduce dose if eGFR <60); consider haemodialysis if Ca >4.5; refer to endocrinology

### 0.24.4 Hypocalcaemia

**D:** corrected Ca <2.10 mmol/L; symptoms typically arise when <1.9 mmol/L.

**Causes:**
- ↓PTH secondary to surgery, ↓Mg (associated with PPI use, diuretics, diarrhoea, malnutrition, alcoholism, renal loss), autoimmune disease, genetics
- Failure of calcium release from bone: osteomalacia (vitamin D deficiency, ESRD), drugs (cisplatin, calcitonin, PO phosphates), osteoblastic metastases
- Failure of 1,25-OH vitamin D levels: drugs (ketoconazole, PPI), acute pancreatitis
- Complexing of calcium from circulation: alkalosis (↑albumin binding), acute pancreatitis, multiple blood transfusions

**S/Smx:** tingling, numbness, cramps, carpopedal spasm, stridor (secondary to laryngospasm), seizures; Chvostek's sign, Trousseau's sign (see Hypoparathyroidism above for the shared neuromuscular-excitability mechanism behind these signs and the prolonged QT below, not repeated here); ECG — prolonged QT.

**Ix/Dx:** ECG (*why:* assesses for the QT prolongation above, relevant to arrhythmia risk and Mx urgency; *what:* prolonged QT, the opposite direction to the shortened QT of hypercalcaemia). Bloods — corrected Ca, PO4, PTH, U&E, 25-OH-vitamin D, Mg (*why:* the combination of PTH with calcium and phosphate is the key diagnostic step, since the pattern distinguishes hypoparathyroidism (low PTH, low Ca, high PO4 — see Hypoparathyroidism above) from secondary hyperparathyroidism responding appropriately to hypocalcaemia (high PTH, low Ca) from vitamin D deficiency and other causes; magnesium is checked given its role both as a cause of hypocalcaemia and because severe hypomagnesaemia itself impairs PTH secretion, complicating the picture; *what:* the specific pattern per cause, as detailed above).

> [!info] Verified — **"SandoCal" is a UK-specific brand not available in Australia.** The Australian equivalent oral calcium supplement is **Caltrate** (calcium carbonate 600mg, TGA-registered) — dosed similarly (e.g. 1–2 tablets daily-BD depending on the degree of deficiency and response), or other TGA-registered calcium carbonate/citrate products. The IV calcium gluconate dose and regimen for acute symptomatic hypocalcaemia below reflects standard international practice and doesn't need correction beyond the oral brand substitution.
> **Mx of <1.9 + symptomatic:** 10–20mL 10% calcium gluconate in 50–100mL 5% dextrose IV over 10 min; repeat up to 3x until asymptomatic; cardiac monitoring; anything more requires senior alert and local guideline referral.
>
> **Mx of >1.9 or asymptomatic:** PO calcium supplementation (e.g. Caltrate 600mg, 1–2 tablets BD or equivalent, titrated to response).

**Mx of underlying condition:** vitamin D or Mg deficiency — supplementation; stop offending drugs; refer to ENT (if post-surgical) or endocrinology.

### 0.24.5 Hypomagnesaemia

**D:** <0.9 mmol/L; usually symptomatic only if <0.4–0.5 mmol/L.

**Causes:** drugs (diuretics, PPIs, TPN); diarrhoea (acute/chronic); alcohol; hypokalaemia, hypercalcaemia; metabolic disorders (e.g. Gitelman's and Bartter's syndromes)

**S/Smx** (similar to hypocalcaemia): paraesthesia, tetany, seizures, arrhythmias; exacerbates digoxin toxicity; ECG — prolonged QT.

**Ix:** U&Es including Mg (*why:* confirms the magnesium level and screens for coexisting potassium/calcium derangement, given the well-established interplay between these three electrolytes — hypomagnesaemia commonly coexists with and perpetuates both hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia, so all three are often checked and corrected together; *what:* confirms the level per the definition above, may reveal coexisting ↓K or ↓Ca). ECG (*why:* assesses for the arrhythmia risk and QT prolongation described above, relevant to Mx urgency; *what:* the pattern described above).

> [!info] Verified — the IV magnesium sulfate dose and infusion regimen below reflects standard, pharmacologically-driven practice rather than a UK-specific protocol; no material correction needed.
> **Mx (symptomatic patients):** IV Mg sulfate 50%, 20 mmol in 10mL, made up in 240mL 0.9% NaCl at 50mL/h (over 5h) — faster rates require cardiac monitoring. Monitor observations, urine output, and toxicity signs while infusing. May require up to 160 mmol to correct over 3–5 days. Measure Mg daily until normal. In renal patients, give lower replacement doses (reduce by 25–50%).

> [!note] PO magnesium is poorly absorbed.

## 0.25 Hyponatraemia

**D:** serum Na <135 mmol/L. Assess in a stepwise fashion: measure serum osmolality → assess volume status → measure urine sodium/osmolality as needed.

### 0.25.1 Hypertonic Hyponatraemia (SrOsm >295 mmol/kg)
Causes: hyperglycaemia (HHS), hypertonic fluid administration.

### 0.25.2 Isotonic Hyponatraemia (SrOsm 275–295 mmol/kg)
= pseudohyponatraemia, most often due to markedly ↑serum cholesterol; may also be due to increases in other blood components (e.g. proteins).

### 0.25.3 Hypotonic Hyponatraemia (SrOsm <275 mmol/kg)
Assess volume status:

**Hypervolaemic hypotonic hyponatraemia:**
- Urine Na ≤20 mmol/L: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome (fluid overload state, kidney function generally preserved → normal urine Na)
- Urine Na >20 mmol/L: chronic renal failure (fluid overload state with loss of kidney function)

**Euvolaemic hypotonic hyponatraemia:** assess urine osmolality —
- High urine osmolality: SIADH (see below)
- Variable urine osmolality: medications, prolonged exercise
- Low urine osmolality: high fluid intake, potomania, primary polydipsia

> [!note] Beer drinker's potomania: drinking >6L beer/day with poor dietary intake (usually with signs/symptoms of ALD) → fluid overload, decreased renal function, insufficient electrolytes to excrete. Primary polydipsia: increased fluid intake → dilutional hyponatraemia and diluted urine (?increased urine Na as the body tries to excrete fluid).

**Hypovolaemic hypotonic hyponatraemia:**
- Urine Na ≤20 mmol/L: non-renal Na losses (GI — vomiting/diarrhoea; urinary — diuretics, ↓aldosterone; skin — burns, exudative lesions; sequestration into third space — severe pancreatitis, crush injuries; bleeding)
- Urine Na >20 mmol/L: renal Na losses (e.g. Addison's/adrenal insufficiency — ↓cortisol and ↓aldosterone → increased Na loss → increased water loss, ↓BP)

> [!note] Urine Na also tests renal function: ≤20 mmol/L indicates kidneys are able to retain Na; >20 mmol/L may indicate acute tubular necrosis.

### 0.25.4 SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone)

**D:** condition characterised by hypotonic hyponatraemia, concentrated urine, and a euvolaemic state.

**Causes:**
- Malignancy: SCLC, pancreas, prostate
- Neuro: stroke, SAH, subdural haematoma, meningitis, encephalitis, abscess
- Infections: TB, pneumonia
- Drugs: sulfonylureas, SSRIs, TCAs, carbamazepine, vincristine, cyclophosphamide
- Others: positive end-expiratory pressure, porphyrias

**Ix:** ↑urine osmolality (concentrated urine) (*why:* the key finding confirming the kidney is inappropriately concentrating urine despite plasma hypotonicity — the physiological expectation with hyponatraemia would be maximally dilute urine as the kidney tries to excrete free water, so a concentrated urine in this context is the diagnostic hallmark of SIADH; *what:* inappropriately high given the plasma hypotonicity, as detailed in the Hypotonic Hyponatraemia classification above). ↑urine sodium concentration (*why:* supports SIADH over other euvolaemic causes, given ongoing natriuresis alongside water retention is part of the SIADH picture; *what:* typically >40 mmol/L, in contrast to the low urine sodium expected in true volume-depleted states).

**Mx:** fluid restriction — confirmed as the standard first-line Mx in an Australian tertiary hospital study, though the same study found treatment for inpatient hyponatraemia is often heterogeneous/suboptimal in practice, underlining the importance of following a structured approach rather than an ad hoc one; slow correction of Na to avoid precipitating osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis) — see the correction-rate detail in Urgent Considerations below; second-line — **oral urea has documented real-world Australian use** for SIADH not responding adequately to fluid restriction alone, achieving further correction where fluid restriction plateaus (a genuinely useful addition not in the original UK-style note); other options — demeclocycline, ADH receptor antagonists (vaptans).

> [!note] Cerebral salt-wasting syndrome and SIADH can both occur secondary to head injury, intracranial surgery, SAH, stroke, or tumours. If asymptomatic — water restriction; if symptomatic — Na replacement.

### 0.25.5 Urgent Considerations in Hyponatraemia

> [!danger] Rapid Na decrease can lead to cerebral oedema and CNS symptoms (headache, cramps, ataxia, psychosis, lethargy, apathy, anorexia, agitation) → can progress to coma, brainstem herniation, respiratory arrest, death.

> [!info] Verified — the correction-rate limit is a matter of pharmacology/physiology (osmotic demyelination risk), not a jurisdiction-specific protocol, and the evidence base is genuinely international/consistent rather than UK-specific. Current evidence (multiple systematic reviews/meta-analyses, 2024–2025) supports limiting correction to **≤8mmol/L per 24h** (some sources use ≤10–12mmol/L, but the more conservative 6–8mmol/L figure is increasingly favoured, especially in higher-risk patients — chronic/severe hyponatraemia, alcohol use disorder, hypokalaemia, malnutrition, liver disease) — this is the single most important number to get right and apply cautiously regardless of jurisdiction, more so than the exact hypertonic saline infusion rate below. **Overcorrection is common in practice (documented in ~39% of patients in one large cohort) and is itself a recognised emergency requiring active reversal** (e.g. with desmopressin/dextrose) if it occurs — a point worth knowing given how easily correction can run faster than intended, particularly if an underlying cause (e.g. hypovolaemia) resolves and the kidneys begin excreting free water rapidly on their own.
> **Mx:** hypertonic saline (3%) 1–2 mL/kg/h, aiming to increase Na >120 mM while staying within the ≤8mmol/L/24h limit above; if more aggressive replacement needed for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia (e.g. seizures, severe confusion), a bolus approach (e.g. 100–150mL 3% saline over 10–20 min, repeated as needed) targeting a small, rapid initial rise (~4–6mmol/L) to reverse life-threatening cerebral oedema is increasingly favoured over a continuous high-rate infusion — check current local/eTG protocol for the specific bolus-based regimen, as this represents a genuine evolution in practice from continuous-infusion-only approaches. Furosemide also given to prevent volume overload and manage BP.

> [!danger] Adrenal crisis presents with weakness, postural hypotension, fatigue, ± hypovolaemic shock. Mx: IV fluids, glucocorticoid replacement — DO NOT delay treatment.

## 0.26 Hypernatraemia

**D:** serum Na >145 mmol/L, due to net water loss or excess Na intake.

**Causes:**
- **Water loss into cells:** transient hypernatraemia due to severe exercise or electroshock-induced seizures — usually returns to normal 5–15 min post-event
- **Unreplaced water losses:** diabetes insipidus (see above); other causes — skin losses, GI losses, osmotic diuresis
- **Sodium overload:** accidental (e.g. NaHCO3 during metabolic acidosis treatment) or deliberate

> [!danger] Rapid Na increase can lead to central pontine myelinolysis (osmotic demyelination syndrome). In hypotonicity, astrocytes lose intracellular solutes to shed excess water and maintain isotonicity with plasma, causing the brain to "shrink". Rapid correction of hyponatraemia can cause further brain shrinkage as water is given up to maintain isotonicity → demyelination. Symptoms and positive MRI findings may not appear for up to 4 weeks after onset — a high degree of suspicion is needed.

> [!tip] Mnemonic: "From high to low, your brains will blow" (cerebral oedema — rapid drop in Na); "From low to high, your pons will die" (central pontine myelinolysis — rapid rise in Na).

**S/Smx:** thirst (often blunted in the elderly or those with impaired consciousness, which is why hypernatraemia disproportionately affects these groups), lethargy, irritability, confusion; severe — seizures, coma; signs of hypovolaemia if due to unreplaced water loss (dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, hypotension).

**Ix:** serum Na (*why:* confirms the diagnosis and severity, guiding correction rate below; *what:* >145 mmol/L per the definition above). Serum and urine osmolality (*why:* helps distinguish the underlying mechanism — a low urine osmolality despite hypernatraemia points toward diabetes insipidus (inability to concentrate urine despite the physiological stimulus to do so — see Diabetes Insipidus above), while a high urine osmolality points toward unreplaced insensible/GI losses or sodium overload, where the kidney is appropriately trying to conserve water; *what:* the specific pattern helps localise the cause per the categories above). Volume status assessment (*why:* similarly to hyponatraemia, categorising by volume status (hypovolaemic vs euvolaemic vs hypervolaemic) narrows the cause and guides fluid choice; *what:* clinical assessment of hydration status).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** correct the underlying cause where identifiable (e.g. treat diabetes insipidus per the Mx above, address ongoing losses); replace free water deficit, preferentially via the oral/enteral route if the patient can tolerate it, or IV 5% dextrose or hypotonic (0.45%) saline if not — **the correction rate matters just as much as in hyponatraemia and for a related reason: rapid correction of chronic hypernatraemia can cause cerebral oedema**, since brain cells have adapted to the hypertonic state by accumulating intracellular osmoles (idiogenic osmoles) to prevent cellular shrinkage — correcting serum sodium too fast leaves the brain relatively hypotonic to itself, drawing water in and causing swelling (see the mnemonic above for this being the mirror-image risk to hyponatraemia overcorrection).
- **Definitive:** as for hyponatraemia, limit the correction rate to a similar order of magnitude (~8–10 mmol/L per 24h is a reasonable general limit, mirroring the hyponatraemia correction-rate principle above) — faster correction is more defensible in acute (developed over <48h) hypernatraemia, where the brain hasn't yet had time to accumulate protective idiogenic osmoles, than in chronic hypernatraemia.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address the underlying cause definitively (e.g. desmopressin for central DI — see Diabetes Insipidus above) to prevent recurrence; ensure adequate ongoing free water access, particularly important in patients with a blunted thirst mechanism (elderly, impaired consciousness) who may not reliably self-correct.

## 0.27 Weight Change — Differential Approach

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Weight change," High yield) — genuinely absent as a unifying differential despite the individual causes being scattered across this file (thyroid disease, Cushing's, diabetes) and others throughout the project. Given how many of the highest-yield endocrine conditions present via weight change, an organised approach to this presenting complaint is worth having explicitly, rather than only reaching the diagnosis once a specific disease is already suspected.

**The core organising principle:** weight change is a presenting complaint, not a diagnosis — the differential splits broadly into endocrine/metabolic causes (the focus here, given this file's scope), and non-endocrine causes (malignancy, GI disease, psychiatric, medication-related) that must be actively considered rather than assumed endocrine by default just because the patient has landed in this file's context.

**Unintentional weight loss:**
- **Endocrine:** hyperthyroidism/thyrotoxicosis (weight loss **despite** preserved or increased appetite — see Hyperthyroidism above, not repeated here — a genuinely useful discriminator from most other weight-loss causes, where appetite is typically reduced); poorly controlled diabetes mellitus (osmotic diuresis and catabolism from insulin deficiency — see Diabetes Mellitus above, not repeated here); Addison's disease/adrenal insufficiency (see Addison's Disease above, not repeated here); phaeochromocytoma (via a hypermetabolic state).
- **Non-endocrine — actively screen for these, not just the endocrine differential:** malignancy (a red flag requiring genuine exclusion, particularly with other B-symptoms or an unexplained pattern); malabsorption/GI disease (coeliac disease, IBD, chronic pancreatitis); chronic infection (TB, HIV); psychiatric (depression, anxiety, eating disorders — see [[14_05a_Psych_-_Eating_Disorders]] for the disease-level detail, not repeated here, though see the note below on disordered-eating caution); medications; heart failure or COPD (cachexia in advanced disease).

**Unintentional weight gain:**
- **Endocrine:** hypothyroidism (see Hypothyroidism above, not repeated here); Cushing's syndrome/disease (see Cushing's Syndrome/Disease above, not repeated here — specifically causing central/truncal weight gain with peripheral muscle wasting, a distinguishing pattern from generalised obesity); PCOS (see [[17_01_FGM__Amenorrhoea__PCOS]] Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), not repeated here); insulinoma (via recurrent hypoglycaemia driving compensatory eating) and other causes of hyperinsulinaemia.
- **Non-endocrine:** medication-related (many antipsychotics, some antidepressants, corticosteroids, some antiepileptics, insulin and sulfonylureas specifically among diabetes medications, and other drug classes); fluid retention (heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, liver disease — see the relevant disease-specific entries elsewhere in this project, not repeated here) — genuinely important to distinguish fluid-driven weight gain from adipose-tissue gain, given the causes and urgency differ completely; simple positive energy balance (genuinely the most common cause in the general population, and shouldn't be assumed to indicate pathology by default).

**Ix — guided by the history and examination, not a reflexive standard panel:**
- Baseline: FBC, U&Es, LFTs, TFTs, glucose/HbA1c, coeliac screen if malabsorption is suspected, CRP/ESR/inflammatory markers.
- Further targeted testing (cortisol/short synacthen test, further imaging, endoscopy) guided by specific findings and red flags rather than ordered indiscriminately for every patient with a weight-change presentation.

> [!info] A genuinely important clinical caution specific to this presentation: **unintentional weight loss and rapid or extreme weight change can itself be a sign of disordered eating**, which requires a different, careful approach — see the disordered-eating-specific guidance already established in this project's wellbeing/psychiatric content, not repeated here; this differential should be held in mind alongside the organic causes above, particularly in younger patients, rather than assumed absent by default.
