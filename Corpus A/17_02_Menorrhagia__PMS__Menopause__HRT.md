## Abnormal Uterine Bleeding — Approach and DDx

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV — four separate rows in the Gynaecology & Breast category ("Abnormal vaginal bleeding," "Intermenstrual bleeding," "Post-coital bleeding," all Medium yield, and "The menstrual cycle and its disorders: ... Abnormal uterine bleeding ..." High yield) had no consolidating entry. The individual *causes* were all built across the 17_ files, but nothing gave the approach to a patient who presents with the *symptom* — which is what an OSCE or MCQ actually tests. Built as an index and approach that points to the existing disease entries rather than duplicating them. Verified against RANZCOG C-Gyn 6 "Investigation of intermenstrual and postcoital bleeding," the Cancer Council Australia clinical pathway "Investigation of people with abnormal vaginal bleeding," and the FIGO PALM-COEIN system, Aug 2026.

- **D:** bleeding from the uterus that is abnormal in *volume, regularity, timing, or duration*. The clinically useful sub-patterns, because each carries a different differential and urgency:
  - **Heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia)** — see Menorrhagia below.
  - **Intermenstrual bleeding (IMB)** — bleeding between otherwise normal periods.
  - **Postcoital bleeding (PCB)** — bleeding after intercourse.
  - **Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB)** — any bleeding ≥12 months after the last period. **Treat as endometrial cancer until proven otherwise** (see [[17_09_Cervical__Vaginal_and_Endometrial_Cancer]] Endometrial cancer, not repeated here).

**First question, always: is she pregnant?** A urinary/serum βhCG comes before any other reasoning in a person of reproductive age, because it redirects the entire differential to ectopic pregnancy and miscarriage (see [[17_04_Ectopic_Pregnancy_and_GTD]] and [[17_03_Termination_of_Pregnancy_and_Miscarriage]] Miscarriage, not repeated here).

**DDx — the FIGO PALM-COEIN system.** Worth actually learning as a structure rather than a mnemonic to recite: the **PALM** half is structural and therefore *imageable or biopsiable*, the **COEIN** half is non-structural and diagnosed on history and bloods. That split is the whole point — it tells you which arm of the work-up will find the answer.

| | Cause | Where it is built |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Polyp (endometrial or cervical) | [[17_09_Cervical__Vaginal_and_Endometrial_Cancer]] Endometrial hyperplasia (adjacent); cervical polyps commonly incidental on speculum |
| **A** | Adenomyosis | [[17_05_PID__Endometriosis__Fibroids]] Adenomyosis |
| **L** | Leiomyoma (fibroids) | [[17_05_PID__Endometriosis__Fibroids]] Fibroids (uterine leiomyomata) |
| **M** | Malignancy and hyperplasia | [[17_09_Cervical__Vaginal_and_Endometrial_Cancer]] Cervical cancer, Endometrial cancer, Endometrial hyperplasia |
| **C** | Coagulopathy | [[10_07_Haemonc_-_Platelet_and_Clotting_Disorders__Neutropaenia]] Von Willebrand disease (vWD) — the one most often missed; consider it in heavy bleeding since menarche |
| **O** | Ovulatory dysfunction | [[17_01_FGM__Amenorrhoea__PCOS]] Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS); also thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia, perimenopause, the extremes of reproductive age |
| **E** | Endometrial (primary local haemostatic disorder) | a diagnosis of exclusion once structural causes are excluded |
| **I** | Iatrogenic | hormonal contraception (breakthrough bleeding, especially in the first 3 months), missed pills, anticoagulants, tamoxifen, copper IUD |
| **N** | Not otherwise classified | e.g. arteriovenous malformation, caesarean scar niche |

**Also consider, and easy to forget because they are not uterine at all:** infection — chlamydia/gonorrhoea cervicitis and PID (see [[17_05_PID__Endometriosis__Fibroids]] Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)) — cervical ectropion, atrophic vaginitis in the postmenopausal patient, trauma, and a retained foreign body (including a forgotten tampon).

> [!danger] Red flags — these change the urgency, not just the work-up
> - **Any postmenopausal bleeding**, however slight or however brief.
> - **Persistent or recurrent PCB or IMB** — these are the two symptoms that specifically prompt exclusion of genital tract malignancy.
> - A visible cervical lesion on speculum — refer regardless of any test result.
> - Haemodynamic compromise, or bleeding with pregnancy-related pain.

**Ix — the sequence matters more than the list:**
1. **βhCG** first, as above.
2. **Speculum and bimanual examination.** Not optional in IMB or PCB — the point is to *see* the cervix. A cervical cancer can be visible when every test is still pending.
3. **Cervical screening (co-test — HPV plus liquid-based cytology).** The genuinely Australia-specific point an intern must not get wrong: in a person with abnormal vaginal bleeding this is a **symptomatic co-test**, ordered on the basis of the symptom, and it is done **irrespective of when their last routine screening test was due or what it showed** — do not defer it to the National Cervical Screening Program schedule (see [[17_09_Cervical__Vaginal_and_Endometrial_Cancer]] Cervical cancer screening for the routine programme itself, not repeated here). Equally important: **a negative co-test does not close the case** — persistent or recurrent PCB, unexplained persistent IMB, or any PMB still require referral to exclude genital tract malignancy.
4. **Endocervical/vaginal swabs or first-pass urine NAAT** for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, particularly in younger patients with IMB or PCB, where cervicitis is a common and eminently treatable cause.
5. **FBC** (*why:* quantifies the consequence of the bleeding and can itself change urgency; *what:* iron-deficiency anaemia) and **ferritin**. **Coagulation screen and von Willebrand studies** if bleeding has been heavy since menarche, or there is a personal/family bleeding history.
6. **TFTs** if there are ovulatory-dysfunction features.
7. **Transvaginal ultrasound** — the structural arm of PALM: assesses endometrial thickness, fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, adnexae.
8. **Endometrial sampling (Pipelle) ± hysteroscopy** where endometrial pathology must be excluded — postmenopausal bleeding, and premenopausal patients with risk factors for hyperplasia/malignancy (prolonged anovulation, obesity, diabetes, unopposed oestrogen, tamoxifen, family history) or bleeding persisting despite treatment.

**Mx:** treat the cause found — follow the linked entry above. Where the work-up is negative and bleeding is heavy but ovulatory, management is as per Menorrhagia below (Mirena IUS, tranexamic acid, mefenamic acid, COCP), not repeated here.

## Menorrhagia

**D**: heavy menstrual bleeding that interferes with quality of life.

**Causes**
- Dysfunctional uterine bleeding (menorrhagia without underlying pathology)
- Anovulatory cycles
- Uterine fibroids
- Hypothyroidism
- Intrauterine devices (copper coil)
- Pelvic inflammatory disease
- Bleeding disorders

**Ix**
- FBC, haematinics if anaemic, TSH, cervical smear, STI screen (as indicated by history)
- If risk factors for endometrial cancer or history suggestive of endometrial pathology: refer for outpatient hysteroscopy ± biopsy
- Imaging: US

**Mx**
- **Mirena IUS**: ↓bleeding via intrauterine levonorgestrel release (causes endometrial atrophy); SE: irregular bleeding for first 4–6 months + progestogenic effects
- **COCP**: effective in ↓bleeding but many contraindications
- **3rd line**: IM progestogens (e.g. medroxyprogesterone)
- If patient does not require contraception: mefenamic acid (useful if dysmenorrhoea also present) or tranexamic acid — start on first day of period
- **Surgery**: indicated for fibroids >3cm, inadequate response to previous treatment, or declined medical Mx — endometrial ablation, myomectomy, hysterectomy

## Premenstrual syndrome (PMS)

**D**: repetitive, cyclical, physical and behavioural symptoms occurring in the luteal phase of the normal menstrual cycle.

**A/P**: serotonin and GABA implicated.

**S/smx**: anxiety, stress, fatigue, mood swings, bloating, breast pain.

**Mx**
- **Mild**: lifestyle changes — regular sleep, exercise, smoking cessation, ↓alcohol; regular, frequent, small, balanced meals rich in complex carbohydrates
- **Moderate**: new-generation COCP (e.g. Yasmin)
- **Severe**: SSRI — can be taken continuously or only during luteal phase (e.g. days 15–28 of cycle)

### Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)
Most severe form of PMS.

> [!info] DSM-5 criteria
> **≥1 of the following must be present:**
> - Mood swings, sudden sadness
> - Anger, irritability
> - Sense of hopelessness, depressed mood, self-critical thoughts
> - Tension, anxiety, feeling on edge
>
> **Plus ≥5 total symptoms**, including:
> - Difficulty concentrating
> - Change in appetite
> - Diminished interest in usual activities
> - Easy fatiguability, ↓energy
> - Feeling overwhelmed
> - Breast tenderness (cyclical mastalgia), bloating, weight gain, joint/muscle aches
> - Sleeping too much or not enough

## Menopause

**D**: period of waning fertility leading up to the last period.

> [!info] Verified against the Australasian Menopause Society (AMS), Aug 2026 — Australian average menopause age is **51 years**, essentially the same as the UK figure (52yo) — not a meaningful difference, but stated as the AMS's own figure rather than a UK statistic. AMS specifically notes most women become menopausal between 50–54 years, with <40 classified as premature menopause and 41–45 as early menopause — terminology worth using consistently with the rest of this entry.
> Average age in Australia is 51yo (AMS).

- Retrospective diagnosis when menses has ceased for ≥12 consecutive months, not attributable to other reasons
- **Premature menopause**: <40yo
- **Perimenopause**: period marked by menstrual irregularity, ending 12 months after final menstrual period

**R**: 40–60yo (tends to occur earlier — in 40s — in Asian women), cancer treatment, smoking (2y earlier), ovarian surgery.

**A**: natural process triggered by declining oocyte numbers.

**P**: ↓ovarian production of progesterone, oestradiol, and testosterone → various symptoms.

**S/smx**
- Vasomotor (80%): hot flushes (may occur every few minutes for >10y), night sweats, palpitations
- Urogenital changes (35%): vaginal dryness/atrophy, urinary frequency, breast/skin atrophy
- Psychological: 10% have anxiety and depression ± short-term memory impairment
- Long-term complications: ↑cardiovascular disease risk, ↑osteoporosis risk

**Mx**
- Confirm menopause (rule out thyroid and psychiatric problems)
- Diet and exercise changes
- Menorrhagia: Mirena coil; consider referral for hysteroscopy if symptoms suggest endometrial pathology
- Use contraception until >1y amenorrhoea if >50yo, >2y if <50yo
- Vaginal dryness: oestrogen cream 0.1% PV nightly for 2w, then 2x/week as required
- HRT (see below)

## Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)

**D**: oestrogen + progesterone replacement.

**Indications**
- Menopausal symptoms: vasomotor, mood disorders, urogenital symptoms
- Premature menopause: primarily for osteoporosis prevention — continue until 50yo

**Benefits**
- ↓vasomotor symptoms via oestrogen — benefit by ~4w, max effect at 3 months
- Urogenital symptoms — benefit may take several months; continue long-term
- ↓osteoporotic fracture risk — sustained/lifelong treatment necessary
- ↓colorectal cancer risk

**Risks**

> [!info] Verified against the Australasian Menopause Society (AMS), Aug 2026 — **the figures below are genuinely consistent with current AMS risk communication**, not UK-specific numbers needing replacement; AMS independently cites the same ~2.3% per-year-of-use breast cancer risk figure and describes it as "very small" in absolute terms, similar in magnitude to other common risk factors like being overweight. AMS-specific framing worth adding: **no increased cardiovascular risk if MHT (AMS's preferred term — Menopausal Hormone Therapy, used interchangeably with HRT) is started within 10 years of menopause onset or before age 60** — the "timing hypothesis," a genuinely important nuance the original note doesn't capture; VTE risk with patches/gels is minimal-to-none, while tablets roughly double the (still very low) baseline risk — consistent with the "lower risk with patches" note below.
> - Breast cancer risk ↑ by 2.3% per year of use, dependent on duration and type of HRT; risk ↓ to baseline 5y after stopping. Highest risk with combined HRT (not seen in those starting HRT for premature menopause); different progestogens carry different risk levels — micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone appear to carry lower risk than synthetic progestogens such as norethisterone
> - Endometrial cancer (RR 2.3) if unopposed oestrogen used in those with a uterus; risk persists ≥5y after stopping
> - ↑VTE risk (≥2x) with oral tablets, though absolute risk remains small (~1 extra case per 1,000 women); minimal or no increased VTE risk with patches or gels; most likely in the first year of use

**Contraindications**
- Breast cancer (past or present)
- Oestrogen-dependent cancer
- Undiagnosed vaginal bleeding
- Untreated endometrial hyperplasia
- Previous idiopathic or current VTE (unless already anticoagulated)
- Active or recent arterial thromboembolic disease (e.g. MI)
- Active liver disease with abnormal LFTs
- Pregnancy
- Thrombophilic disorder
