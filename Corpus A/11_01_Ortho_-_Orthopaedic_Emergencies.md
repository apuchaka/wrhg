## Septic arthritis

> [!note] See [[11_07b_Ortho_-_Osteomyelitis__Osteochondritis_Dissecans__Fat_Embolism__Charcot_Joint__Osteomalacia]] Osteomyelitis for the full skin-infection-to-invasive-*S. aureus*-disease pathway specifically documented in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians, including the specific finding that osteomyelitis/septic arthritis together are the second most common site for these invasive infections after skin/soft tissue infection itself — not repeated here.

- **D:** infection of ≥1 joints caused by pathogenic inoculation of microbes.
- **R:** underlying joint disease, prosthetic joint, ↑age (especially >80 years old), immunosuppression (e.g. HIV, diabetes), IVDU, contiguous spread (e.g. via skin infection or ulcers), exposure to ticks.
- **A/P:** 90% Staph or Strep — direct or haematogenous inoculation.

> [!danger] Consider *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* in young, sexually active patients.

- **S/Smx:** a hot, swollen, acutely painful joint with restriction of movement = septic arthritis until proven otherwise. Usually a single joint, but do not rule out if ≥2 joints affected. More insidious onset if TB. ± fever.
- **Ix:** synovial fluid sampling (prior to antibiotics if possible; under ultrasound guidance), blood cultures, joint imaging. Bloods (WCC, ESR, CRP, etc).
- **Mx:** IV antibiotics covering Gram-positive cocci — flucloxacillin or clindamycin IV for 2 weeks then PO. Needle aspiration to decompress the joint ("aspirate to dryness"). Joint washout may be needed.

> [!warning] Refer to orthopaedics for prosthetic joints or the hip.

- **P:** delayed or inadequate treatment can cause irreversible joint destruction and fatality.

> [!note] See also [[11_10_Ortho_-_Paediatric_Orthopaedics]] Septic arthritis vs transient synovitis, where the Kocher criteria for diagnosis of septic arthritis, for paediatric septic arthritis.

### Septic arthritis of a replaced joint

> [!warning] Refer to orthopaedics.

- Usually *Staph epidermidis*, other coagulase-negative Staph, or *Staph aureus*.
- Requires aspiration/biopsy in a sterile environment — 5 samples.
- Debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention.
- **Two-stage revision:** first operation removes the infected replacement and all suspected tissue, joint washout, antibiotic spacer, and IV antibiotics; second operation removes the antibiotic spacer, washes out the joint, and inserts a new total joint replacement. Success rates ~88–96%.
- **Single-stage revision:** infected replacement removed, washout, and replacement inserted in one operation.
- **P:** delayed treatment can lead to fibrosis, joint contracture, or extension of infection to deep palmar spaces and other structures.

## Compartment syndrome

- **D:** elevated interstitial pressure in a closed osteofascial compartment causing microvascular compromise.
- **R:** trauma, bleeding disorder, compression support (e.g. tight casts, dressings), thermal injury (burns), intense muscular activity (chronic).

> [!tip] Mnemonic — "6 Ps"
> - **P**ressure (earliest sign)
> - **P**ain (especially on movement, even passive)
> - **P**araesthesia
> - ± **P**allor
> - ↓**P**ulse
> - **P**aralysis

> [!danger] Gap-filled — the "6 Ps" were listed as if roughly equal-weight signs, without the genuinely critical clinical point that they occur in a **meaningful, mechanistically-driven order**, and that waiting for the later signs means the diagnostic window has likely already been missed. **Why the order matters:** compartment pressure rises progressively, but different tissues have different sensitivity to the resulting ischaemia. **Nerve fibres are exquisitely sensitive to reduced perfusion** — this is why pain (out of proportion to the apparent injury, worse with passive stretch) and paraesthesia appear **early**, reflecting genuine but still-reversible nerve dysfunction. **Arterial pulses, by contrast, require compartment pressure to exceed arterial pressure specifically to be lost** — a much higher threshold than what's needed to compromise venous and capillary outflow (which happens much earlier, and actually worsens compartment pressure further in a vicious cycle by trapping more fluid in the compartment). **This means pulselessness is a genuinely late, ominous finding, not an early diagnostic sign** — by the time the pulse is lost, compartment pressure is already severe. **Paralysis is similarly late**, reflecting more advanced, potentially irreversible ischaemic damage to muscle and nerve tissue. **The genuinely critical practical consequence: a normal, palpable pulse does NOT exclude compartment syndrome** — relying on pulse checks to rule it out is a dangerous misconception, given the pulse is preserved throughout the entire early, still-reversible phase of the condition. Diagnosis and treatment should be driven by pain/paraesthesia and clinical suspicion (and measured compartment pressure where available), not by waiting for pulselessness or paralysis to develop.

- **Ix/Dx:** measure intracompartmental pressure (>20mmHg is abnormal, >40mmHg is diagnostic). Imaging (e.g. XR) not useful, as the pathology is unlikely to show up.
- **Mx:**
  - Relieve pressure — release dressing; fasciotomy (within 6h of symptom onset).
  - Rhabdomyolysis — see the dedicated entry below for the full Ix/Mx detail, not repeated here.
  - If necrotic tissue is found, consider debridement ± amputation.

## Rhabdomyolysis

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Rhabdomyolysis," Medium yield) — genuinely absent as a standalone entry despite being referenced as a complication of compartment syndrome immediately above, and being relevant to several other crush/ischaemic injury contexts throughout this project.

- **D:** breakdown of skeletal muscle tissue releasing intracellular contents (myoglobin, potassium, phosphate, creatine kinase) into the circulation.
- **A/P:** any cause of significant muscle injury/necrosis — trauma/crush injury (including prolonged compression, e.g. a fall with an extended "long lie" before discovery, not just direct crush), compartment syndrome (per above), extreme exertion, seizures, prolonged immobilisation, certain drugs/toxins (statins, particularly in combination with interacting drugs; illicit stimulants), severe electrolyte disturbance, and infection/sepsis-related myositis.
- **S/Smx:** muscle pain, swelling, and weakness in the affected area — though **symptoms can be surprisingly mild or absent relative to the degree of biochemical derangement**, meaning a normal-appearing patient doesn't exclude significant rhabdomyolysis if the mechanism is compatible; **classically described dark "tea-coloured" urine** reflects myoglobinuria, though this is a late and inconsistently-present sign, not something to wait for before investigating.
- **Ix:** **CK (creatine kinase)** (*why:* the key diagnostic and severity marker; *what:* markedly elevated, often several times the upper limit of normal — there's no universally agreed diagnostic threshold, but a level substantially above baseline in the appropriate clinical context supports the diagnosis, and trending CK over time helps track resolution). U&Es (*why:* screens for the acute kidney injury complication below and for the electrolyte derangements directly caused by muscle breakdown; *what:* rising creatinine if AKI develops; **hyperkalaemia** from intracellular potassium release — a genuinely dangerous, potentially arrhythmogenic complication requiring the same urgent management principles as hyperkalaemia from any cause; hypocalcaemia (calcium is drawn into damaged muscle) can also occur). Urinalysis (*why:* urine dipstick positive for "blood" without visible red cells on microscopy is a classic pattern reflecting myoglobinuria rather than true haematuria, given the dipstick reagent can't distinguish haemoglobin from myoglobin; *what:* blood-positive dipstick with a bland/acellular microscopy supports myoglobinuria). ECG (*why:* screens for hyperkalaemia-related changes given the arrhythmia risk above; *what:* peaked T waves, widened QRS, or other hyperkalaemic changes if significant hyperkalaemia present).
- **Mx:**
  - **Immediate/acute:** **aggressive IV fluid resuscitation** — the cornerstone of treatment, aimed at maintaining good urine output to flush myoglobin through the kidneys and reduce the AKI risk below; treat hyperkalaemia urgently if present, following the same general principles as hyperkalaemia from any cause; identify and address the underlying cause (e.g. relieve compartment syndrome per the entry above, stop a causative drug).
  - **Definitive:** urinary alkalinisation (IV sodium bicarbonate) is sometimes used, aiming to reduce myoglobin's nephrotoxicity, though evidence for benefit over aggressive fluid resuscitation alone is mixed and it's not universally used; monitor CK trend and renal function closely, given the risk of AKI can evolve over the following days even after initial stabilisation.
  - **Chronic/long-term:** haemodialysis is reserved for severe AKI/refractory hyperkalaemia not responding to medical management — a minority of cases, given most rhabdomyolysis-related AKI resolves with adequate fluid resuscitation and supportive care if treated promptly.
- **Complications:** acute kidney injury (myoglobin is directly nephrotoxic, and precipitates within renal tubules, particularly in the setting of dehydration/acidic urine — this is the major complication driving the aggressive fluid resuscitation approach above), hyperkalaemia-related arrhythmia, DIC in severe cases, compartment syndrome itself can both cause and (via ongoing muscle ischaemia) worsen rhabdomyolysis in a feedback loop.

## Spinal cord compression

- **A:** trauma, vertebral fracture, intervertebral disc herniation, tumours, infection.
  - **Where the cause is malignant, the fuller entry is [[04_Neurology]] Malignant Spinal Cord Compression (MSCC)** — it owns the oncological presentation, including the back pain that precedes neurological signs by weeks and the UMN pattern that distinguishes cord compression from cauda equina syndrome. This entry covers cord compression from any cause; not repeated there.
- **S/Smx:**
  - Sensory: change in sensation below the affected level.

> [!danger] Pain and hemi-sensory loss.

  - Motor: weakness or paralysis.
  - Autonomic: constipation, urinary retention, dizziness (hypotension), cold/shivering/drowsiness, erectile dysfunction, abdominal pain and distention (ileus), syncope (bradycardia).

> [!info] Physical exam — reflexes. Absent reflexes should heighten suspicion.
> - Anal wink — contraction of the anal sphincter on stimulation of the perianal area.
> - Bulbocavernous reflex — tapping the dorsum of the penis causes contraction of pelvic floor muscles.
> - Cremasteric reflex — stimulus to the upper thigh causes scrotal elevation.
> - Babinski's sign positive is abnormal.

> [!note] Anatomy note: the conus medullaris ends at L1–L2, and the cauda equina extends from L2 to Co1.

> [!danger] Mx — spinal cord compression is a genuine emergency regardless of the underlying cause, and the general approach below applies while cause-specific management (below, and in the Cauda equina/Spinal tumours entries that follow) is arranged in parallel:
> - **Urgent whole-spine MRI** (with gadolinium where malignancy is suspected) — the imaging modality of choice; CT/CT myelogram if MRI contraindicated or unavailable. **Do not delay empirical treatment while awaiting imaging** if the clinical picture is convincing.
> - **Spinal immobilisation** (cervical collar ± thoracolumbar motion restriction) if a traumatic cause is suspected, to prevent further cord injury during assessment and transfer.
> - **Corticosteroids where a malignant or inflammatory cause is suspected** — dexamethasone reduces vasogenic oedema and is associated with improved neurological outcomes, started as soon as compression is suspected rather than only once confirmed. **The specific dose genuinely varies across current guidance** — recent (2026) NICE guidance for metastatic spinal cord compression specifies 16mg oral (or an equivalent parenteral dose); other current protocols (e.g. Emergency Neurological Life Support) favour a lower 10mg IV loading dose then 4mg every 6 hours, reflecting evidence that lower doses achieve comparable efficacy with a better safety profile than the much higher doses (e.g. 100mg loading) used historically — check current local protocol given this is a genuinely unsettled area rather than a single fixed international standard.
> - **Cause-specific definitive management, arranged urgently in parallel with the above:** surgical decompression for a compressive lesion amenable to surgery (see Cauda equina syndrome below for the disc-prolapse-specific timing evidence, not repeated here); radiotherapy ± surgery for malignant cord compression (see Spinal tumours below, not repeated here); urgent antibiotics ± surgical drainage if an infective cause (e.g. epidural abscess) is suspected.
> - **Analgesia** — most patients require opioid-level pain relief given the severity of the presenting pain.

### Cauda equina syndrome

- **D:** refers to any symptoms/signs below AND radiological confirmation of compression. Most commonly caused by central disc prolapse at L4/L5 or L5/S1.
  - **A second full entry exists at [[04_Neurology]] Cauda Equina Syndrome** — the two agree (whole-spine MRI, decompression within 24–48h, LMN pattern) and that one adds the anatomy (L2–S5 roots below the conus at ~L1/L2) and the saddle-anaesthesia questions to ask directly. Linked by the pairs audit (2026-08-29); the headers differ only in letter case, which is why the duplicate check never paired them.
- **S/Smx:** low back pain (common); bilateral sciatica (50% of cases); impotence; saddle anaesthesia; sensorimotor loss in lower extremities; neurogenic bladder dysfunction (usually overflow incontinence — a later sign associated with poorer prognosis); ± bowel dysfunction.
- **Ix:** urgent (same-day, out-of-hours where necessary) whole-spine MRI — genuinely time-critical given the diagnosis is confirmed radiologically, and imaging delay directly translates into treatment delay.
- **Mx:** **surgical decompression is the definitive treatment, and is genuinely time-critical** — a surgical/neurosurgical emergency. Current evidence supports decompression **within 48 hours of symptom onset**, given later surgery is associated with significantly worse neurological, bladder, and sexual function outcomes (though some benefit from decompression can still occur beyond this window, so late presentation should not be treated as futile). Urgent referral to spinal/neurosurgery for same-day assessment once suspected clinically — **do not wait for MRI confirmation before making the referral**, given the imaging and surgical pathway both need to move in parallel; analgesia and catheterisation (for urinary retention/overflow incontinence) as supportive measures while arranging definitive care.
- **P:** complete symptom resolution occurs in under half of surgically treated patients even with prompt decompression — genuinely important to convey honestly, given cauda equina syndrome can leave permanent bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction even when management is appropriately urgent, not just when treatment is delayed.

### Back pain red flags → urgent MRI

> [!danger] Red flags for back pain
> - Age <20 or >50
> - Progressive pain
> - Night/supine pain that prevents or disturbs sleep, or rest pain
> - Pain aggravated by straining
> - Thoracic back pain
> - Mechanical pain (↑ when standing, sitting, moving)
> - Localised spinal tenderness
> - Claudication
> - No symptomatic improvement with therapy
> - Unexplained weight loss
> - Past history of cancer
> - IVDU / steroid use
> - Symptoms hinting at cauda equina syndrome

### Spinal tumours

- Most often secondary to metastatic cancer — more common in lung, breast, prostate cancers.
- **S/Smx:** as with other spinal cord lesions; back pain is the earliest and most common symptom.
- **Ix:** whole spine MRI.
- **Mx:** high-dose PO dexamethasone; urgent oncology assessment to consider radiotherapy or surgery; palliation if not feasible.

## Open fractures

- **D:** fracture with skin breach.

> [!info] Gustilo-Anderson classification
> - **Type I:** wounds ≤1cm, minimal contamination or muscle damage.
> - **Type II:** wounds 1–10cm, moderate soft tissue injury.
> - **Type IIIA:** >10cm, high-energy, extensive soft tissue damage + contaminated. Adequate tissue for flap coverage. Farm injuries are automatically at least IIIA.
> - **Type IIIB:** same as IIIA but with extensive periosteal stripping; inadequate tissue for flap coverage.
> - **Type IIIC:** with vascular injury requiring vascular repair (regardless of degree of soft tissue injury).

- **Mx:**
  - ABCDE — full exposure to look for associated injuries.
  - Antibiotics required + tetanus booster (if no booster in the last 5 years).
  - Definitive management is surgical — ideally in theatre within 6h of injury: remove foreign material, remove devitalised tissue, extensive irrigation of the wound, stabilise & set fracture.

### Management of closed fractures

- Immobilise the fracture, including proximal and distal joints.
- Monitor and document neurovascular status, especially after reduction and immobilisation.
- No antibiotics needed if closed.

## Infective myositis (pyomyositis)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Infective Myositis," Medium yield) — genuinely absent despite being a recognised, if uncommon, cause of a hot/painful limb that's an important differential for the more commonly-taught entities (septic arthritis, cellulitis, necrotising fasciitis) already built throughout this project.

- **D:** a purulent bacterial infection of skeletal muscle, most classically forming a discrete intramuscular abscess.
- **A/P:** **Staph aureus causes >90% of cases** — the same dominant organism as septic arthritis above, worth noting as a pattern; historically termed "tropical pyomyositis" given higher prevalence in tropical/developing regions, but genuinely occurs worldwide including in temperate climates, so geography shouldn't be used to rule it out. The exact mechanism of muscle seeding during transient bacteraemia is incompletely understood — animal studies suggest normal muscle is relatively resistant to infection, with prior trauma, ischaemia, or strenuous activity to the affected muscle appearing to predispose it to seeding during bacteraemia.
- **R:** trauma/muscle injury (including intramuscular injections — a recognised iatrogenic risk factor), strenuous physical activity, skin infections or breaches, IVDU, diabetes, immunosuppression (including HIV), connective tissue disease.
- **S/Smx:** classically affects large muscle groups — thigh muscles most commonly, though **paraspinal and iliopsoas involvement is a specifically important, easily-missed presentation** given it can closely mimic more common causes of back/hip pain (mechanical back pain, sciatica), leading to diagnostic delay; fever, localised muscle pain and swelling, and reduced range of movement of the affected limb. **A genuinely important diagnostic pitfall: presentation can be insidious and non-specific**, particularly with deep muscle involvement (paraspinal/iliopsoas), so infective myositis should be considered in a patient with unexplained fever and focal deep muscle pain not responding to standard analgesia, especially in someone with a relevant risk factor above.
- **Ix:** inflammatory markers (FBC, CRP, ESR) (*why:* supports an infective/inflammatory process, though non-specific; *what:* typically raised, though may be normal early or in an immunocompromised patient, so a normal result doesn't exclude the diagnosis if clinical suspicion is high). Blood cultures (*why:* identifies the causative organism, particularly valuable given deep-muscle aspiration/biopsy isn't always immediately feasible or may itself be sterile in early/non-purulent disease — repeated blood cultures over several days can improve yield if initial cultures are negative but suspicion remains high; *what:* may grow *S. aureus* or another causative organism). MRI (*why:* the imaging modality of choice, given its superior soft-tissue resolution for identifying muscle oedema/inflammation and any discrete abscess collection, directly guiding both diagnosis and the drainage-vs-antibiotics-alone decision below; *what:* muscle oedema/enhancement ± a defined fluid collection consistent with abscess). Ultrasound is a reasonable, more accessible first-line imaging option, particularly useful for image-guided aspiration where a collection is identified, though less sensitive than MRI for early/diffuse disease without a discrete collection.
- **Mx:**
  - **Immediate/acute:** empirical broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering *S. aureus* (including MRSA cover where locally relevant, per the same general principle as septic arthritis/cellulitis elsewhere in this project) pending culture results; supportive care, with escalation to sepsis management (see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Sepsis) if the patient is systemically unwell.
  - **Definitive:** image-guided or surgical drainage for any identified discrete abscess, given antibiotics alone are unlikely to adequately penetrate/resolve a purulent collection — the same general "source control matters as much as antibiotics" principle as abscesses elsewhere in this project; antibiotics de-escalated/tailored once culture and sensitivity results are available, with a typical total duration of several weeks (often IV transitioning to oral, analogous to the osteomyelitis/discitis duration principles in [[11_07b_Ortho_-_Osteomyelitis__Osteochondritis_Dissecans__Fat_Embolism__Charcot_Joint__Osteomalacia]] and [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]] Discitis, not repeated here).
  - **Chronic/long-term:** monitor for and address any underlying predisposing condition (diabetes control, HIV management, addressing an injection-drug-use source) given these are recognised risk factors for both the initial episode and recurrence.

## Added from unverified layer — the two other closed-space hand infections
`SRC:K2_Skin_and_Soft_Tissue_Infection §0.3` `UNVERIFIED — model knowledge, not source-checked.` `NO-BASELINE — absent from the corpus before this merge; no inherited layer disagrees with it.`

> [!danger] **Felon — infection of the fingertip pulp**
> The pulp is **divided into closed compartments by fibrous septa running from the periosteum to the skin**, so infection there raises compartment pressure rather than spreading. The result is a **tensely swollen, exquisitely painful fingertip with throbbing pain that is worse than the appearance suggests.**
> **The pressure is the problem:** untreated it causes **skin necrosis, and osteomyelitis of the distal phalanx or septic arthritis of the DIP joint.**
> **It needs surgical drainage, not antibiotics alone** — the same principle as the tendon sheath below, for the same anatomical reason. Refer to hand surgery.

> [!warning] **Paronychia — infection of the nail fold**
> **Acute** — usually *S. aureus*, after a hangnail, nail-biting or a manicure. Erythema and swelling of the nail fold, progressing to a visible collection.
> **If it is fluctuant it must be drained; antibiotics alone will not resolve a collection**, and a neglected one can track around the nail as a run-around abscess or under the nail plate, which then has to come off.
> **Chronic** — over weeks in wet-work occupations, associated with *Candida* and irritant exposure. **Treated as a barrier and dermatitis problem, not as an acute bacterial infection**, and antibiotics do not help.

## Tendon sheath infection (hand)

> [!info] Kanavel's cardinal signs (flexor tenosynovitis)
> 1. Exquisite tenderness over the sheath, limited to the sheath.
> 2. Finger sits in a resting flexed position.
> 3. Exquisite pain on passively extending the finger, more marked proximally.
> 4. Fusiform ("sausage") swelling of the whole finger.
>
> If all 4 signs are present, >90% sensitive for flexor tenosynovitis.

- **Mx:** refer to hand surgeons. Elevation, IV antibiotics. Open drainage and washout required.
