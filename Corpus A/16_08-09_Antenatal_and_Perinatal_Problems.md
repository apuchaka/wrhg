## Small for gestational age (SGA)

**D**: <10th centile for gestational age, or abdominal circumference <10th centile.

**R**: maternal age >40, smoker, cocaine use, previous SGA baby, previous stillbirth, maternal/paternal SGA, chronic HTN, DM, renal impairment, antiphospholipid syndrome, heavy antepartum bleeding, echogenic bowel, pre-eclampsia, low PAPP-A.

**A**: abnormal trophoblast invasion tends to cause **asymmetrical** growth restriction (head sparing, ↓abdominal circumference). Genetic abnormalities (e.g. trisomies), congenital abnormalities and infections usually cause **symmetrically** small fetus.

**Screening**: mothers with risk factors monitored more closely; if <10th centile or static growth → refer for fetal US.

**Mx**
- Once identified: serial fetal size measurements; umbilical artery Doppler from 26–28w + every 2–3w
- Normal Dopplers: aim induction at 37w
- Abnormal Dopplers: urgency of delivery determined by other factors; may require C-section
- Offer corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity up to 35+6w
- After birth: temperature regulation important; feed within 2h of birth + monitor for hypoglycaemia

**P**: adulthood risks — ↑HTN, coronary artery disease, T2DM, autoimmune thyroid disease.

## Large for gestational age (LGA)

**D**: >95th centile in weight for gestation.

**R**: FHx of LGA babies, maternal DM, obesity.

**Mx**: prepare for shoulder dystocia delivery. Babies prone to hypoglycaemia and hypocalcaemia — prompt feeding, monitor electrolytes. Left colon syndrome may develop (self-limiting condition clinically mimicking Hirschsprung disease).

## Abdominal pain in pregnancy — DDx

### Pregnancy-related causes

> [!danger] Red flags requiring urgent workup
> - **Pre-eclampsia** — especially in later pregnancy
> - **Cardiac causes** — chest, back, or epigastric pain, severe enough for opioids, requires full cardiac workup
> - **Placental abruption** — triad of abdominal pain + uterine rigidity/tenderness + vaginal bleeding (clinical diagnosis, don't rely on US); 1 in 80–200 pregnancies; high fetal loss if >50% placenta affected; prepare for DIC (33–50%) and PPH
> - **Uterine rupture**

- Uterine fibroids, uterine torsion, ovarian tumours

### Other causes

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| Appendicitis | ~1:1000 pregnancies; ↑mortality, ↑perforation risk; presentation may differ due to shifted appendix position; fetal mortality 1.5% (simple) vs ~30% (perforated). **Don't delay surgery — laparoscopy.** |
| Cholecystitis | ~1–6:10,000 pregnancies; similar presentation to non-pregnant (subcostal pain, N&V); US confirms stones; usually self-resolving, lap chole safe if surgery required |
| Gastroenteritis | Common; manage at home if otherwise well (ORS + rest); beware sepsis |
| Rectus sheath haematoma | Rare; Dx via US; laparoscopy if Dx in doubt or patient in shock |
| Pancreatitis | Rare, ↑mortality; Dx via serum amylase |
| UTI / pyelonephritis | See antenatal care notes (16.04) |

## Pre-eclampsia

**D**: disorder of pregnancy characterised by:
- New-onset HTN (SBP ≥140 and/or DBP ≥90 mmHg) after 20w
- Proteinuria (≥300mg/24h urine, dipstick ≥2+, or protein:creatinine ratio ≥30mg/mmol)
- Where proteinuria is absent: thrombocytopenia (platelets <100), renal insufficiency (SrCr >90µmol/L), impaired liver function, pulmonary oedema, or new-onset headache not attributable to other conditions

> [!info] Risk factors for prophylactic aspirin
> **High risk factors**: HTN disease in previous pregnancy, chronic HTN, CKD, T1/T2DM, autoimmune disease (e.g. SLE)
>
> **Moderate risk factors**: nulliparity, ≥40yo, previous pregnancy >10y ago, BMI ≥35 at booking, FHx of pre-eclampsia, twins

**A/P**: failure of normal trophoblast invasion → maladaptation of maternal spiral arterioles → vasoconstriction and capillary leaking → HTN + complications.

**S/smx (if severe)**: HTN (typically >160/110), proteinuria, headache, visual disturbances/papilloedema, RUQ/epigastric pain, hyperreflexia, HELLP syndrome.

**Ix**: urinalysis + bloods (Dx and complication screening), fetal US, umbilical artery Doppler velocimetry, amniotic fluid assessment.

**Mx**
- **Prevention**: in women with ≥1 high-risk or ≥2 moderate-risk factors — aspirin 150mg PO nightly, commencing before 16 weeks' gestation (ideally from 11–12 weeks, given earlier initiation hasn't shown additional benefit) and ceasing after 34 weeks — verified against the SOMANZ (Society of Obstetric Medicine of Australia and New Zealand) Hypertension in Pregnancy Guideline, the Australian-specific reference for this dose, Aug 2026. This is a genuinely more specific figure than a "75–150mg" range — SOMANZ specifically recommends 150mg based on RCT evidence in this population, though international guidance is not fully settled (WHO favours 75mg, citing postpartum haemorrhage risk at higher doses) — 150mg nightly is the correct Australian-specific standard to use here.
- New onset suspected: refer for emergency secondary care assessment; BP ≥160/110 → likely admission and observation; if BP remains high after antihypertensives, may need **prophylactic magnesium sulfate — given to prevent progression to eclampsia** (about **1%** of pre-eclampsia does progress).

> [!warning] **Magnesium sulfate is named here with no dose and no monitoring, and this is the entry where prophylaxis is actually started** — before any seizure has happened. The regimen and, more importantly, the **stop criteria** are owned by [[16_14-15_Obstetric_Emergencies]] Eclampsia, verified there against Queensland Clinical Guidelines: **4g IV over 20 minutes** via a controlled infusion device, then **1g/h for 24 hours**, continued for 24 hours after the last seizure or after delivery. **Stop for RR <12/min, loss of tendon reflexes, or urine output <20 mL/h** — magnesium toxicity is the reason this drug is monitored rather than simply given, and none of that was reachable from here. Linked by the G34 round, 2026-08-29.
- For HTN Mx (>150/100): PO labetalol; nifedipine (e.g. if asthmatic) or hydralazine
- Plan for baby's birth

## HELLP syndrome

**= H**aemolysis, **E**levated **L**iver enzymes, **L**ow **P**latelets.

**R**: white ethnicity, mother >35yo, obesity, chronic HTN, DM, autoimmune disorders, migraine, abnormal placentation (e.g. molar pregnancy), previous pregnancy with pre-eclampsia.

**A/P**: thought to be a severe form of pre-eclampsia (~10–20% of severe pre-eclampsia patients develop HELLP).

> [!info] Gap-filled — HELLP's three components were named without explaining why this specific triad occurs together, or how it connects to the pre-eclampsia mechanism established above. **All three components stem from the same underlying process**: pre-eclampsia's endothelial damage (see the trophoblast invasion/spiral arteriole mechanism above, not repeated here) becomes severe enough to cause **widespread microthrombi and fibrin deposition within small blood vessels** — a microangiopathic process. As red cells are forced through these partially obstructed, fibrin-laden vessels, they're mechanically sheared and fragmented, producing **haemolysis** (the schistocytes/fragmented red cells seen on blood film below are the direct visual evidence of this mechanical damage). The same fibrin deposition and microthrombi occurring specifically within the **hepatic sinusoids** cause localised ischaemia and periportal/focal hepatocyte necrosis, releasing **elevated liver enzymes**. And the same widespread endothelial damage and microthrombi formation **consumes platelets** faster than they can be replaced, producing **thrombocytopenia**. This is precisely why HELLP is understood as an extension of pre-eclampsia's endothelial dysfunction rather than three coincidentally co-occurring abnormalities — one underlying process (microangiopathy) manifesting in three different ways depending on which tissue bears the brunt of the vascular damage.

**S/smx**: N&V, RUQ pain, lethargy.

**Ix**: FBC with differential incl. platelets (↓Hb, thrombocytopenia); peripheral blood smear (schistocytes = haemolysis); liver enzymes (AST/ALT >70, or >2x ULN); urinalysis; clotting screen.

**Mx**
- If suspected: start IV magnesium sulfate to prevent seizures
- Start dexamethasone
- Definitive Mx: deliver the baby

**Complications**: eclampsia, fetal IUGR/prematurity, liver involvement, heart failure, haemorrhage (placental abruption, intra-abdominal haemorrhage, ICH).

## Oligohydramnios

**D**: reduced amniotic fluid — amniotic fluid index ≤5cm (or <5th centile).

**Causes**
- Idiopathic (cause may be found after baby is born)
- Maternal: uteroplacental insufficiency (e.g. pre-eclampsia), medications (e.g. NSAIDs)
- Placental: abruption, thrombosis/infarction
- Fetal: chromosomal abnormalities (e.g. Potter sequence — bilateral renal agenesis + pulmonary hypoplasia → ↓urine production → ↓amniotic fluid); growth restriction or death; post-term gestation; ruptured fetal membranes

## Polyhydramnios

**D**: excessive amniotic fluid — suspect if uterine size large for gestational age; amniotic fluid index ≥24cm.

**Causes**
- Idiopathic
- Fetal structural anomaly impeding swallowing (e.g. oesophageal atresia, cleft lip)
- Fetal neuromuscular disorder impeding swallowing
- Genetic syndromes (e.g. trisomies, Prader-Willi)
- High fetal cardiac output state (e.g. severe anaemia)
- Maternal diabetes mellitus
- Macrosomia
- Hydrops fetalis

## Prematurity

**D**: infant born before 37w gestation. Extreme prematurity: <28w. Term infants: 37–41w.

**R**: intrauterine infection, preterm premature rupture of membranes, pre-eclampsia/PIH, placental abruption, antepartum haemorrhage.

**Risks/complications in premature infants**

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Respiratory distress syndrome | 2° immature lungs/surfactant deficiency; Mx: antenatal corticosteroids + postnatal surfactant; ↑risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia |
| Sepsis | ↑risk with ↓gestational age and invasive procedures |
| Anaemia of prematurity | Treat with adequate nutrition + iron supplementation |
| Nutritional deficiencies | Early breast milk or premature formula |
| Jaundice | 2° inadequate GI bilirubin losses + poor enteral nutrition; Mx: phototherapy |
| Intraventricular haemorrhage / white matter injury | 2° immature cerebral blood vessels; ↑mortality and neurodevelopmental dysfunction |
| Necrotising enterocolitis | Mx: antenatal corticosteroids + breast milk ± probiotics |
| Patent ductus arteriosus | — |
| Retinopathy of prematurity | Associated with excessive O2 exposure; maintain sats 85–93% until 32w post-menstrual age; regular ophthalmology screening |
| Cerebral palsy / neurodevelopmental impairment | — |
| ADHD / behavioural sequelae | — |
| ↑risk of abuse/neglect | — |

> [!note] Extreme prematurity carries the highest mortality risk of all prematurity categories.

## Postmaturity

**D**: infant born after 42w pregnancy.

**R**: previous prolonged pregnancy.

**Risks to baby & mother**: intrapartum deaths (4x risk), early neonatal deaths (3x risk), ↑induction/operative delivery rates, ↑placental insufficiency risk, macrosomia (>4kg)/shoulder dystocia/fetal injury, more ossified/less mouldable fetal skull, ↑meconium passage in labour (↑meconium aspiration risk), ↑fetal distress in labour, ↑C-section rates.

**Mx**
- At 38w visit: discuss plan, arrange 41w visit if not delivered
- Membrane sweep: finger inserted through cervix, membrane palpated — thought to induce natural prostaglandins; offered at 40w and 41w visits
- Induction at 41w reduces fetal death: vaginal prostaglandin + oxytocin
- If mother declines induction: twice-weekly CTG + amniotic fluid depth estimation (detects fetuses becoming hypoxic)

## Birth injuries

| Injury | Description |
|---|---|
| Moulding (not an injury) | Overriding of skull bones |
| Cephalhaematoma | Subperiosteal swelling, limited by suture lines, fluctuant; may take weeks to resolve; may contribute to jaundice |
| Caput succedaneum | Oedematous scalp swelling superficial to periosteum; **not** limited by suture lines; from venous congestion + exuded serum during labour ("chignon" if ventouse-caused); resolves in first few days |
| Erb's palsy | Brachial trunk nerve damage — flaccid arm/hand in fixed posture; most resolve; exclude fractured clavicle, arrange physio; unlikely to improve further if not resolved by 6 months |
| Subaponeurotic haematoma | Blood between aponeurosis and periosteum; associated with vacuum extraction; may contribute to anaemia or jaundice |
| Skull fractures | Associated with difficult forceps delivery or impacted head at 2nd-stage C-section; parietal/frontal bones most affected; check CNS signs → neurosurgery consult |
| Intracranial injuries | ICH associated with difficult/fast labour, instrumental or breech delivery, especially in premature babies; anoxia may cause intraventricular haemorrhage; asphyxia may cause ICH → cerebral palsy (extradural, subdural, or subarachnoid); supportive Mx |
| Fetal laceration | 1–2% of C-section deliveries (more common in breech C-section and post-membrane-rupture C-section); most superficial, heal without scarring; warn parents when consenting for C-section |

*See 15.23 for minor neonatal problems.*
