## 0.1 Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

**D:** acute decline in kidney function. According to KDIGO, defined as:
- ↑SCr ≥26.5 µmol/L in 48h, OR
- ↑SCr ≥1.5x baseline in the last 7 days, OR
- Urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6h

> [!info] AKI staging (KDIGO)
> **Stage 1:** ↑SCr ≥26.5 µmol/L, or ↑SCr 1.5–1.9x baseline, or UO <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12h
> **Stage 2:** ↑SCr 2.0–2.9x baseline, or UO <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥12h
> **Stage 3:** ↑SCr ≥3x baseline, or ↑SCr ≥353.6 µmol/L, or UO <0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥24h, or anuria for ≥12h, or initiation of RRT, or (in patients <18yo) eGFR <35 mL/min

**A — divided into pre-renal, intrinsic, and post-renal causes:**
- **Pre-renal:** hypovolaemia (dehydration, shock, diarrhoea), renal artery stenosis
- **Intrinsic:** glomerulonephritis; acute tubular necrosis (45% of cases, e.g. secondary to sepsis); acute interstitial nephritis; rhabdomyolysis (e.g. fall + long lie); tumour lysis syndrome
- **Post-renal:** kidney stones in ureter/bladder, benign prostatic hyperplasia, external compression of the ureter

**R:** ↑age, underlying kidney disease (e.g. diabetic nephropathy), hospitalisation for various causes (15% of inpatients develop AKI), drugs (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, ACEIs, ARBs, diuretics)

**S/Smx:** may be asymptomatic until late; usually picked up on routine bloods or with decreased urine output. Pulmonary and peripheral oedema; arrhythmias secondary to electrolyte disturbances; features of uraemia (encephalopathy, pericarditis).

**Ix:**
- Bloods: U&Es (*why:* confirms the diagnosis and severity per the KDIGO staging above, and screens for the electrolyte complications (hyperkalaemia especially) that drive much of AKI's acute danger; *what:* rising creatinine per the staging criteria, ± hyperkalaemia)
- Urinalysis (*why:* helps localise the likely cause — blood and protein suggest an intrinsic glomerular process, while nitrites/leukocytes suggest an infective/obstructive cause — narrowing the pre-renal/intrinsic/post-renal differential above; *what:* consider intrinsic cause if positive blood & protein; consider infection if positive nitrites, leukocytes)
- Urine output (*why:* both a diagnostic criterion in its own right (see the KDIGO definition above) and a marker of ongoing renal perfusion/function to trend; *what:* ideally measured via catheter for accuracy, particularly in unwell/oliguric patients where precise fluid balance matters)
- Imaging — renal US (*why:* specifically screens for post-renal (obstructive) causes, which are often readily reversible if identified promptly — a genuinely different and more urgent Mx pathway than intrinsic or pre-renal AKI; *what:* hydronephrosis/hydroureter would indicate obstruction requiring urgent decompression)

> [!tip] Mx mnemonic "STOP AKI"
> **S**epsis — screen and treat
> **T**oxins — identify and stop (stop drugs that worsen renal function: NSAIDs except baby aspirin, aminoglycosides, ACEI, ARBs, diuretics; stop drugs with increased toxicity risk in AKI: metformin, lithium, digoxin)
> **O**ptimise volume status and BP — IV crystalloid 500mL bolus over 15 min (caution in HF, cirrhosis); withhold antihypertensives; may require inotropes
> **P**revent harm — treat reversible causes (e.g. obstruction); treat life-threatening complications; modify medication doses
> **A**void nephrotoxins; monitor **K**idney function; **I**nvestigate cause

### 0.1.1 Indications for Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT)

> [!danger] Mnemonic "AEIOU"
> **A**cidosis (pH <7.1) | **E**lectrolyte imbalance (e.g. K >6.5) | **I**ntoxication ("SLIME" — salicylates, lithium, isopropanol, methanol, ethylene glycol) ± drug overdose | **O**verload ± pulmonary oedema | **U**raemia (pericarditis, encephalopathy, end-organ failure)

**Mx of hyperkalaemia in AKI:** cardiac protection with IV calcium chloride or calcium gluconate + ECG; IV insulin/glucose + salbutamol nebuliser (see Endocrinology section for detail).

## 0.2 Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

**D:** abnormal kidney structure or function for ≥3 months. By definition this includes patients with only one kidney (e.g. kidney donors).

**A:** diabetic nephropathy, hypertension, chronic glomerulonephritis, chronic pyelonephritis, adult polycystic kidney disease, others

### 0.2.1 Staging

**By GFR (6 grades):**
| Grade | GFR |
|---|---|
> [!danger] **Before using any of the numbers below: eGFR is an estimate with conditions attached, and the corpus states the number in four files without stating them anywhere.** Added after the M10 audit found the same shape in the cognitive screening cutoffs — a threshold quoted in one place, its validity caveat in another or in none.
>
> **eGFR assumes creatinine is at steady state, so it is invalid in AKI.** *The mechanism:* the equations infer filtration from a creatinine that is presumed stable. In an evolving AKI, creatinine is still rising and lags behind the true fall in filtration — so the reported eGFR **overestimates function, and does so most when the patient is deteriorating fastest.** This is the trap worth carrying: the AKI staging above and the CKD G-staging below are in the same file, and **an eGFR reported during an AKI does not stage anything.** AKI is staged on creatinine change and urine output, which is why those criteria exist separately.
>
> **It is also inaccurate at extremes of muscle mass**, because creatinine comes from muscle. It **over**estimates function in someone with little muscle — the frail older patient, an amputee, someone with a spinal cord injury or advanced cirrhosis — and **under**estimates it in a very muscular person. The frail-elderly case is the one that matters most in practice: a "normal" eGFR in a small, sarcopenic 85-year-old can conceal genuinely impaired clearance, which is a **drug-dosing** problem before it is a staging one.
>
> **Practical consequence:** for renally-cleared drugs with a narrow margin, dose off the number the product information specifies (often creatinine clearance, not eGFR) and treat eGFR as a screening and staging tool rather than a dosing one — and never dose off an eGFR taken during an unstable creatinine.

| G1 | >90 mL/min with evidence of kidney damage (histopathology, imaging, or lab findings) |
| G2 | 60–89 mL/min |
| G3a | 45–59 mL/min |
| G3b | 30–44 mL/min |
| G4 | 15–29 mL/min |
| G5 (end-stage) | <15 mL/min |

**By albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR):**
| Category | ACR |
|---|---|
| A1 (normal/mildly increased) | <3 mg/mmol |
| A2 (moderately increased) | 3–30 mg/mmol |
| A3 (severe) | >30 mg/mmol |

> [!note] Albumin is not normally excreted in urine, so an elevated ACR indicates severity. Protein:creatinine ratio is also used to monitor kidney function, but ACR is preferred in diabetics.

**S/Smx:** asymptomatic early on, picked up on routine bloods; non-specific symptoms (lethargy, anorexia/weight loss, insomnia, nausea/vomiting); in later-stage kidney failure — anaemia, pruritus (secondary to uraemia).

**Ix:** bloods (U&Es) (*why:* establishes the eGFR-based staging above and trends progression over time — CKD is defined by persistence (≥3 months), so serial measurement matters more than a single value; *what:* eGFR per the G-staging above). Urinalysis + ACR/PCR (*why:* establishes the albuminuria-based staging above and screens for an underlying glomerular cause; *what:* the A-category per the table above). Imaging ± biopsy (*why:* screens for a structural cause (e.g. polycystic kidneys, obstruction) and, if the cause remains unclear or a specific glomerular diagnosis would change Mx, biopsy provides histological confirmation; *what:* identifies structural abnormalities or a specific glomerular disease pattern).

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians experience a dramatically disproportionate burden of CKD**, particularly in remote communities: AIHW data show Indigenous Australians are roughly twice as likely as non-Indigenous Australians to have biomedical signs of CKD, and the treatment rate for end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is around **5 times higher** — rising to an estimated 18–20 times higher incidence in remote areas specifically compared with metropolitan areas. The causes are genuinely multifactorial (low birthweight, high rates of diabetes and hypertension, infectious disease burden, and structural/access factors), not attributable to any single cause.
> - **A genuine access-to-treatment disparity compounds the incidence disparity**: only around 12% of Indigenous Australians with treated ESKD have a functioning kidney transplant, compared with 45% of non-Indigenous treated ESKD patients — meaning Indigenous Australians with kidney failure are both more likely to develop it and less likely to receive the treatment modality with the best long-term outcomes.
> - **A genuinely important, easily overlooked practical consequence**: haemodialysis is not available in most remote communities, forcing patients to relocate — often permanently — to metropolitan or regional centres for treatment, disrupting kinship ties and connection to Country that are recognised as important to wellbeing. **South Australia's own Mobile Dialysis Truck** is a direct system-level response to this specific problem — a service that travels to remote communities for 1–2 week periods so patients can dialyse on Country, reconnecting with family and community during treatment, illustrating that addressing this disparity requires service-delivery innovation, not just clinical management.

**Mx:**
1. **Slow progression:** optimise DM control; optimise HTN control (ACEI or ARB — can cause a small ↓GFR, ≤25% reduction is acceptable; SGLT2 inhibitors have evidence of benefit in reducing progression, dapagliflozin only for now; furosemide especially when GFR ≤45 mL/min, also helps ↓K); treat glomerulonephritis
2. **Reduce risk of complications:** exercise, weight loss, stop smoking; dietary advice (control dietary sodium intake especially); atorvastatin 20mg for primary prevention of cardiovascular disease
3. **Treat complications:** mineral bone disease management; anaemia management
4. **End-stage renal disease:** renal replacement therapy; renal transplant. At ESRD, if a patient does not receive RRT, average life expectancy is ~6 months.

### 0.2.2 Mineral Bone Disease (in CKD)

**P:** CKD → decreased vitamin D activation → decreased Ca. CKD → decreased PO4 excretion → increased PO4 also causes bone degradation (osteomalacia). Leads to secondary hyperparathyroidism — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Secondary Hyperparathyroidism for the full mechanism (the kidney's dual role in vitamin D activation and phosphate excretion), not repeated here.

**Mx:**
- Normalise PO4: decrease dietary PO4; PO4 binders (e.g. sevelamer) — avoid calcium-based binders (risk of hypercalcaemia and vascular calcification)
- Normalise vitamin D: supplementation with alfacalcidol
- Normalise PTH: parathyroidectomy if severe

### 0.2.3 CKD-Related Anaemia

**P:** CKD → decreased erythropoietin → decreased RBC production → anaemia. CKD also causes decreased iron absorption. Results in a normochromic normocytic anaemia (normal Hb concentration per cell, just fewer RBCs). Can lead to left ventricular hypertrophy.

**Mx:** target Hb 10–12 g/dL. Iron supplementation to optimise iron levels first-line; if no improvement, offer EPO analogues (e.g. darbepoetin); patients may need blood transfusions.

### 0.2.4 Renal Replacement Therapy Modalities

**Haemodialysis:** patient connected to a machine that "cleans" the blood; usually required 3x/week, each session 3–5h. Access routes: arteriovenous fistula, graft, or central venous catheter.

> [!note] Gap-filled — haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis expanded beyond this brief mechanics-only description, given complications of both modalities are genuinely high-yield and frequently tested, following the same reasoning already applied to Renal transplant below.

- **Complications:**
  - **Intradialytic hypotension** — genuinely common (occurring in roughly 1 in 10 sessions), multifactorial (rapid fluid removal outpacing vascular refill, autonomic dysfunction, cardiac factors) — managed acutely by reducing/pausing the ultrafiltration rate and fluid administration, with prevention via careful assessment of the patient's genuine "dry weight" target.
  - **Dialysis disequilibrium syndrome (DDS):** a genuinely important complication to recognise — acute neurological deterioration (headache, nausea, confusion, and in severe cases seizures or coma from cerebral oedema) caused by rapid solute (particularly urea) removal from the blood outpacing removal from the brain, creating an osmotic gradient that drives water into brain tissue. **Highest risk in patients starting dialysis for the first time with significant pre-existing uraemia** — precisely the scenario where the temptation to dialyse aggressively and rapidly correct the biochemistry is greatest, which is exactly why it's dangerous; **Mx/prevention is to dialyse more gently in this specific scenario** (shorter sessions, lower blood/dialysate flow rates) rather than aggressively normalising urea in one session.
  - **Vascular access complications:** infection, thrombosis, and stenosis of the fistula/graft/catheter — access problems are a genuinely major, recurring source of hospital presentations in dialysis patients, not a rare technical footnote.
  - **Longer-term:** cardiovascular disease (the leading cause of death in dialysis patients, reflecting both pre-existing renal-disease-related cardiovascular risk and dialysis-specific haemodynamic stress), dialysis-related amyloidosis, and anaemia (see CKD-Related Anaemia above, not repeated here).

**Peritoneal dialysis:** filtration occurs in the patient's abdomen; requires surgical insertion of a PD catheter, through which the patient instills and drains PD fluid.
- **Continuous ambulatory PD:** each exchange lasts 30–40 min, dwell time 4–8h; patient can carry on normal activities while the dialysis solution is in their abdomen.
- **Automated PD:** patient connected to a machine that fills and drains the abdomen overnight.
- **Complications:**
  - **Peritonitis — the most significant and common complication**, most often caused by *Staphylococcus epidermidis* via the catheter — presents with abdominal pain, cloudy dialysate, and fever; a genuine emergency-adjacent presentation requiring prompt antibiotic treatment (typically intraperitoneal) and same-day assessment, given delayed treatment is associated with a substantial rise in mortality and can progress to more severe intra-abdominal complications if not addressed.
  - **Exit-site and tunnel infections:** distinct from frank peritonitis, though can progress to it if untreated — managed with topical/oral antibiotics initially, with catheter removal considered for refractory or recurrent infection.
  - **Catheter-related mechanical complications:** malfunction, migration, dialysate leak, and (less commonly) bowel perforation at insertion.
  - **Encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (EPS):** a rare but serious long-term complication — progressive fibrous encasement of the bowel loops from chronic exposure to PD solutions and repeated peritonitis episodes, risk increasing substantially with duration of PD (particularly beyond 5 years) — can progress to bowel obstruction and carries significant mortality, and is part of why PD duration and peritonitis frequency are actively monitored over a patient's time on this modality rather than PD being treated as an indefinitely sustainable option without review.
- **Contraindications:** significant peritoneal adhesions (from previous abdominal surgery), abdominal hernias, and a colostomy/stoma — all compromise the peritoneal cavity's suitability as the dialysis membrane.

**Renal transplant:** kidney from a live or deceased donor, usually placed in the right or left iliac fossa and attached to external iliac vessels (native kidneys remain in place). Requires lifelong immunosuppression to prevent rejection. Donor kidney lifespan ~10–15 years.

> [!note] Gap-filled — renal transplant expanded beyond this brief modality-comparison mention, given it's High-yield in its own right and the CSV specifically distinguishes it from the dialysis modalities above. Verified against ANZDATA (the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry, which collects outcome data from all renal units across both countries) and current transplant immunology literature, Aug 2026.

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue specific to transplant *access* — distinct from the incidence disparity already established for CKD above (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), not repeated here). **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians with treated end-stage kidney disease are dramatically less likely to receive a transplant than non-Indigenous Australians with the same condition** — AIHW data show only around 12% of Indigenous Australians with treated ESKD have a functioning transplant, compared with 45% of non-Indigenous treated ESKD patients. This means the disparity compounds at every stage: Indigenous Australians are both more likely to develop kidney failure **and** less likely to receive the treatment modality (transplant) with the best long-term outcomes once they do — this is a genuine access-to-care gap layered on top of the incidence gap, not explained by incidence alone. Contributing factors are recognised as multifactorial — including geographic distance from transplant centres, workup/listing barriers, and living-donor availability — rather than attributable to a single cause.

- **Immunosuppression principles:** typically a combination regimen rather than a single agent — commonly a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or ciclosporin), an antiproliferative agent (mycophenolate), and corticosteroids, sometimes with induction therapy (e.g. basiliximab or an anti-thymocyte preparation) around the time of transplant itself; **no single national standard protocol exists in Australia** — the specific regimen is determined by the treating transplant unit, so specifics should be checked against the individual centre's protocol rather than assumed fixed. **Drug levels require close monitoring given the narrow therapeutic window of several agents** (particularly calcineurin inhibitors) — target levels are typically higher in the early post-transplant period and reduced over time as rejection risk falls.

### 0.2.5 Rejection

- **Hyperacute rejection:** occurs immediately (within minutes to hours) due to pre-formed recipient antibodies against the donor organ — now rare given routine pre-transplant crossmatching and HLA typing are specifically designed to prevent this.
- **Acute rejection:** occurs days to weeks (risk highest in the first weeks to months) post-transplant — **T-cell-mediated** (the immune system recognises the graft as foreign and mounts a cellular attack) or **antibody-mediated** (donor-specific antibodies developing post-transplant) — the two mechanisms carry different treatment approaches, so biopsy characterisation matters, not just recognising "rejection" as a single entity.
- **Chronic rejection:** a gradual, progressive decline in graft function over months to years, reflecting cumulative immune-mediated injury (interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy, transplant glomerulopathy) — genuinely harder to treat than acute rejection, given the injury is often established and less reversible by the time it's detected.
- **Mx of rejection:** increased immunosuppression (higher dose of existing agents, or an additional/alternative agent) — inpatient or outpatient depending on severity; immunosuppression itself carries meaningful downstream risk (see Complications below), so the treating team must balance halting rejection against worsening those risks.

### 0.2.6 Complications of transplant and lifelong immunosuppression

> [!info] The genuinely important shift in thinking to hold in mind: immunosuppression trades the risk of rejection for a different, ongoing set of risks — infection and malignancy chief among them — rather than being a risk-free solution once the transplant itself has succeeded.

- **Infection:** immunosuppression increases susceptibility to opportunistic infection — CMV reactivation/infection is a particularly important and common complication specifically associated with transplant immunosuppression, warranting active surveillance in many protocols; standard community infections also run a higher-risk course.
- **Malignancy:** increased incidence of skin cancer and lymphoma (including post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease, PTLD) compared to the general population, reflecting reduced immune surveillance of malignant cells over years of immunosuppression — lifelong skin cancer surveillance and sun protection advice is a standard, important part of ongoing transplant follow-up given this risk.
- **Recurrence of the original renal disease:** the disease that caused the original kidney failure can recur in the transplanted kidney (particularly relevant for some glomerulonephritides) — worth actively considering when graft function unexpectedly declines, rather than assuming rejection or a new unrelated problem by default.
- **Metabolic/cardiovascular:** immunosuppressive agents (particularly corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors) contribute to post-transplant diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia — ongoing cardiovascular risk management remains important post-transplant, not something that resolves once the kidney itself is functioning.
- **Drug interactions and nephrotoxicity:** calcineurin inhibitors are themselves nephrotoxic at supratherapeutic levels, creating a genuine tension between under-immunosuppression (rejection risk) and over-immunosuppression (direct graft toxicity plus the infection/malignancy risks above) — this is why drug-level monitoring matters so much, not just as a dosing formality.

**P:** current 1-year graft survival rates exceed 90%, though outcomes depend on donor type (living donor grafts generally outperform deceased donor grafts) and ongoing adherence to immunosuppression and follow-up.

## 0.3 Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Polycystic kidney disease," High yield) — previously only mentioned in passing as a risk factor/cause within CKD, urinary stones, and hydrocele DDx sections; given a full dedicated entry here.

**D:** a genetic disorder characterised by the progressive development of multiple fluid-filled cysts in the kidneys, ultimately leading to CKD/ESRD in most affected individuals.

**A:** **Autosomal dominant PKD (ADPKD)** — by far the most common inherited kidney disease; mutations in *PKD1* (~85% of cases, typically earlier and more severe onset of ESRD) or *PKD2* (~15%, typically later onset, milder course). **Autosomal recessive PKD (ARPKD)** — much rarer, presents in infancy/childhood, often with a more severe course including significant hepatic fibrosis.

**P:** the *PKD1*/*PKD2* gene products (polycystin-1 and polycystin-2) are involved in normal renal tubular epithelial cell function and cilia signalling; mutation leads to abnormal cell proliferation and fluid secretion within tubules → progressive cyst formation and enlargement → progressive compression and destruction of normal renal parenchyma → declining renal function over decades.

**S/Smx:** often asymptomatic for years, detected incidentally or via family screening; flank/abdominal pain (from cyst enlargement, haemorrhage into a cyst, or stone/infection); haematuria (cyst rupture into the collecting system); hypertension (often an early and prominent feature, occurring even before significant renal impairment — driven by activation of the renin-angiotensin system from cystic distortion of renal architecture); palpable, enlarged, irregular kidneys on examination; recurrent UTIs/pyelonephritis (cysts can become infected and are difficult to treat given poor antibiotic penetration into cyst fluid); renal stones.

> [!info] Extrarenal manifestations — a genuinely important part of ADPKD, since it's a systemic disease of ciliary dysfunction, not purely a kidney disease
> **Hepatic cysts** — very common, usually don't impair liver function even when extensive (unlike the renal cysts, which do progressively impair kidney function). **Intracranial (berry) aneurysms** — a critical association, present in up to 10% of ADPKD patients (higher with a family history of aneurysm/SAH specifically) — see [[04_Neurology]] Cerebral Aneurysm and Subarachnoid Haemorrhage for the fuller detail on this, not repeated here; screening (MR angiography) is considered for patients with a family history of aneurysm/SAH or high-risk occupations, rather than routinely for all ADPKD patients. **Cardiac valve abnormalities** — mitral valve prolapse in particular. **Colonic diverticula.** **Abdominal wall/inguinal hernias.**

**Ix:** renal ultrasound is the primary diagnostic tool (*why:* non-invasive, widely available, and sufficient to make the diagnosis in most cases using age-specific cyst-number criteria, given the cyst burden required for diagnostic confidence increases with age as cysts accumulate over time; *what:* multiple bilateral renal cysts, with age-adjusted diagnostic criteria — e.g. fewer cysts are diagnostic at a younger age than at an older age, since some cyst formation occurs with normal ageing too). Genetic testing (*why:* used selectively — for atypical presentations, family planning/genetic counselling purposes, or when imaging is equivocal — rather than routinely, since imaging alone is usually sufficient for diagnosis in the classic case; *what:* identifies the specific *PKD1* or *PKD2* mutation, which also carries prognostic information given *PKD1*'s typically more severe course). Genetic counselling and family screening (*why:* ADPKD is autosomal dominant with high penetrance, so first-degree relatives have a 50% risk and often benefit from counselling about screening, family planning, and the implications of a diagnosis; *what:* informs at-risk relatives of their options). Regular monitoring — BP, renal function (eGFR), urinalysis (*why:* tracks disease progression and allows proactive management of the complications above (hypertension especially, given its role in accelerating progression); *what:* declining eGFR and worsening proteinuria track disease progression over time).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — this is a chronic, progressive genetic disease managed over decades):** **blood pressure control is the single most impactful modifiable intervention** — aggressive BP control (often targeting a lower-than-standard target given the disease-accelerating effect of hypertension specifically in PKD) slows progression to ESRD; **tolvaptan** (a vasopressin V2-receptor antagonist) is a disease-specific, PBS-listed option in Australia for patients with rapidly progressing disease, working by reducing the cyst-fluid-secretion-driving effect of vasopressin on collecting duct cells — a genuine disease-modifying therapy rather than purely symptomatic Mx, though requires monitoring for hepatotoxicity and aquaresis-related symptoms (polyuria, thirst).
- **Symptomatic/complication-specific:** analgesia for cyst-related pain; treat UTIs promptly (with attention to antibiotic cyst penetration — fluoroquinolones penetrate cyst fluid better than many other classes); manage stones per the Urinary Tract Stones section above; cyst decompression/drainage rarely needed but considered for large, symptomatic cysts.
- **End-stage disease:** renal replacement therapy or transplantation, as for any cause of ESRD (see Chronic Kidney Disease above) — PKD patients are generally good transplant candidates once ESRD is reached, given the disease doesn't recur in a transplanted kidney (unlike some other primary renal diseases).

**P:** highly variable — *PKD1* mutations typically progress to ESRD by the 50s-60s, *PKD2* mutations often later (60s-70s or beyond); disease-specific therapy (tolvaptan) and aggressive risk factor management can meaningfully slow progression, making early diagnosis and proactive management genuinely valuable rather than purely prognostic information.


## 0.4 Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome

| | Nephritic syndrome | Nephrotic syndrome | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A/P** | Glomerular inflammation → GBM damage → loss of RBCs into urine → dysmorphic RBCs, haematuria | Podocyte damage → impaired charge barrier → proteinuria | Severe GBM damage → loss of RBCs and proteins into urine |
| **Presentation** | Haematuria; RBC casts in urine; ↓GFR → oliguria, azotaemia, **hypertension** (from sodium/water retention and volume expansion — see note below); proteinuria (<3.5 g/day usually, may be higher if severe) | Proteinuria (>3.5 g/day); oedema, hypoalbuminaemia; ↑hepatic lipogenesis; hypercholesterolaemia; frothy urine with fatty casts; associated with hypercoagulable state | Proteinuria (>3.5 g/day); haematuria; mixed features |
| **Examples** | GN associated with infections, Goodpasture syndrome, IgA nephropathy, Alport syndrome, membranoproliferative GN | Focal segmental GN, minimal change disease (the commonest cause in children — see [[15_10_Paeds_-_UTI__Nephrotic_Syndrome__Glomerulonephritis]] Nephrotic syndrome), membranous nephropathy, amyloidosis, diabetic GN — primary or secondary | Most commonly associated with diffuse proliferative GN, membranoproliferative GN |

> [!note] Gap-filled — hypertension was genuinely absent from the nephritic presentation above, despite being one of the classic clinical features distinguishing nephritic from nephrotic syndrome (nephrotic syndrome is classically normotensive, or even relatively hypotensive from the low oncotic pressure described above, in contrast). **Mechanism — genuinely a different pathway from PCKD's RAAS-driven hypertension established elsewhere in this file, not repeated here**: nephritic syndrome's hypertension is a **volume-overload** phenomenon — glomerular inflammation and GBM damage reduce GFR, impairing the kidney's ability to excrete sodium and water; the resulting sodium/water retention expands intravascular volume, directly raising blood pressure. This is precisely why nephritic syndrome's hypertension often improves as the underlying glomerular inflammation resolves and GFR recovers, rather than requiring the same chronic antihypertensive management that RAAS-driven hypertension (e.g. in PCKD or CKD more broadly) typically does — a genuinely useful distinction when thinking through management and expected disease course, not just an isolated additional symptom to memorise.

> [!note] Gap-filled — the core nephrotic triad (proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema) and the associated hypercholesterolaemia were listed together in the table above without the causal chain connecting them. **The core chain:** podocyte damage (per the A/P above) impairs the glomerular filtration barrier's charge/size selectivity, causing heavy **proteinuria** — losing albumin faster than the liver can replace it causes **hypoalbuminaemia** — since albumin is the principal driver of plasma oncotic pressure, its loss reduces the oncotic force that normally holds fluid within the vasculature, so fluid shifts into the interstitial space, producing **oedema** (classically periorbital and dependent, e.g. ankle swelling, and can become generalised/anasarca in severe disease). **Hypercholesterolaemia** stems from a related but distinct mechanism: the liver's response to low plasma oncotic pressure (and possibly loss of a lipid-regulating factor in the urine alongside albumin) is a **generalised, non-specific increase in hepatic protein and lipoprotein synthesis** — the liver "upregulates production" broadly in response to the low oncotic state, and this includes increased synthesis of cholesterol-carrying lipoproteins alongside the (inadequate, given ongoing urinary loss) attempt to replace albumin — the same generalised hepatic response also drives the increased clotting factor synthesis contributing to the hypercoagulable state described below.

> [!note] Gap-filled — the hypercoagulable state in nephrotic syndrome was stated in the table above without explanation. **Why nephrotic syndrome causes hypercoagulability:** the same impaired glomerular charge barrier that allows albumin to leak into the urine also allows loss of smaller anticoagulant proteins, particularly **antithrombin III**, into the urine — its loss removes a key natural brake on the coagulation cascade. This is compounded by increased hepatic synthesis of clotting factors (part of the same generalised hepatic response driving the hypercholesterolaemia and hypoalbuminaemia already listed above) and increased platelet aggregability. The clinically important consequence is a genuinely elevated risk of **renal vein thrombosis specifically**, alongside the more general VTE risk — worth actively considering in a nephrotic patient with new flank pain, haematuria, or a sudden deterioration in renal function, not just a theoretical risk to mention in passing.

## 0.5 Diabetic Nephropathy

**D:** albuminuria (increased urinary albumin excretion ≥30mg/g) + progressive ↓eGFR, against a background of DM (T1 or T2), usually with retinopathy.

**A/P:** metabolic changes (↑glucose, ↑lipids) and haemodynamic changes (HTN) both promote increased inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, fibrosis, and glomerular hyperfiltration. Glomerular mesangium expansion due to increased glucose and glomerular pressure. Glomerular sclerosis and fibrosis follow.

**R:** sustained ↑BGC, HTN, family history of HTN and CKD, obesity, smoking

**Screening:** all patients screened annually using urinary albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR) on an early morning specimen (*why:* annual screening allows early detection before symptoms develop, since — as noted below — clinical signs only appear once disease is advanced, and early detection is precisely what allows the disease-modifying Mx below to be started while still beneficial; *what:* ACR >2.5 = microalbuminuria, the earliest detectable marker of diabetic nephropathy).

**S/Smx:** oedema and retinopathy only become apparent when kidney disease becomes severe.

> [!info] Verified against KDIGO 2024 CKD Guideline (the international reference standard Australia follows closely for CKD/diabetic nephropathy — there isn't a materially different Australian-specific alternative) and the RACGP diabetes BP target already established in [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Drugs for T2DM, Aug 2026 — the BP target below needs correction, but the ACEI/ARB threshold is broadly consistent with current international guidance.
> **Mx:** dietary protein restriction; tight glycaemic control; **BP target <130/80 mmHg specifically for diabetes with CKD/albuminuria** (not the flatter <140/90 figure below, which is the more general/less specific target — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] for the fuller AU-verified detail on this distinction, not repeated here); ACEI or ARB — KDIGO 2024 recommends starting for moderately-to-severely increased albuminuria (ACR category A2/A3) in diabetes, broadly consistent with the "start when ACR ≥3" threshold below (noting units matter here — this is mg/mmol, the unit convention used in Australia and the UK, not the mg/g convention sometimes seen in US sources) — dosed to the highest tolerated dose, not just "started" at a low dose and left there; control dyslipidaemia (statins, per the Australian CVD risk calculator — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Hypercholesterolaemia for the fuller AU-verified detail, not repeated here). SGLT2 inhibitors are now also a KDIGO 2024-recommended addition for T2DM + CKD with eGFR ≥20 — a genuinely useful addition not in the original UK-style note, given how much CKD/diabetes management has evolved to include this drug class (see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] T2DM Stepwise Drug Management for the fuller detail on when SGLT2i are now used).

**P:** diabetic kidney disease is associated with worsening proteinuria, earlier complications, and worse performance on dialysis, plus other DM-related complications.

## 0.6 Lupus Nephritis

**D:** severe manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

> [!info] WHO classification
> Class I: normal kidney | Class II: mesangial GN | Class III: focal/segmental proliferative GN | Class IV: diffuse proliferative GN (most common, most severe) | Class V: diffuse membranous GN | Class VI: sclerosing GN

**Class IV findings:** microscopy — glomeruli show endothelial and mesangial proliferation, 'wire-loop' appearance, capillary wall thickening secondary to immune complex deposition; electron microscopy — subendothelial immune complex deposits; immunofluorescence — granular pattern.

**A/P:** type III hypersensitivity — immune complex formation secondary to IgG antibodies against dsDNA and nuclear proteins, deposited in the kidneys causing inflammation.

**S/Smx:** fever, oedema, HTN, arthralgia, myalgia, malar rash, frothy urine.

**Ix:** bloods — FBC, U&Es, ESR, ANA, anti-Smith, anti-dsDNA, complement levels (*why:* supports the SLE diagnosis and specifically screens for active disease — falling complement (C3/C4) and rising anti-dsDNA titres are classic markers of a lupus flare, useful both diagnostically and for monitoring disease activity/treatment response over time; *what:* positive ANA/anti-dsDNA/anti-Smith support SLE; low complement supports active immune-complex-mediated disease). Biopsy + histology (*why:* the definitive test — biopsy is essential not just to confirm lupus nephritis but to determine the WHO class above, since Mx intensity and drug choice differ substantially by class (see the Mx box below); *what:* the specific class-defining features described above, e.g. the wire-loop appearance and immune complex deposition pattern of Class IV).

> [!info] Verified against the KDIGO 2024 Lupus Nephritis Guideline, Aug 2026 — lupus nephritis management is genuinely driven by international consensus (KDIGO, EULAR, ACR all broadly converge) rather than a UK-specific protocol, so this isn't really a UK-vs-Australia issue — but the guideline has been significantly updated since the original note was written, with a new drug (belimumab) now incorporated as a first-line option.
> **Mx:** hydroxychloroquine recommended for all patients with LN unless contraindicated (a foundational addition not in the original note). Control HTN (RAS blockade — see Diabetic Nephropathy above for the general ACEI/ARB principles, applicable here too). For active Class III/IV LN, glucocorticoids **plus any one of:** (i) mycophenolic acid analogues (**MPAA (mycophenolic acid analogues — mycophenolate mofetil or mycophenolate sodium)** — e.g. mycophenolate) — largely consistent with the original note's "mycophenolate first-line"; (ii) low-dose IV cyclophosphamide — consistent with the original note; **(iii) belimumab plus either MPAA or low-dose cyclophosphamide (triple therapy) — a genuinely new KDIGO 2024 addition, particularly preferred for patients with repeated kidney flares or high relapse risk**; or (iv) MPAA plus a calcineurin inhibitor (voclosporin, tacrolimus, or ciclosporin) when kidney function isn't severely impaired (eGFR >45), particularly preferred for patients with heavy proteinuria from podocyte injury. Following initial therapy, MPAA is preferred for maintenance and should continue for ≥36 months — genuinely longer than older teaching may have suggested, and mycophenolate remains preferred over azathioprine for maintenance (lower risk of progression to ESRD), consistent with the original note. Current guidance also favours lower cumulative glucocorticoid dosing than historical practice, using IV methylprednisolone pulses at treatment start to allow faster oral steroid tapering.

## 0.7 ANCA-Associated Glomerulonephritis

**GN associated with:** granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's), eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss), microscopic polyangiitis, renal-limited vasculitis. Involves immune complex deposition.

**A/P sequence:** (1) neutrophil priming, (2) neutrophil activation, (3) acute inflammation and necrosis, (4) chronic inflammation and scarring.

**S/Smx of renal disease:** haematuria and proteinuria.

**Ix:** AKI (↑SrCr) (*why:* reflects the degree of renal involvement/damage from the vasculitic process; *what:* rising creatinine, potentially rapidly progressive given the necrotising nature of ANCA-associated GN). ANCA testing (*why:* the key serological test supporting the diagnosis and distinguishing the specific subtype — c-ANCA/PR3 is more associated with granulomatosis with polyangiitis, while p-ANCA/MPO is more associated with microscopic polyangiitis and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, though overlap exists; *what:* positive ANCA with the specific pattern supporting the likely clinical subtype). Biopsy + histology (*why:* confirms the diagnosis and the specific pattern of glomerular injury (typically a pauci-immune, necrotising and crescentic pattern, distinguishing it from the immune-complex-heavy pattern of lupus nephritis above despite this section's heading); *what:* necrotising/crescentic glomerulonephritis with minimal immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence — the "pauci-immune" pattern that's actually the hallmark distinguishing feature).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** steroids (often high-dose initially, particularly for rapidly progressive disease) — the priority is halting active inflammation quickly given the risk of rapid, irreversible renal damage.
- **Definitive:** immunosuppressant (e.g. methotrexate, or more typically rituximab or cyclophosphamide for induction in current international practice for severe disease) ± IVIG or plasma exchange for severe/rapidly progressive cases.
- **Chronic/long-term:** supportive care and ongoing immunosuppression for maintenance, given ANCA-associated vasculitis is a relapsing-remitting condition requiring long-term monitoring rather than a one-off treatment course.

## 0.8 Myeloma Kidney

50% of multiple myeloma patients will have kidney disease, associated with poorer prognosis. Monoclonal production of immunoglobulins → light chain deposition within the renal tubules → renal damage, presenting as dehydration and increasing thirst.

**Other causes of renal impairment in myeloma:** amyloidosis, nephrocalcinosis, nephrolithiasis.

**Ix:** Bence Jones proteins in urine (*why:* the classic diagnostic finding, reflecting free light chains filtered by the glomerulus and excreted — directly indicating the underlying pathophysiological process of light chain deposition described above; *what:* positive Bence Jones proteinuria supports myeloma kidney as the cause of renal impairment, though serum free light chain assays are now often used alongside/instead of urine testing in contemporary practice).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** aggressive IV fluid hydration is a key early intervention, given light chain nephrotoxicity is exacerbated by dehydration/concentrated tubular fluid — this is a genuinely time-sensitive intervention to limit further renal damage while definitive oncological Mx is arranged.
- **Definitive:** treat underlying cancer (myeloma-specific chemotherapy/targeted therapy, outside the scope of this renal-focused entry).
- **Chronic/long-term:** renal replacement therapy if renal failure is established/irreversible despite treatment of the underlying myeloma.

## 0.9 Acute Interstitial Nephritis

**D:** a pattern of AKI localised to the kidney tubulo-interstitial space, usually triggered by medications.

**A:** drugs — especially antibiotics (penicillin, rifampicin), NSAIDs, allopurinol, furosemide. Systemic disease — SLE, sarcoidosis, Sjögren's syndrome. Infections — Hantavirus, staphylococci.

**P:** histology shows marked interstitial oedema and interstitial infiltrate in the connective tissue between renal tubules.

**S/Smx:** fever, rash, arthralgia, HTN; FBC shows eosinophilia; mild renal impairment.

**Ix:** monitor SrCr regularly (*why:* trends the degree of renal impairment and response to Mx (particularly withdrawal of the offending drug, the cornerstone of treatment below); *what:* typically mild-moderate elevation, improving once the trigger is removed). Identify underlying cause (*why:* the specific trigger — drug, infection, systemic disease — directly determines Mx, since drug-induced AIN is managed primarily by stopping the drug rather than targeted pharmacotherapy; *what:* a temporal relationship to a new medication is the most common finding, supported by the eosinophilia and rash described above).

**Mx:** accurate fluid balance monitoring (daily weights, UO chart); monitor U&Es daily; review medications and stop the likely offending drug — the single most important intervention, since this is fundamentally a drug reaction in most cases; investigate and treat underlying cause if not drug-related (systemic disease, infection); supportive therapy (O2, fluids); urology referral if required (e.g. if an obstructive component is suspected).

## 0.10 Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN)

**D:** necrosis of the renal tubular epithelial cells resulting in AKI — usually reversible if identified and treated early.

**Causes divided into ischaemic and nephrotoxic:**
- Ischaemic: shock, sepsis
- Toxins: aminoglycosides, myoglobin (secondary to rhabdomyolysis), contrast dyes, lead

**S/Smx:** oliguria/anuria, hypotension, ↑HR; other non-specific symptoms (thirst, malaise, anorexia).

**Ix:** U&Es (*why:* confirms the AKI and its severity, trended to assess the phase of illness (see the three-phase note below); *what:* deranged/rising creatinine, consistent with intrinsic AKI). Urine dip/microscopy — muddy brown casts (*why:* the classic and relatively specific finding for ATN, reflecting sloughed tubular epithelial cells, distinguishing this intrinsic cause from pre-renal or post-renal AKI and from other intrinsic causes like glomerulonephritis (which would show dysmorphic RBCs/RBC casts instead — see Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome above); *what:* granular "muddy brown" casts on microscopy).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** treat underlying cause (address sepsis/shock if ischaemic, stop/avoid the nephrotoxin if toxin-mediated); IV fluid boluses to optimise renal perfusion, with the same caution around volume overload risk noted in the general AKI Mx (STOP AKI mnemonic) above.
- **Chronic/long-term:** supportive care through the recovery phase below, with close monitoring during the polyuric phase specifically, since large urine volumes during recovery can themselves cause dehydration/electrolyte derangement if not matched with adequate fluid replacement.

> [!note] Three phases: oliguric phase, polyuric phase, recovery phase. Injury to epithelial cells causes detachment of the basement membrane and tubular dysfunction.

## 0.11 Urinary Tract Infections

**D:** infection of the kidneys, bladder, or urethra. Cystitis = infection of the bladder.
- **Complicated UTI:** in patients with functional or structural impairments reducing antibiotic efficacy, or involving the kidneys, or occurring during pregnancy
- **Recurrent UTI:** ≥2 separate culture-proven episodes of acute UTI with symptoms within 6 months, or >3 UTIs in 12 months

### 0.11.1 Infectious Cystitis

**R:** F>M, history of UTI, urinary catheter, anatomical/functional abnormality of the urinary tract, frequent sexual intercourse, DM, spinal cord injuries, pregnancy, immunosuppression

**P:** bacteria usually ascend the urinary tract ± bladder urine stasis impairs defence.

**S/Smx:**
- <65yo: dysuria, new nocturia, cloudy-looking urine
- ≥65yo: new-onset dysuria with ≥2 of: fever within the last 12h, new frequency/urgency, new incontinence, new or worsening delirium, new suprapubic pain, new haematuria

> [!danger] Think SEPSIS if fever, loin/back pain, nausea/vomiting, or renal angle tenderness on exam.

**Ix:** urine dipstick positive for leukocytes and nitrites.

> [!warning] Do NOT routinely urine dip patients ≥65yo or with catheters (almost always positive anyway) — dip & MC&S for elderly to guide treatment via sensitivities. Urine MC&S for complicated/recurrent UTI or haematuria. Imaging (US or CT) to look for structural abnormalities if indicated.

> [!info] Verified against Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic (March 2025 update) and Australian Pharmacist reporting on this overhaul, Aug 2026 — **this is a genuinely major and current change, not a minor dose adjustment.** As of March 2025, trimethoprim is **no longer first-line** for acute cystitis in non-pregnant adults, due to high and rising *E. coli* resistance (~27% in one Melbourne study) — this is a significant, recent, and highly testable shift from older teaching (including the UK-sourced content this entry replaced, which is no longer carried here), so don't rely on trimethoprim-first-line as current.
> **Mx — non-complicated cystitis, current AU first line (March 2025 eTG):**
> 1. **First line: nitrofurantoin 100mg PO 6-hourly for 5 days** (note the AU duration is 5 days, not the 3 days of the UK-sourced figure this entry replaced)
> 2. **Second line: fosfomycin 3g PO as a single dose**
> 3. **Third line: trimethoprim 300mg PO daily for 3 days** — now explicitly a third-line, not first-line, option
> Methenamine hippurate is **not** effective for acute cystitis treatment (only used prophylactically) — worth knowing given it's sometimes confused for an active-treatment option. Symptomatic Mx — ibuprofen 400mg PO 8-hourly for up to 3 days is an explicitly endorsed analgesic option in current AU guidance (alongside/instead of paracetamol); patient education — drink plenty of water, pee after sex.
>
> **In pregnant women:** nitrofurantoin (except near term) for 7 days, or amoxicillin/cefalexin. Trimethoprim contraindicated (teratogenic, particularly first trimester — folate antagonism). Requires test of cure. This pregnancy-specific guidance is not superseded by the March 2025 general-population change above — pregnancy UTI Mx follows its own separate protocol given the trimethoprim contraindication already existed regardless of resistance patterns.
>
> **In males:** the March 2025 hierarchy above (nitrofurantoin → fosfomycin → trimethoprim) applies here too, for a longer duration consistent with the complicated-UTI-equivalent status of UTI in males; do not routinely refer to urology for a first, uncomplicated episode.
>
> **≥65yo and catheterised patients:** urine dip does not guide treatment; treat only if symptomatic; remove/replace catheter; antibiotics per the hierarchy above, for a duration guided by clinical response and any culture results.

### 0.11.2 Pyelonephritis

**D:** severe infectious inflammatory disease of the renal parenchyma, calyces, and pelvis.

**R:** UTI, DM, stress incontinence, foreign body, anatomical abnormality, frequent sex, immunosuppression, pregnancy

**A/P:** causative agents similar to UTIs. Acute uncomplicated pyelonephritis usually secondary to an ascending UTI. Haematogenous seeding of the kidneys can occur in bacteraemic patients.

**S/Smx:** flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, flu-like symptoms, fever, nausea/vomiting; rapid symptom development (within 1 day).

**Ix:** urine dipstick, urine C&S (≥100,000 CFU/mL), FBC, ESR/CRP, U&Es, blood culture (15–30% bacteraemic).

> [!info] Verified against Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic (March 2025 update) and a 2025 Emergency Medicine Australasia study of actual ED prescribing, Aug 2026 — **cefalexin alone is not the current first-line recommendation.** Current eTG guidance recommends **amoxicillin+clavulanate or ciprofloxacin** for empirical pyelonephritis, specifically because *E. coli* resistance to ampicillin/amoxicillin (~55%) and trimethoprim (~37.5%) is too high for monotherapy with either to be reliable — the same Australian study found ampicillin, amoxicillin, or trimethoprim monotherapy was "most likely to be inappropriate" for confirmed *E. coli* pyelonephritis, the most common causative organism. The March 2025 update also introduced a **higher amoxicillin-clavulanate dose specifically for pyelonephritis — 875+125mg PO every 8 hours** — to ensure adequate antimicrobial exposure against Enterobacterales.
> **Mx — uncomplicated:** amoxicillin+clavulanate 875+125mg PO 8-hourly, or ciprofloxacin, for 7–10 days, adjusted based on MC&S once available. Analgesia as needed. Patient education (drink plenty of water). **Complicated (or severe/septic):** admission, IV antibiotics (broader-spectrum, per local/eTG severe-sepsis guidance — not detailed further in this source) ± increased support — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Shock (Septic shock) for the general sepsis Mx pathway this feeds into, not repeated here.

### 0.11.3 Chronic Pyelonephritis
Definitive diagnosis based on histology, but biopsies are rare. Presumptive clinical diagnosis: vesicoureteral reflux or prior surgery for obstruction/recurrent UTIs + imaging studies. Treat underlying cause; may require nephrectomy ± antibiotics.

### 0.11.4 Prostatitis

**D:** inflammation of the prostate, usually associated with recent/ongoing bacterial infection.

**R:** UTI, BPH, catheter, procedures

**A/P:** usually ascending infection, similar causative organisms to cystitis (*E. coli*).

**Ix:** urine dipstick (*why:* a rapid screen supporting a UTI-associated infective process; *what:* positive for leukocytes and nitrites). Urine C&S (*why:* identifies the causative organism and guides targeted antibiotic therapy, particularly important given the prolonged treatment course and the consequences of undertreated prostatitis (chronic prostatitis, abscess); *what:* typically grows *E. coli* or another uropathogen). Blood culture if febrile (*why:* prostatitis can be associated with bacteraemia/sepsis, so febrile patients need this screened for and, if positive, informs both severity assessment and antibiotic duration/route; *what:* may be positive, particularly in more severe presentations).

> [!info] Verified — the quinolone-class choice (ciprofloxacin) for acute bacterial prostatitis reflects broad international consensus, given quinolones' favourable prostatic tissue penetration compared to most other oral antibiotic classes — this is a pharmacokinetic property, not a UK-specific choice, though note rising quinolone resistance is prompting exploration of oral beta-lactam alternatives (e.g. cefuroxime, high-dose amoxicillin+clavulanate) in some recent international literature, particularly for *E. coli* prostatitis. No single AU-specific eTG source with a different drug class was found this session — treat ciprofloxacin as a reasonable default but check current eTG for the specific first-line recommendation and duration before relying on it for a specific patient.
> **Mx:** PO quinolone (e.g. ciprofloxacin) x14 days, NSAIDs, relief of obstruction ± sepsis 6 pathway.

### 0.11.5 Epididymitis

**D:** inflammation of the epididymis; acute if ≤6 weeks. Termed epididymo-orchitis if concurrent testicular inflammation.

**R:** unprotected sex, bladder outflow obstruction, catheter

**A/P:** depends on risk factors — MSM usually STI-related; older males usually UTI-related. Retrograde infection from urethra/bladder likely.

**S/Smx:** unilateral scrotal pain; gradual-onset swelling; tenderness, hot, erythematous, swollen hemiscrotum.

**Ix:** urethral swab (*why:* identifies urethritis and any associated organisms, relevant given the frequent STI aetiology; *what:* may show discharge/inflammation). Urine dipstick, urine microscopy, C&S (*why:* screens for a UTI-associated cause, particularly relevant in older men without sexual risk factors where the aetiology is more likely urinary than sexually transmitted (see A/P above); *what:* positive leukocytes/nitrites, culture may identify a uropathogen). NAAT for chlamydia and gonorrhoea (*why:* the definitive test confirming an STI cause, essential given how commonly epididymo-orchitis in sexually active men is chlamydia/gonorrhoea-related, and directly determines which Mx pathway below applies; *what:* positive NAAT confirms the STI cause and the specific organism(s) involved).

> [!info] Verified against the Australian STI Management Guidelines for Use in Primary Care (sti.guidelines.org.au), Aug 2026 — the Australian regimen for STI-suspected epididymo-orchitis differs in a genuinely important way from the UK-sourced note: doxycycline duration is longer (14 days, not 10–14), and the alternative to doxycycline is azithromycin rather than a different fluoroquinolone choice.
> **Mx:** treat sexually active men with epididymo-orchitis presumptively for gonorrhoea and chlamydia — **ceftriaxone 500mg in 2mL of 1% lignocaine IM, single dose, PLUS EITHER doxycycline 100mg PO BD for 14 days OR azithromycin 1g PO stat, repeated 1 week later.** For men who engage in insertive anal sex, treat empirically as above initially, but consider enteric organism coverage if response is poor (seek specialist advice, rather than defaulting to empirical fluoroquinolone monotherapy as the UK-sourced note suggests) — in severe cases, treatment may need to continue for up to 3 weeks. Bed rest, scrotal support, and analgesia are commonly required; complete resolution of swelling may take several weeks, though a substantial response should occur within 4–5 days. If UTI/non-STI cause is more likely (e.g. older male, no sexual risk factors, urinary symptoms predominate) — treat per the UTI/pyelonephritis Mx above rather than the STI regimen. Advise no sexual contact for 7 days after treatment starts or until symptoms resolve, whichever is later; contact tracing for STI-related cases.

### 0.11.6 Balanoposthitis

**D:** inflammation of the glans penis and prepuce (balanitis = inflammation of the glans penis alone).

**R:** congenital or acquired dysfunctional foreskin, uncircumcised state, poor hygiene, over-washing, HPV

**S/Smx by cause:**
| Cause | Features |
|---|---|
| Candidiasis | After sex; itching; white, non-urethral discharge |
| Contact/allergic dermatitis | Itchy ± painful ± clear non-urethral discharge |
| Eczema or psoriasis | Very itchy; history of inflammatory skin conditions elsewhere |
| Bacterial (staph) | Painful ± itch; yellow non-urethral discharge |
| Anaerobic | ± itchy; very offensive yellow non-urethral discharge |
| Lichen planus | ± itchy; Wickham's striae, violaceous papules |
| Circinate balanitis | Not itchy, no discharge; painless erosions; associated with reactive arthritis |
| Lichen sclerosus | ± itchy; white plaques, scarring |
| Zoon balanitis (plasma cell balanitis) | Not itchy; clearly circumscribed inflamed areas |

**Ix:** swab for MC&S, virology.

**Mx:** gentle saline washes, wash under the foreskin, 1% hydrocortisone for itch. Candidiasis — topical clotrimazole for 2 weeks. Bacterial — PO flucloxacillin/clarithromycin. Anaerobic — topical/PO metronidazole. Dermatitis, circinate balanitis — low-potency topical steroid (hydrocortisone). Lichen sclerosus, Zoon balanitis — high-potency topical steroid (clobetasol) ± circumcision.

## 0.12 Urinary Retention

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Urinary retention," Medium yield) — previously only mentioned in passing (as a cause of raised PSA). This entry covers acute and chronic retention as distinct entities, cross-referencing BPH above for the most common underlying cause rather than repeating it.

**D:** **Acute urinary retention (AUR)** — sudden inability to voluntarily pass urine, associated with pain and a palpable/percussible bladder — a urological emergency. **Chronic urinary retention (CUR)** — a gradual, often painless increase in post-void residual volume, which may present incidentally or with overflow incontinence rather than an inability to void at all.

**A:**
- **Obstructive:** BPH (the most common cause in men — see Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia above, not repeated here), urethral stricture, prostate cancer, constipation/faecal impaction (a commonly overlooked mechanical cause, especially in the elderly), phimosis/paraphimosis, blood clots (clot retention, e.g. post-TURP or with bladder malignancy)
- **Neurogenic:** cauda equina syndrome (see [[04_Neurology]] Cauda Equina Syndrome — urinary retention is a core red-flag feature of this diagnosis, not repeated here), spinal cord injury/compression, multiple sclerosis, diabetic autonomic neuropathy
- **Pharmacological:** anticholinergics, opioids, tricyclic antidepressants, sympathomimetics, alcohol
- **Other precipitants of acute-on-chronic retention:** post-operative (especially after spinal/epidural anaesthesia, pelvic surgery), UTI, constipation, immobility

**S/Smx:**
- **AUR:** severe suprapubic pain, inability to void, palpable/percussible distended bladder, often a clear precipitant (post-operative, new medication, constipation, alcohol).
- **CUR:** often painless despite large residual volumes (a key distinguishing feature from AUR); overflow incontinence (involuntary leakage as the overdistended bladder passively overflows); may present with recurrent UTIs, renal impairment from chronic back-pressure (high-pressure chronic retention specifically), or incidentally on imaging.

> [!danger] Always examine for and actively exclude cauda equina syndrome in a patient with urinary retention, particularly if accompanied by back pain, bilateral leg symptoms, or saddle anaesthesia — see [[04_Neurology]] Cauda Equina Syndrome for the full red-flag assessment, not repeated here. This is a genuine "don't miss" differential given how time-critical cauda equina syndrome is.

**Ix:** bladder scan (*why:* confirms retention and quantifies the volume, both diagnostically and to guide catheter size/urgency; *what:* elevated post-void residual, often several hundred mL to >1L in significant retention). U&Es (*why:* screens for renal impairment from chronic back-pressure, particularly relevant in high-pressure chronic retention where ongoing obstruction causes progressive kidney damage; *what:* may show AKI or established CKD in longstanding high-pressure retention). Urinalysis/MSU (*why:* screens for a precipitating or resulting UTI, common both as a cause and consequence of retention; *what:* may be positive if infection is present or has resulted from stasis). PSA — **deferred until after catheterisation and typically for some weeks**, since acute retention itself falsely elevates PSA (see the Prostate Cancer section above for the fuller list of PSA-elevating confounders, not repeated here). Consider spinal imaging/neurological assessment if a neurogenic cause is suspected (see the cauda equina red-flag box above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (AUR):** urethral catheterisation — genuinely urgent given the pain and risk of ongoing renal damage; note the volume drained and monitor for **post-obstructive diuresis** (a large, brisk urine output following decompression of a chronically distended bladder, which can itself cause dehydration/electrolyte derangement if not matched with adequate fluid replacement — monitor fluid balance closely after catheterisation for significant retention). Trial without catheter (TWOC) once the precipitant has resolved (e.g. after stopping an offending medication, treating constipation) — if TWOC fails, this suggests an underlying obstructive process (most commonly BPH) requiring definitive urological Mx.
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause per the specific disease sections referenced above (BPH, urethral stricture, cauda equina syndrome, etc.) — urinary retention itself is a sign/complication, not a standalone disease, so definitive Mx always depends on identifying and addressing the cause.
- **Chronic/long-term (CUR):** intermittent self-catheterisation or long-term indwelling catheter for patients with an irreversible cause (e.g. established neurogenic bladder); regular monitoring of renal function given the ongoing back-pressure risk in unmanaged high-pressure chronic retention.

## 0.13 Incontinence

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Incontinence," High yield; "Stress Incontinence," High yield; "Urge Incontinence," Medium yield) — genuinely absent despite being a very commonly examined topic, distinct from the retention/overflow-related incontinence mentioned briefly above.

**D:** involuntary leakage of urine — several distinct subtypes with different mechanisms and Mx.

**Types:**
- **Stress incontinence** — leakage with increased intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, sneezing, laughing, exercise) — due to weakness of the pelvic floor/urethral sphincter mechanism, most commonly following childbirth (vaginal delivery, particularly instrumental) or as a consequence of pelvic surgery (e.g. prostatectomy in men) or menopause-related tissue changes.
- **Urge incontinence** (overactive bladder) — leakage preceded by a sudden, compelling urge to void, often with an inability to reach the toilet in time — due to detrusor overactivity (involuntary detrusor muscle contractions during the filling phase); may be idiopathic or secondary to a neurological cause (e.g. MS, stroke, Parkinson's disease — see [[04_Neurology]] for the relevant disease-level content, not repeated here), bladder irritation (UTI, stones, malignancy), or bladder outlet obstruction.
- **Mixed incontinence** — features of both stress and urge incontinence together — genuinely common, particularly in older women.
- **Overflow incontinence** — leakage from an overdistended bladder in chronic urinary retention (see Urinary Retention above) — the bladder is essentially "full to overflowing" rather than actively contracting, an important mechanistic distinction from urge incontinence despite superficially similar presentations.
- **Functional incontinence** — normal bladder function, but the patient is unable to reach the toilet in time due to external factors (mobility impairment, cognitive impairment, environmental barriers) — a genuinely important category to consider, especially in frail/elderly patients, since the fix is addressing the functional barrier rather than treating the bladder itself.

**R:** female sex, increasing age, obesity, pregnancy/vaginal delivery (stress), menopause, chronic cough/constipation (raised intra-abdominal pressure, stress), neurological disease (urge), pelvic organ prolapse, previous pelvic surgery.

**S/Smx:** characterise by the pattern above — timing/triggers of leakage, volume, associated urgency, and any relevant obstetric/surgical/neurological history are the key discriminating history points between the subtypes.

**Ix:** urinalysis (*why:* excludes UTI as a reversible contributing/precipitating cause, and screens for haematuria which would warrant further investigation for malignancy; *what:* may be positive if infection present). Bladder diary (*why:* objectively characterises the voiding pattern and volumes over several days, often more revealing than a single history-taking encounter and useful for both diagnosis and monitoring treatment response; *what:* documents fluid intake, voiding frequency/volume, and leakage episodes with their triggers). Post-void residual (bladder scan) (*why:* screens for an overflow component (chronic retention) that could be masquerading as or contributing to other incontinence types; *what:* elevated residual suggests overflow incontinence rather than pure stress/urge). Urodynamic studies (*why:* reserved for cases where the diagnosis is unclear, symptoms are mixed, or prior to surgical Mx being considered — directly measures bladder pressure/function during filling and voiding to confirm the underlying mechanism; *what:* confirms detrusor overactivity (urge), demonstrates leakage with raised abdominal pressure (stress), or reveals another specific pattern).

**Mx:**
- **Stress incontinence:** pelvic floor muscle training (first-line, genuinely effective for many patients and should be tried before considering surgery) — supervised programs are more effective than unsupervised; lifestyle modification (weight loss, treat chronic cough/constipation); surgical options (e.g. mid-urethral sling procedures) for those not responding to conservative Mx; duloxetine is an option in some contexts for patients declining/unsuitable for surgery, though less effective than surgical Mx.
- **Urge incontinence/overactive bladder:** bladder training (first-line — scheduled voiding, gradually increasing intervals) combined with lifestyle modification (reduce caffeine/fluid intake as appropriate); antimuscarinics (e.g. oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin) or beta-3 agonists (e.g. mirabegron, useful particularly when antimuscarinic side effects — dry mouth, constipation, cognitive effects especially in the elderly — limit tolerability) if bladder training alone is insufficient; botulinum toxin bladder injections or sacral nerve stimulation for refractory cases.
- **Overflow incontinence:** treat the underlying retention per the Urinary Retention Mx above — not repeated here.
- **Functional incontinence:** address the functional barrier directly (mobility aids, environmental modification, treat/optimise cognitive impairment where possible) rather than treating the bladder, given the bladder itself is functioning normally.
- **All types:** continence pads/products for symptomatic management alongside definitive Mx; address any contributing/reversible factors (UTI, constipation, medication review) regardless of subtype.

## 0.14 Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

**D:** lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) caused by bladder obstruction due to prostate enlargement (transitional zone).

> [!info] LUTS
> **Storage symptoms:** frequency, urgency, nocturia, incontinence
> **Voiding symptoms:** weak stream, dribbling, dysuria, straining

**A:** hyperplasia of epithelial and stromal compartments.

**P:**
- Static component: increased tissue causes urethral lumen narrowing
- Dynamic component: increased prostatic smooth muscle tone mediated by α-receptors

**R:** >50yo

> [!danger] Refer to urology if: haematuria, neurological disease (?neurogenic bladder), history of prior urological surgeries and urethral stricture, recurrent or persistent UTI, retention/palpable bladder, renal impairment secondary to BPH, abnormal DRE, abnormal PSA levels.

**Ix:** urine dipstick (*why:* screens for an alternative/co-existing cause of LUTS, particularly infection, which would need its own treatment and could mimic or exacerbate BPH symptoms; *what:* typically normal in uncomplicated BPH — an abnormal result should prompt consideration of infection or another cause rather than assuming BPH). PSA (*why:* screens for coexisting prostate cancer, given LUTS overlap between BPH and prostate cancer, and also somewhat correlates with prostate volume; *what:* age-adjusted limits apply — see Prostate Cancer below for the fuller current AU-specific PSA interpretation framework, not repeated here). International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) (*why:* quantifies symptom severity in a standardised, reproducible way, both supporting the diagnosis and providing a baseline to track treatment response over time; *what:* the specific severity category per the scoring below). Voiding diary (*why:* objectively characterises the voiding pattern (frequency, volumes, nocturia), useful when the history alone is ambiguous or to distinguish BPH from other causes like polyuria from diabetes/diuretics; *what:* documents the actual pattern rather than relying on patient recall). ± US, CT/MRI, cystoscopy (*why:* reserved for specific indications — assessing post-void residual/hydronephrosis (US), excluding other structural pathology, or direct visualisation when the diagnosis is unclear or complications are suspected; *what:* not routine first-line for straightforward BPH, used selectively per the red-flag/referral criteria above).

> [!info] IPSS: 7 questions, scale 0–5. Covers incomplete emptying, frequency, intermittency, urgency, weak stream, straining, nocturia, ± QoL 0–6. Mild 0–7, moderate 8–19, severe 20–35.

**Mx:**
- **Conservative:** watchful waiting; lifestyle modifications (decrease evening fluids, decrease caffeine/alcohol, change diuretic timing)
- **Medical:** α-blockers (doxazosin, tamsulosin) first-line for symptom relief; 5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride) especially for patients at risk of progression; PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil) to improve LUTS, ED, and QoL; anticholinergics (tolterodine)
- **Surgical:** refer to urologists if complications secondary to BPH (renal insufficiency, recurrent bladder stones) or refractory response to medications → transurethral resection of prostate (TURP)

### 0.14.1 TURP Syndrome

**D:** life-threatening complication of TURP, secondary to large volumes of glycine irrigation fluid being absorbed systemically when prostatic venous sinuses are opened during resection.

**P:** hyponatraemia; breakdown of glycine causing hyperammonaemia and visual changes.

**S/Smx:** appear 15 min to 24h post-op. Respiratory — distress, hypoxia, pulmonary oedema. GI — nausea/vomiting. Visual disturbances (e.g. blindness). Neuro — confusion, seizures, coma. AKI. Reflex bradycardia.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** treat underlying cause (e.g. stop surgery — recognising and halting ongoing fluid absorption is the single most important step); symptomatic — resuscitation (ABC); fluid overload — furosemide; seizures — benzodiazepines; severe hyponatraemia — hypertonic saline, applying the same cautious correction-rate principle as for any severe hyponatraemia (see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Urgent Considerations in Hyponatraemia for the fuller detail on avoiding overcorrection/osmotic demyelination, not repeated here).
- **Chronic/long-term:** intraoperative prevention is the real long-term Mx — limiting resection time, using the lowest effective irrigation pressure, and increasingly performing bipolar TURP with saline irrigation instead of glycine specifically to avoid this complication altogether, worth knowing as the modern practice shift that has reduced TURP syndrome incidence.

## 0.15 Urinary Tract Stones

**D:** presence of crystalline stones (calculi) within the urinary system (kidneys and ureter).

| Stone type | Frequency | Urine pH | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium oxalate | 85% | Variable | R: hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia (citrate normally makes calcium more soluble); radio-opaque; hyperuricosuria may cause uric acid stones onto which calcium oxalate binds |
| Cystine | 1% | Acidic to normal | Inherited recessive disorder of transmembrane cystine transport → decreased intestinal/renal tubular absorption; multiple stones may form; relatively radiodense (may contain sulphur) |
| Uric acid | 5–10% | Acidic | R: gout, high-purine diet (precipitates at low urinary pH), high cell turnover (malignancy), ileostomy (more acidic urine), inborn errors of metabolism; radiolucent |
| Calcium phosphate | 10% | Normal to alkaline | R: RTA types 1 and 3 (↑urinary pH → supersaturation of urine with Ca, PO4); radio-opaque (bone-like density) |
| Struvite | 2–20% | Alkaline | Magnesium, ammonium, and PO4 — form under alkaline conditions due to urease-producing bacteria (associated with chronic infections); slightly radio-opaque; staghorn calculi (renal pelvis + ≥2 calyces) secondary to *Ureaplasma urealyticum* and *Proteus* infections |

**R:** dehydration, medullary sponge kidney, polycystic kidney disease, beryllium or cadmium exposure.

**Drugs increasing calcium stone risk:** loop diuretics, steroids, acetazolamide, theophylline.

**S/Smx:** ureteric/renal colic — severe, intermittent, stabbing pain radiating from loin to groin (patient unable to keep still due to pain); haematuria (usually microscopic); systemic symptoms (nausea/vomiting, ↑HR, fever); loin/renal angle tenderness; iliac fossa tenderness.

> [!info] The mechanism behind the "loin to groin" pattern — genuinely useful clinically, not just a fixed phrase to memorise. Ureteric pain is visceral, referred pain, and its location tracks the dermatomes sharing the same spinal segments as the ureter's sympathetic afferent supply **at the level the stone currently sits** — as the stone migrates distally down the ureter, the pain migrates with it. A stone in the **renal pelvis or proximal ureter** refers pain to the **flank/loin** (T10–T12 dermatomes); as it progresses toward the **vesicoureteric junction**, referred pain shifts toward the **groin, and can extend to the scrotum/labia and anterior thigh** (L1, via the ilioinguinal and genitofemoral nerve territories). **This has genuine clinical value beyond just recognising the pattern**: groin/genital pain suggests the stone has already migrated close to the bladder — a location associated with a substantially higher likelihood of spontaneous passage — while pain confined to the flank suggests a more proximal stone, less likely to pass without intervention.

**Ix:** urine dip + culture (*why:* screens for a concurrent/contributing infection, particularly relevant given struvite stones are directly infection-driven (see the table above) and any infection proximal to an obstructing stone is a genuine emergency (infected obstructed system) requiring urgent decompression; *what:* may show haematuria, and positive culture supports an infective component or struvite stone specifically). Bloods — U&Es, FBC, CRP (*why:* U&Es assess for renal impairment from obstruction; FBC/CRP screen for infection/systemic inflammatory response, again relevant to the infected-obstruction emergency; *what:* may show AKI if obstructive, or raised inflammatory markers if infected). Ca and urate levels (*why:* screens for the metabolic risk factors driving calcium and uric acid stone formation specifically (see the stone-type table above), relevant to secondary prevention Mx below; *what:* hypercalcaemia or hyperuricaemia would suggest a specific metabolic driver worth addressing).

> [!info] Partially re-verified with additional findings, Aug 2026 — non-contrast CT KUB remains confirmed as the internationally standard gold-standard imaging modality for suspected renal colic (not UK-specific), so that part of the note still doesn't need correction. On the specific timeframe: Australia does **not** have a stone-specific "within 14h/24h" imaging access target matching the UK's BAUS/NICE standard — the relevant Australian framework instead is the **National Emergency Access Target (NEAT)**, a whole-of-encounter standard requiring 90% of ED presentations to be admitted, discharged, or transferred within **4 hours**, which indirectly drives urgency for same-visit imaging without being a stone-specific target. Genuinely different in emphasis too: the **Australasian College for Emergency Medicine's Choosing Wisely recommendation actively advises *against* routine/repeat CT KUB** in younger, low-risk patients with typical renal colic that settles with analgesia and no complicating features — a more imaging-conservative stance than the UK approach, reflecting radiation-exposure concerns rather than a same-day-imaging mandate for everyone. In practice, Australian EDs are documented (per a 2025 Emergency Medicine Australasia audit) as still experiencing long imaging waits for renal colic despite this. Treat "within 14h" as illustrative of general urgency (same-visit imaging preferred over delayed outpatient studies) rather than an Australian benchmark, and note that ACEM's guidance leans toward being more selective about ordering CT KUB at all in low-risk patients, not just about how quickly it's done.

**Mx:**
- Analgesia (diclofenac PR/IM), antiemetic (metoclopramide), IV fluid resuscitation
- Stones ≤5mm: watchful waiting (usually pass within 4 weeks of symptom onset)
- More severe stones: shock wave lithotripsy, percutaneous nephrolithotomy, ureteroscopy, open surgery
- Ureteric obstruction: emergency — nephrostomy tube, ureteric catheters, or stents — **particularly urgent if the obstructed system is also infected (infected hydronephrosis), given the risk of rapid sepsis/renal destruction if decompression is delayed**

**Shock wave lithotripsy:** shock wave generated externally; internal cavitation bubbles and mechanical stress cause fragmentation. Risks — solid organ injury, ureteric obstruction. Requires analgesia during/after. Suitable for most patients with a stone burden <2cm aggregate.

**Ureteroscopy:** for patients in whom lithotripsy is contraindicated (e.g. pregnancy); a stent is usually left for 4 weeks post-procedure.

**Percutaneous nephrolithotomy:** access to the renal collecting system; intra-corporeal lithotripsy or fragmentation performed, then fragments removed. Reserved for complex renal calculi and staghorn calculi.

**Prevention:**
- Calcium stones: increased water intake, decreased animal protein, decreased salt ± thiazide diuretics
- Oxalate stones: cholestyramine and pyridoxine
- Uric acid stones: allopurinol, urinary alkalinisation (bicarbonate)

## 0.16 Erectile Dysfunction (ED)

**D:** inability to achieve or maintain an erection satisfactory for sexual intercourse.

**Causes:**
- Psychogenic: anxiety, depression
- Drugs: SSRIs, β-blockers, recreational drugs, tobacco, alcohol
- Vascular: hypercholesterolaemia, atheroma, DM
- Metabolic/endocrine: azotaemia, hypogonadism, hyperthyroidism, hyperprolactinaemia
- Neurological: Parkinson's disease, stroke/TIA, spinal injury, neurological damage following pelvic surgery, pelvic fracture, autonomic neuropathies
- Penile: cavernositis, Peyronie's disease, previous priapism

**S/Smx:** presence of morning erections strongly suggests a psychogenic cause. Small testicles/lack of secondary sexual characteristics — endocrine cause. Lack of lower limb pulses — vascular cause. Neuro deficits in S2–4 — neurological cause.

**Ix:** bloods — glucose, lipids, U&Es, free testosterone, FSH/LH, prolactin, thyroid function (*why:* ED is frequently the first presenting symptom of an underlying vascular, metabolic, or endocrine disease — this broad screening panel reflects ED's role as a genuine "sentinel symptom" for cardiovascular and endocrine risk, not just a standalone problem, so a thorough work-up is warranted even when a psychogenic component seems likely; *what:* identifies diabetes/dyslipidaemia (vascular risk), hypogonadism (low testosterone with high or low FSH/LH depending on primary vs secondary cause), hyperprolactinaemia, or thyroid dysfunction as contributing causes — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] for the relevant disease-level detail on any abnormal result, not repeated here). Dynamic cavernosometry ± angiography (*why:* reserved for more specialist assessment when a vascular (venous leak or arterial insufficiency) cause is suspected and not adequately characterised by the initial screen, particularly relevant if surgical/interventional Mx is being considered; *what:* identifies the specific vascular mechanism if present).

> [!note] Measure free testosterone between 9–11am; measure for all men presenting with ED.

**Mx:**
- Psychotherapy or specialist sexual counselling for psychogenic causes
- PO PDE5 inhibitors (e.g. sildenafil)
- Sublingual apomorphine
- Intracavernosal — prostaglandins, α-blockers, papaverine
- Patients cycling >3h/week with ED should be advised to stop (?evidence uncertain)
- Refer young men with lifelong difficulty achieving erection to urology

> [!note] Peyronie's disease: fibrous tissue in the penis causing curvature during erection; associated with Dupuytren's contracture.

## 0.17 Urological Cancers

### 0.17.1 Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC)

**D:** renal malignancy arising from the renal parenchyma/cortex — 90% of kidney cancers, 2% of all cancers.

**R:** smoking, M>F (3:1), >55yo, black/American-Indian ethnicity, obesity, HTN, family history, hereditary syndromes (VHL, tuberous sclerosis), acquired renal cystic disease

> [!info] TNM staging
> T1: tumour ≤7cm, confined to kidney | T2: tumour >7cm, confined to kidney | T3: extends into major veins, invades adrenal gland or perinephric tissue | T4: extends beyond Gerota's fascia or distant metastases

**S/Smx:** haematuria, loin pain, abdominal mass; fever of unknown origin. Endocrine effects: ↑EPO, PTH, renin, ACTH. Paraneoplastic hepatic dysfunction syndrome. Left-sided varicocele (nutcracker syndrome — compression of left renal vein). Stauffer syndrome (cholestasis/hepatosplenomegaly, paraneoplastic, secondary to ↑IL-6).

**Ix:** bloods — FBC, U&Es (*why:* screens for paraneoplastic effects (e.g. the erythropoietin-driven polycythaemia noted above) and baseline renal function relevant to surgical planning, particularly given nephrectomy is the definitive Mx; *what:* may show polycythaemia if EPO-secreting, or baseline renal impairment). CT-TAP pre- and post-IV contrast (*why:* the key staging investigation, characterising the tumour and screening for the local invasion/metastatic spread that determines the TNM stage above and thus the surgical approach; *what:* enhanced CT to assess size, local extent, invasion, metastases — directly informing the T1/T2 vs T3/T4 distinction that determines partial vs total nephrectomy below). Isotope bone scan if bony metastases suspected (*why:* RCC has a recognised tendency toward bony metastasis, so symptomatic patients (bone pain) warrant specific screening beyond the CT-TAP; *what:* identifies bony metastatic deposits if present).

**Mx:** surgery — partial nephrectomy if T1 tumour, otherwise total nephrectomy. Trials: α-interferon and IL-2, receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Manage anaemia also.

**P:** adverse risk factors — extracapsular spread, renal vein invasion, lymph node involvement. Periodic radiological follow-up recommended in most cases.

### 0.17.2 Bladder Cancer

**D:** malignancy of the urinary bladder.
- Urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma — 90% of cases
- SCC — 1–7% of cases
- Adenocarcinoma — 2% of cases

**R:** smoking, exposure to aromatic amines (rubber and dye industries), arsenic (especially painters and hairdressers), >65yo, pelvic radiation, systemic chemotherapy, schistosoma infection (especially in Arabic populations — increased SCC risk), M>F (3:1), chronic bladder inflammation, family history

> [!info] TNM staging
> Ta: non-invasive papillary carcinoma | T1: invades subepithelial connective tissue | T2a: invades superficial muscularis propria | T2b: invades deep muscularis propria | T3: extends to perivesical fat | T4a: invades uterus, prostate, or bowel | T4b: invades pelvic sidewall, abdominal wall | N1: single regional LN in true pelvis | N2: multiple regional LNs in true pelvis | N3: metastasis to common iliac LN

**Transitional cell carcinoma:** solitary or multifocal. 70% papillary growth pattern — superficial, better prognosis. 30% mixed papillary/solid or pure solid growth — more prone to local invasion, worse prognosis.

**S/Smx:** 85% present with painless, frank haematuria.

**Ix:** urinalysis (*why:* confirms the haematuria that typically prompts investigation, and screens for a concurrent infective cause of haematuria before assuming malignancy; *what:* haematuria, often macroscopic given the classic presentation above). Staging — cystoscopy, biopsy, pelvic MRI, CT-TAP, PET-CT for metastases (*why:* cystoscopy + biopsy provides direct visualisation and histological diagnosis, essential given Mx differs so substantially by invasion depth (superficial vs muscle-invasive, per the TNM staging above); imaging (MRI/CT-TAP/PET-CT) stages local and distant spread, directly determining whether TURBT alone is sufficient or radical treatment is needed; *what:* the specific stage per the TNM criteria above, feeding directly into the Mx tiering below).

**Mx:** transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) may be sufficient for superficial lesions + intravesical chemotherapy. May require radical cystectomy and ileal conduit, or radical radiotherapy.

**P:** lymph node involvement markedly decreases survival. 5-year survival: T3 ≈35%, T4a ≤25%.

### 0.17.3 Prostate Cancer

**D:** malignancy of the glandular prostate.

**R:** >50yo, ?black ethnicity, family history

**P:** originates from high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia → 95% adenocarcinomas. 70% multifocal, in the peripheral zone. Graded using the Gleason grading system. Lymphatic spread — obturator lymph nodes first.

**S/Smx:** most often asymptomatic. As tumour size increases — LUTS, haematuria, haematospermia, pain (back, perineal, testicular). DRE — asymmetrical, hard, nodular enlargement with loss of the median sulcus. Metastases usually bony, causing bone pain.

**Ix:** multiparametric MRI first-line (*why:* now the standard initial investigation ahead of biopsy, since it can identify which patients genuinely need biopsy (avoiding unnecessary biopsies and their complications, noted below) and can target biopsy to suspicious areas when performed; *what:* reported using a 5-point Likert scale (≥3: offer prostate biopsy; 1–2: consider, discuss with patient) — this MRI-first approach represents a significant evolution from older "PSA then biopsy everyone" pathways).

> [!warning] Complications of transrectal US-guided (TRUS) biopsy: sepsis (1%), pain (≥2 weeks in 15%, severe in 7%), fever (5%), haematuria, rectal bleeding.

> [!info] Verified against the RACGP's updated 2024/2025 PSA testing guidelines (published in AJGP December 2025) and the new "2026 Guidelines for the Early Detection of Prostate Cancer in Australia," Aug 2026 — the Australian framework is structured quite differently from the UK NICE age-banded single-PSA-threshold model, and reflects a genuinely recent (2024-2025) shift in RACGP's own position (RACGP had previously recommended *against* routine PSA screening from 2009 until this update — worth knowing as context, since older Australian teaching/sources may still reflect the pre-2024 "don't screen" stance).
> **Screening/testing offer:** offer PSA testing to men from age 50 (average risk), or from age 45 for men with a first-degree relative with prostate cancer (high risk) — after discussing risks/benefits with the patient, rather than a blanket screening recommendation.
> **Referral/further-investigation criterion:** **two elevated PSA levels >3.0 ng/mL, combined with a free:total PSA ratio <25%, within a 3-month timeframe**, in men <70yo — this combined-criteria approach (repeat testing + free:total ratio, not a single PSA cutoff) is a materially different structure from the UK's simpler single-PSA age-banded threshold (3/4/5 ng/mL by age band) — don't teach the UK-style flat age-banded threshold as the Australian standard. Medicare rebate for the follow-up MRI requires it to be ordered by a urology specialist, a genuinely Australian health-system-specific detail with no UK equivalent.
> Abnormal DRE remains an independent trigger for further investigation regardless of PSA result, consistent with the general principle in the original note.

> [!note] PSA can be raised by BPH, urinary retention, prostatitis & UTI (wait 4 weeks before testing), ejaculation (wait 48h), vigorous exercise (wait 48h), instrumentation of the urinary tract (e.g. catheters), DRE. Poor sensitivity and specificity for prostate cancer.

**Mx:**
- Localised cancer (T1/T2): watchful waiting, radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy
- Locally advanced cancer (T3/T4): hormonal therapy (anti-androgens), radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy
- Metastatic cancer: anti-androgen therapy
  - Synthetic GnRH agonists (e.g. goserelin — decreases LH, decreases testosterone); must be covered with an anti-androgen initially to prevent tumour flare
  - Bicalutamide — non-steroidal anti-androgen, blocks the androgen receptor
  - Cyproterone acetate — steroidal anti-androgen, prevents DHT binding
  - Abiraterone — androgen synthesis inhibitor, 2nd line
- Other options: bilateral orchidectomy, chemotherapy with docetaxel

## 0.18 Other Urology Topics

### 0.18.1 Circumcision

**Medical indications:** phimosis, recurrent balanitis, balanitis xerotica obliterans, paraphimosis. Can be done under LA or GA.

> [!danger] Exclude hypospadias first — the foreskin may be needed for surgical repair.

> [!info] Verified — Australia's situation is actually the reverse of what this UK-specific note implies, not simply "not applicable." Medicare in Australia **does** provide a rebate for non-medically-indicated (including religious/cultural) circumcision via MBS item 30654, despite Medicare rebates technically being restricted to "clinically relevant" services — this has been a long-running point of advocacy controversy (calls to defund it have not succeeded as of the most recent MBS Review Taskforce recommendations), rather than a clean NHS-style exclusion. The out-of-pocket cost for non-medically-indicated circumcision in Australia is typically only partially offset by the Medicare rebate (the schedule fee/rebate is a relatively small fixed amount, e.g. ~$41, against a much higher total procedure cost), so most of the cost is still borne privately — practically similar in effect to the UK's non-funding, but for a different underlying reason (a small, incomplete rebate rather than an explicit exclusion).

### 0.18.2 Vasectomy

**D:** male sterilisation involving disruption of sperm flow via the vas deferens.

Failure rate 1:2000 (more effective than female sterilisation). Semen analysis must be performed twice after the operation before unprotected sex is permitted (usually at 12 weeks).

**Complications:** bruising, haematoma, infection, sperm granuloma, chronic testicular pain (15%).

**Vasectomy reversal:** success rate 55% if performed within 10 years — should be regarded as irreversible.

### 0.18.3 Priapism

**D:** penile erection >4h in the absence of sexual excitation.

> [!danger] Medical emergency.

**A:** thromboembolic states (e.g. sickle cell disease), neoplastic disease, spinal shock, drugs

**Ix:** cavernosal blood gas analysis (*why:* the key test distinguishing ischaemic (low-flow) from non-ischaemic (high-flow) priapism, a distinction that fundamentally changes Mx urgency and approach — ischaemic priapism is the genuine emergency requiring urgent decompression, while non-ischaemic priapism is typically managed more conservatively; *what:* ischaemic priapism shows a hypoxic, acidotic, hypercarbic picture similar to venous blood, given the erection reflects trapped, poorly-oxygenated blood rather than ongoing arterial inflow) or US (*why:* an alternative/adjunct method assessing cavernosal blood flow directly, particularly useful for distinguishing high-flow from low-flow priapism when blood gas sampling is difficult or to identify a fistula in high-flow cases; *what:* absent/minimal flow in ischaemic priapism vs preserved/increased flow in non-ischaemic priapism).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (ischaemic priapism — the genuine emergency):** aspiration of blood from the cavernosa ± saline flush to clear pooled blood; if this fails, intracavernosal injection of a vasoconstrictive drug (e.g. phenylephrine) — time-critical, given prolonged ischaemic priapism risks permanent erectile tissue damage/fibrosis and future ED.
- **Definitive:** surgical options (e.g. shunt procedures) if the above measures fail to resolve the ischaemic episode.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address the underlying cause where identifiable (e.g. haematology input for sickle cell disease, medication review if drug-induced) to reduce recurrence risk, given priapism can recur especially in conditions like sickle cell disease.

## 0.19 Urethral and Bladder Trauma

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Trauma e.g. urethral injury," High yield) — completely absent from the source notes despite being a classic exam topic, particularly the specific examination findings suggesting urethral injury after pelvic trauma.

**D:** injury to the urethra or bladder, most commonly in the context of pelvic trauma (fractures) or, for the urethra specifically, straddle injuries.

**A:**
- **Urethral injury:** pelvic fracture (posterior urethral injury — at the prostatomembranous junction, typically from shearing forces as the pelvis fractures) or straddle injury (anterior/bulbar urethral injury — direct blunt trauma, e.g. falling astride an object); iatrogenic (catheterisation trauma, particularly relevant to know as a "don't make it worse" principle below).
- **Bladder injury:** blunt trauma with a full bladder (the distended bladder is more vulnerable to rupture) — pelvic fracture-associated (extraperitoneal rupture, more common) or direct blow to a full bladder (intraperitoneal rupture, more common, and surgically more urgent given urine leaks into the peritoneal cavity).

**S/Smx:**
- **Urethral injury (classic triad):** blood at the urethral meatus (the single most important examination finding — its presence should prompt urgent urological assessment before any catheterisation attempt), inability to void, and a palpably distended bladder. **High-riding or boggy/impalpable prostate on DRE** is a classically taught sign of posterior urethral injury (displacement from the shearing pelvic fracture), though its reliability is debated in more recent literature — don't rely on this sign alone to exclude injury. Perineal/scrotal haematoma (a "butterfly" pattern haematoma is classic for anterior urethral injury given the fascial planes blood tracks along).
- **Bladder injury:** suprapubic pain/tenderness, gross haematuria (present in the large majority of significant bladder injuries — its absence makes clinically significant bladder injury less likely), inability to void, signs of associated pelvic fracture.

> [!danger] **Do NOT attempt urethral catheterisation if urethral injury is suspected** (blood at the meatus, high-riding prostate, perineal haematoma, or significant pelvic fracture mechanism) — catheterisation attempts can convert a partial urethral injury into a complete transection, or introduce infection into a disrupted urethra. Obtain urological input and appropriate imaging first.

**Ix:** retrograde urethrogram (*why:* the definitive test for suspected urethral injury, performed **before** any catheterisation attempt when injury is suspected — directly visualises the urethra and identifies the site/extent of disruption without risking further injury from a blind catheter pass; *what:* extravasation of contrast at the injury site confirms and localises the injury). CT cystogram (*why:* the definitive test for suspected bladder injury, and importantly must be performed with the bladder adequately distended with contrast — an underfilled cystogram can miss a rupture, a genuine pitfall worth knowing; *what:* extravasation of contrast — intraperitoneal (contrast outlining bowel loops) vs extraperitoneal (contrast confined to the pelvis) pattern distinguishes the two types with different Mx implications, as above). These injuries frequently occur alongside major trauma, so full trauma assessment/imaging (e.g. CT for associated pelvic fracture and other injuries) proceeds in parallel per standard major trauma protocols, not detailed further in this source.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** standard major trauma resuscitation takes priority given the frequent association with significant pelvic trauma; if urethral injury is suspected, do not catheterise (see above) — a suprapubic catheter may be needed for urinary drainage instead, placed by a clinician experienced in the technique.
- **Definitive (urethral injury):** partial injuries may be managed with a period of urinary diversion (suprapubic catheter) and delayed definitive repair; complete disruption typically requires either early or delayed surgical realignment/repair depending on the specific injury pattern and associated injuries, decided by urological specialists.
- **Definitive (bladder injury):** extraperitoneal rupture can often be managed conservatively with catheter drainage alone, given the extraperitoneal space tends to tamponade and heal; intraperitoneal rupture generally requires surgical repair, given ongoing urine leak into the peritoneal cavity risks peritonitis and doesn't reliably heal with drainage alone.
- **Chronic/long-term:** urethral stricture is a recognised long-term complication of urethral injury (particularly after posterior urethral disruption), sometimes requiring delayed reconstructive surgery — patients should be counselled about this possibility and followed up accordingly.

## 0.20 Testicular Torsion

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Testicular Torsion"/"Torsion of testis," both High yield) — previously only covered within the compact Testicular Lumps comparison table further below; given a full dedicated entry here given its status as a true time-critical surgical emergency, distinct from the other more chronic/subacute conditions in that table.

**D:** twisting of the spermatic cord, causing constriction of the testicular blood supply — a surgical emergency where delayed treatment risks testicular loss.

**R:** <25yo (peak incidence 13–15yo), neonates (a separate, less common presentation — extravaginal torsion), bell-clapper deformity (a congenital anatomical variant where the tunica vaginalis abnormally envelops the entire epididymis and distal spermatic cord, allowing the testis to rotate freely within it — the anatomical basis for most adolescent/young-adult torsion), previous episodes of intermittent torsion/detorsion, cold weather, trauma.

**A/P:** twisting of the spermatic cord constricts venous outflow first, then arterial inflow as twisting progresses → venous congestion and oedema → ischaemia → infarction and necrosis if not relieved promptly.

**S/Smx:** sudden, severe onset of unilateral scrotal/testicular pain, often waking the patient from sleep; pain may be referred to the lower abdomen or groin ± nausea and vomiting (which can misdirect toward a GI diagnosis if the scrotal exam is not performed); "high-riding" testicle (elevated position due to cord shortening from twisting); horizontal lie of the affected testis (loss of the normal vertical orientation); **absent cremasteric reflex on the affected side** (a useful but not perfectly sensitive/specific sign — the mechanism is mechanical, not neurological: the cremaster muscle fibres wrap around and are physically continuous with the spermatic cord, so when the cord twists, the muscle's own ability to contract and elevate the testis is directly disrupted by the torsion itself, rather than the reflex's afferent/efferent nerve pathway being damaged — this is why the reflex specifically fails on the torted side while remaining intact on the normal contralateral side, and why it's a genuinely useful lateralising sign); **negative Prehn's sign — pain is NOT relieved by elevating the testis**, in contrast to epididymo-orchitis where elevation classically eases pain (positive Prehn's sign) — though this sign is not reliable enough to exclude torsion on its own and should never delay urgent assessment/surgical referral.

> [!danger] Testicular torsion is a clinical diagnosis and a time-critical surgical emergency — **do not delay surgical referral for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.** Testicular salvage rates fall sharply with time: salvage is high (>90%) if surgery occurs within 6 hours of symptom onset, but drops substantially beyond 12–24 hours, and is low beyond this window. "Time is testicle" — the same urgency principle as "time is brain" in stroke.

**Ix:** this is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis, and investigation should never delay surgical exploration when suspicion is high — Doppler ultrasound ("whirlpool sign" — the twisted appearance of the spermatic cord, and absent/reduced testicular blood flow) can support the diagnosis and help distinguish torsion from other causes of acute scrotal pain (e.g. epididymo-orchitis, torsion of a testicular appendage) **when the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain and obtaining the scan will not meaningfully delay definitive Mx** — but a normal or equivocal ultrasound should never override strong clinical suspicion, given the ultrasound's imperfect sensitivity and the catastrophic consequence of a missed/delayed diagnosis.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** urgent surgical exploration — this is the priority, and should not be delayed for imaging or other investigations if clinical suspicion is high, given how directly outcome depends on time to detorsion (see above); analgesia and antiemetics while arranging theatre; **manual detorsion can be attempted by an experienced clinician while awaiting surgery** (classically rotating the testis "open book" — laterally — though direction can vary, and successful detorsion is confirmed by symptom relief), but this does not replace the need for surgical exploration and fixation, since manual detorsion can be incomplete or the testis can re-torse.
- **Definitive:** surgical exploration, detorsion, and assessment of testicular viability; **bilateral orchidopexy (fixation) is performed regardless of which side was affected** — the contralateral (unaffected) side is fixed prophylactically at the same operation, since the bell-clapper anatomical variant predisposing to torsion is typically bilateral, and the contralateral testis remains at ongoing risk of torsion in the future if not fixed. Orchidectomy (removal) if the affected testis is non-viable at exploration.
- **Chronic/long-term:** patients who undergo orchidectomy may wish to discuss a testicular prosthesis; fertility counselling may be relevant given the loss of one testis, though the remaining testis is generally sufficient for fertility in most cases.

**P:** testicular salvage is highly time-dependent (see above) — this is the single most important prognostic factor, far outweighing any other clinical feature, which is why the overriding message in this topic is urgency of surgical referral over diagnostic certainty.

## 0.21 Testicular Cancer

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Testicular cancer," High yield) — previously only covered within the compact Testicular Lumps comparison table below (a single row), exactly parallel to how Testicular Torsion above was expanded from that same table into its own full entry; given the same treatment here for consistency, given its equal High-yield status. Also closes a related gap: beta-hCG was absent from the Tumour markers table in [[10_11a_Oncology_-_Common_Cancers__Carcinogens__Tumour_Markers]] Tumour markers despite being one of the classic markers for this exact cancer — cross-referenced below rather than duplicated in that table.

**D:** malignancy arising from the testis — the most common cancer in young men (peak incidence age 15–35), and genuinely one of the most curable solid cancers when caught and treated appropriately, making early recognition and referral highly consequential.

**R:** cryptorchidism (undescended testis, even if surgically corrected) — the single most established risk factor; family history of testicular cancer; personal history of testicular cancer in the contralateral testis; infertility; Klinefelter syndrome; increased height (a genuinely real, if less intuitive, epidemiological association).

**A/P — germ cell tumours account for the large majority of testicular cancers, and split into two clinically distinct categories with different behaviour and Mx:**
- **Seminoma:** typically presents at a somewhat older age within the overall young-adult range, tends to be slower-growing and more radiosensitive/chemosensitive, and carries a generally excellent prognosis even with more advanced disease.
- **Non-seminomatous germ cell tumours (NSGCT)** — a mixed category including embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma, and teratoma — tend to present somewhat younger, grow and spread more rapidly, and are managed somewhat more aggressively given this behaviour, though modern chemotherapy has made even advanced NSGCT highly treatable.

**S/Smx:** classically a painless testicular lump or diffuse enlargement — noticed by the patient or found incidentally; discrete, craggy, hard lesion on examination, sometimes with an associated hydrocele; gynaecomastia can occur (from beta-hCG cross-reacting with LH receptors, or from oestrogen-secreting tumours), a genuinely useful if easily-overlooked associated finding. **Any painless testicular lump should be assumed malignant until proven otherwise** and investigated promptly — the painless nature is precisely why presentation can be delayed, unlike the acute pain of torsion or epididymo-orchitis which the same testicular-lumps table above compares this against.

**Ix:**
- **Scrotal ultrasound** — first-line imaging, essentially always performed for any suspicious testicular lump.
- **Serum tumour markers — genuinely central to both diagnosis and ongoing management, not just a supportive test:** **AFP** (alpha-fetoprotein — elevated in yolk sac tumour/embryonal components; **never elevated in pure seminoma**, so an elevated AFP with seminoma-appearing histology means the tumour should be treated as NSGCT regardless of the histological label), **beta-hCG** (elevated in choriocarcinoma and, notably, in a meaningful proportion of seminomas too — around 15% — so its presence doesn't exclude seminoma the way AFP elevation does), and **LDH** (a non-specific marker of tumour burden, useful for staging and monitoring rather than histological subtyping). **Markers must be drawn before orchidectomy** — baseline levels are needed for staging and for tracking post-treatment response, given persistently elevated or rising markers after surgery are often the earliest sign of residual or recurrent disease, sometimes preceding radiological evidence.
- **Staging CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** once the diagnosis is confirmed, to assess for nodal/metastatic spread (retroperitoneal lymph nodes are the classic first site).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/definitive: radical inguinal orchidectomy** — genuinely the standard surgical approach for both diagnosis and initial treatment; **a trans-scrotal biopsy or trans-scrotal approach is specifically avoided**, given the risk of disrupting normal lymphatic drainage and seeding tumour cells into the scrotal skin/inguinal nodes, altering the pattern of potential spread.
- **Further Mx guided by histology, stage, and post-orchidectomy marker levels:** surveillance alone for low-stage disease with normalising markers; adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy (seminoma is particularly radiosensitive) for higher-stage or persistently marker-positive disease; retroperitoneal lymph node dissection is sometimes used, particularly for residual NSGCT masses after chemotherapy.
- **Fertility:** sperm banking should be offered and discussed **before** orchidectomy/chemotherapy wherever possible, given both the surgery itself (loss of one testis) and subsequent chemotherapy can affect fertility — this is a genuinely time-sensitive counselling point that shouldn't be an afterthought once treatment has already started.

**P:** genuinely excellent overall — 5-year survival exceeds 90% even accounting for more advanced presentations, reflecting how chemosensitive and radiosensitive most testicular germ cell tumours are; this makes testicular cancer one of the clearest examples in oncology where prompt diagnosis and appropriate specialist Mx translate into excellent outcomes even from advanced disease, rather than prognosis being primarily determined by stage at diagnosis alone.
