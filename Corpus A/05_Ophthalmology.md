## The Red Eye — Regional Approach and DDx

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Eye — Red eye," High yield, and "Eye — Painful eye," Medium yield) — the source notes cover the individual disease entities extensively but never assembled them into the unified presentation-led DDx a clinician actually needs at the bedside when a patient presents simply with "a red eye." This table cross-references the disease sections already built throughout this file rather than repeating their detail.

**Approach:** the single most useful triage question is *pain vs no pain*, followed by *vision affected or not* — these two questions alone separate the sight-threatening/urgent causes from the benign, self-limiting ones far more reliably than the redness pattern itself.

| Painful, vision often affected (urgent) | Painful, vision usually preserved | Painless, vision preserved |
|---|---|---|
| Acute angle-closure glaucoma (see Angle-Closure Glaucoma below) | Corneal abrasion (see below) | Subconjunctival haemorrhage (see below) |
| Anterior uveitis (see Anterior Uveitis below) | Episcleritis (see Episcleritis and Scleritis below) | Conjunctivitis, most cases (see Conjunctivitis below) |
| Scleritis (see Episcleritis and Scleritis below) | Blepharitis (see Blepharitis below) | Dry eye / blepharitis-associated redness |
| Keratitis, incl. microbial (see Keratitis below) | | |
| Endophthalmitis (see below) | | |

**Red flags warranting same-day/urgent ophthalmology review regardless of which category above:** reduced visual acuity, severe pain, photophobia, a fixed/irregular/poorly-reactive pupil, corneal opacity or visible infiltrate, hypopyon, recent intraocular surgery (endophthalmitis risk — see below), contact lens wear with pain (higher risk of severe microbial keratitis, including *Pseudomonas* and acanthamoeba — see Keratitis above), and any history of trauma.

**Closing note:** conjunctivitis is the most common cause of a red eye overall but should be a diagnosis of exclusion once vision-threatening causes are excluded, particularly in a painful or vision-affected presentation — see Conjunctivitis above for the specific bacterial/viral/allergic differentiation once the more urgent causes above have been ruled out.

## Subconjunctival Haemorrhage

**D:** bleeding beneath the conjunctiva from small, superficial blood vessels — a benign, self-limiting condition, but one that can look alarming to patients and must be distinguished from more serious causes of a red eye.

**R:** trauma (including vigorous coughing, sneezing, straining, vomiting — raised venous pressure alone can cause it), anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, hypertension, bleeding disorders, contact lens use, idiopathic (very common with no identifiable trigger).

**S/Smx:** a well-demarcated area of bright red blood under the conjunctiva, sharply bordered by normal-appearing conjunctiva; **crucially, no pain, no photophobia, no visual disturbance, and no discharge** — the presence of any of these should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis rather than assuming subconjunctival haemorrhage.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis on inspection alone in most cases (*why:* the appearance is characteristic and no further Ix is needed for an isolated, painless, vision-preserved presentation; *what:* the well-demarcated blood patch described above). Check blood pressure (*why:* screens for undiagnosed/poorly controlled hypertension as a contributing factor, and is worth checking opportunistically given how easy it is; *what:* may be elevated). Review anticoagulant/antiplatelet medications and ask about a bleeding tendency or recurrent episodes (*why:* recurrent or unusually severe subconjunctival haemorrhage can be the first sign of an underlying bleeding disorder or over-anticoagulation, worth a specific screen rather than assuming trauma/idiopathic in every case; *what:* identifies a modifiable cause if present). If there is a history of trauma, examine for an underlying globe injury (*why:* a subconjunctival haemorrhage following significant trauma can mask a more serious injury (globe rupture, orbital fracture) underneath; *what:* full ocular exam to exclude this, rather than attributing all post-traumatic redness to a benign subconjunctival bleed).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute tier — this is entirely self-resolving and needs no active treatment):** reassurance — the blood will resorb over 1–2 weeks, often changing colour (like a bruise) as it does; artificial tears if there's any associated mild irritation.
- **Chronic/long-term:** if recurrent, investigate and address any underlying cause identified above (BP control, review of anticoagulation dosing/indication, bleeding disorder work-up if suggested by the pattern).

**P:** excellent — complete resolution expected, with no visual sequelae.

**A metallic corneal foreign body left in place oxidises and leaves a `rust ring`** in the surrounding stroma. **Removing the foreign body is not the end of the job** — the ring itself impairs epithelial healing and, if central, affects vision, so it usually needs removal too, generally with a slit lamp and a burr by someone experienced.

**Always evert the upper lid** in a patient with a foreign-body sensation and nothing visible: a subtarsal foreign body produces vertical linear corneal abrasions from blinking, which is a diagnostic pattern.


## Corneal Abrasion

**D:** a defect in the corneal epithelium, most commonly from direct trauma.

**R:** trauma (fingernail, branch, paper edge — classic mechanisms), contact lens wear/removal, foreign body, exposure/dry eye (reduced blink/tear film increasing vulnerability).

**S/Smx:** significant pain (corneal innervation is dense, so even small abrasions are disproportionately painful), foreign body sensation, photophobia, watering, blepharospasm (involuntary eyelid closure), red eye; visual acuity may be mildly reduced if the abrasion is central/large, but should not be severely affected — significant visual loss should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis or a search for a complication.

**Ix:** fluorescein staining with a blue light/Wood's lamp (*why:* the key diagnostic test — fluorescein pools in the epithelial defect and fluoresces under blue light, directly visualising the abrasion's size and shape; *what:* a bright green-yellow area corresponding to the epithelial defect). Careful examination for a retained foreign body, including eyelid eversion to check the underside of the upper eyelid (*why:* a retained foreign body is a common cause of a "corneal abrasion" that doesn't heal as expected, and vertical/linear abrasions specifically suggest a foreign body trapped under the upper lid dragging across the cornea with each blink — a specific pattern worth recognising; *what:* may reveal a foreign body requiring removal before the abrasion will heal).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** topical antibiotic (to prevent secondary infection while the epithelium heals, particularly relevant for contact-lens-related abrasions given the higher risk of *Pseudomonas*); analgesia; avoid topical anaesthetic for ongoing/take-home use (provides only short-term relief and can impair healing/mask a worsening problem if used repeatedly by the patient — use only for the purposes of the examination itself, not as a prescribed take-home treatment).
- **Definitive:** most simple abrasions heal within 24–48h with the above supportive measures; larger or contact-lens-related abrasions may need closer follow-up given the higher infection risk.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address the underlying risk factor if recurrent (e.g. contact lens technique/hygiene review, management of an exposure/dry eye process).

**P:** excellent for simple abrasions; recurrent corneal erosion syndrome (the abrasion recurring, often overnight/on waking, due to poor epithelial adhesion after the initial injury) is a recognised complication worth warning patients about.

## Endophthalmitis

**D:** severe inflammation of the intraocular fluids (aqueous and/or vitreous humour), almost always infective in origin — a genuine ophthalmic emergency given the risk of rapid, permanent vision loss.

**R:** recent intraocular surgery (particularly cataract surgery — the most common precipitant, see Cataracts below), penetrating eye trauma, endogenous spread from a systemic infection/bacteraemia (less common, but relevant in immunocompromised or septic patients), pre-existing keratitis progressing to deeper involvement (see Keratitis below).

**S/Smx:** rapid onset (hours to days) of severe eye pain, marked redness, significantly reduced visual acuity, hypopyon, lid swelling; systemic symptoms may be present if the source is endogenous/septic.

**Ix:** urgent ophthalmology referral for vitreous/aqueous sampling (*why:* identifies the causative organism, essential for targeting the intravitreal antibiotic choice, given how rapidly vision can be lost if empirical therapy is inadequate; *what:* Gram stain and culture of the sampled fluid). Blood cultures if endogenous spread is suspected (*why:* identifies a systemic source requiring its own treatment alongside the eye; *what:* may identify the causative organism and its systemic source).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** this is a same-day ophthalmic emergency — intravitreal antibiotics (injected directly into the eye, given the poor penetration of systemic/topical antibiotics into the vitreous) started empirically pending culture results, given how quickly vision can be lost with delayed treatment.
- **Definitive:** vitrectomy for severe cases, particularly with significant vision loss at presentation, to physically remove infected/inflamed vitreous alongside antibiotic therapy.
- **Chronic/long-term:** even with prompt treatment, visual outcomes are often significantly and permanently reduced — patients need realistic counselling about this rather than an expectation of full recovery.

**P:** guarded even with prompt treatment; poor if treatment is delayed — this is one of the genuine "minutes to hours matter" ophthalmic emergencies.

## Retinal Detachment

**D:** detachment of the neuroretina from the underlying epithelial pigment layer.

**R:** DM (secondary to vitreous humour traction causing retinal breaks), myopia (> -8), ↑age, previous cataract surgery (accelerates posterior vitreous detachment), eye trauma

**S/Smx:**
- New onset floaters or flashes — pigment cells entering the vitreous space (floaters) or traction on the retina (flashes)
- Sudden onset, painless, progressive visual field loss — "curtain falling"; shadow progressing to the centre of the visual field from the periphery
- If the macula is involved, central visual acuity and visual outcomes become much worse
- Peripheral visual fields decrease; central acuity decreases to hand movements if macula is detached
- RAPD if the optic nerve is involved
- On fundoscopy: red reflex lost; retinal folds — pale, opaque or wrinkled (if the break is small, may appear normal)

**Ix:** visual acuity (*why:* baseline functional assessment and a key prognostic marker — macula-off detachment with reduced acuity is more urgent and carries a worse visual prognosis than macula-on; *what:* reduced if macula involved, may be near-normal if detachment is peripheral only). Slit-lamp exam (*why:* assesses the anterior segment and can reveal pigment cells in the anterior vitreous ("tobacco dust"/Shafer's sign), a strong indicator of retinal breaks; *what:* may show pigment cells or other anterior segment findings). Fundoscopy (*why:* the key diagnostic examination, directly visualising the detached retina; *what:* red reflex lost; retinal folds — pale, opaque or wrinkled). US of the eye (*why:* used specifically when media opacity — e.g. vitreous haemorrhage, cataract — prevents direct fundus visualisation; *what:* identifies the detached retina as an echogenic membrane within the vitreous cavity). CT/MRI (*why:* reserved for cases where trauma/orbital injury is suspected as the cause, to assess for other orbital/globe injury; *what:* not routine for uncomplicated retinal detachment).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** same-day referral to an eye emergency department (or the nearest ED with ophthalmology cover) — this is a genuine ophthalmic emergency, particularly if the macula is still attached (macula-on detachment), since visual outcome depends heavily on how quickly the retina is reattached before the macula itself detaches.
- **Definitive:** posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) without break/tear — laser treatment to reduce retinal detachment risk (PVD = vitreous detaches from retina; if it separates incompletely, can cause traction and pull on the retina → increased detachment risk). Pneumatic retinopexy — surgeon positions an air bubble to push the retina back into place, then freezes it there. Vitrectomy — for significant vitreous traction.
- **Chronic/long-term:** the fellow eye carries an increased risk of detachment given shared risk factors (myopia, age, prior vitreous changes) — patients should be counselled on new-onset floater/flash symptoms in either eye and the need for urgent review if these occur.

## Vitreous Haemorrhage

**A:** secondary to proliferative diabetic retinopathy (>50%), posterior vitreous detachment, or ocular trauma.

**S/Smx:** acute/subacute course — painless vision loss/haze, ± visual field defects if severe, red hue in vision, floaters, dark spots.

**Ix:** fundoscopy (*why:* the initial examination, though view may be significantly obscured by the haemorrhage itself; *what:* haemorrhage visible, though a dense bleed may prevent adequate fundus visualisation). Slit-lamp exam (*why:* assesses the anterior vitreous for red blood cells, useful even when the posterior view is obscured; *what:* RBCs in anterior vitreous). US (*why:* essential when the haemorrhage is dense enough to obscure the fundus view, to actively exclude the more urgent differential of retinal tear/detachment underlying the bleed; *what:* screens for an associated retinal tear/detachment that would need separate urgent Mx). Fluorescein angiography (*why:* identifies areas of retinal neovascularisation once the haemorrhage has cleared enough to visualise, relevant to guiding laser treatment; *what:* identifies leaking/abnormal new vessels). Orbital CT (*why:* used specifically if an open globe injury is suspected, to assess for globe rupture/foreign body; *what:* not routine unless trauma is the suspected cause).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** identify and address the underlying cause where urgent (e.g. exclude retinal detachment via US as above); most vitreous haemorrhage itself is managed conservatively in the first instance, with positioning advice (keeping the head elevated to allow blood to settle inferiorly, away from the visual axis) while the underlying cause is addressed.
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause — panretinal photocoagulation for proliferative diabetic retinopathy (see Diabetic Retinopathy below), retinal detachment repair if present; vitrectomy for non-clearing haemorrhage or when the underlying pathology needs direct surgical access.
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing management of the underlying cause (e.g. diabetic control and retinopathy surveillance) to prevent recurrence.

## Retinal Vein Occlusion

**D:** interruption of the normal venous drainage from the retinal tissue.
- Central vein = CRVO
- Branch of vein = BRVO

**R:** ↑age (>65), HTN, cardiovascular disease, glaucoma, polycythaemia, DM, hyperlipidaemia, smoking, ↑BMI

**Ix:** fundoscopy (*why:* the key diagnostic examination, directly visualising the characteristic pattern; *what:* widespread hyperaemia (tortuous, dilated venous system proximal to the occlusion), severe retinal haemorrhages ("stormy sunset"), retinal oedema). OCT and fluorescein angiography are often used by ophthalmology to assess macular oedema extent and guide anti-VEGF/laser decisions, though not detailed further in this source.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** no emergency intervention reverses the occlusion itself — Mx is directed at complications rather than the occlusion.
- **Definitive:** conservative (watch and wait) — manage comorbidities, reduce risk, monitor for complications. Macular oedema — anti-VEGF injections. Retinal neovascularisation — laser photocoagulation.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address underlying cardiovascular/metabolic risk factors (HTN, DM, hyperlipidaemia) given their role in both causing the occlusion and driving general vascular risk; regular ophthalmology follow-up to monitor for and treat complications as they develop.

## Retinal Artery Occlusion

**D:** interruption of the normal arterial supply to the retinal tissue — equivalent to a stroke, secondary to thromboembolism from atherosclerosis or arteritis (e.g. GCA).

**S/Smx:** sudden, painless, unilateral visual loss; relative afferent pupillary defect; 'cherry red' spot on a pale retina.

**Ix:** fundoscopy (*why:* the key diagnostic finding; *what:* the classic cherry-red spot — a pale, oedematous retina with the fovea appearing relatively red due to the underlying choroidal circulation showing through the thinner foveal retina). Given this is genuinely equivalent to a retinal stroke, the same urgent stroke-style work-up applies once suspected — screening for the embolic/arteritic source (carotid imaging, ECG/echo for cardioembolic sources, ESR/CRP and temporal artery assessment if GCA is a possibility given the very different urgent Mx pathway — see [[04_Neurology]] Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis), covered under the headache section there) is essential, not optional, given the systemic vascular risk this reveals.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** this is an ophthalmic and vascular emergency — treat as a stroke-equivalent event; intra-arterial thrombolysis has been used but with mixed results and is not routinely recommended; if GCA is suspected as the cause, do not delay high-dose steroids pending biopsy confirmation, given the risk of bilateral involvement/other vascular complications (see [[04_Neurology]] Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis) for the urgency principle, which applies equally here).
- **Definitive:** treat underlying conditions (manage the embolic source, GCA if present).
- **Chronic/long-term:** secondary vascular risk factor management, given this event signals the same systemic vascular risk as any other embolic/atherosclerotic event.

**P:** poor — retinal tissue has very limited tolerance for ischaemia, so visual recovery is generally poor regardless of Mx, making the systemic vascular risk-factor identification arguably more clinically important than the (limited) treatment options for the eye itself.

## Anterior Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy (AION)

90–95% non-arteritic, 5% arteritic (giant cell arteritis).

**Giant Cell Arteritis-related AION:** primarily due to occlusion of the posterior ciliary artery (branch of the ophthalmic artery). Temporary visual loss = amaurosis fugax. Permanent visual loss is the most feared complication of temporal arteritis and may develop suddenly. Diplopia may also result from involvement of any part of the oculomotor system (e.g. cranial nerves). Associated with headache (85%), jaw claudication (65%), temporal artery tenderness, PMR features (50%), systemic symptoms.

**Ix:** ESR/CRP and temporal artery biopsy for the arteritic (GCA) subtype (*why:* confirms/excludes GCA as the cause, which fundamentally changes both urgency and treatment duration compared to non-arteritic AION; *what:* markedly elevated inflammatory markers support GCA — see [[04_Neurology]] Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis) for the fuller Ix detail already built there, not repeated here). Non-arteritic AION is more of a diagnosis of exclusion based on the clinical picture and absence of GCA features/markers.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (arteritic/GCA-related):** high-dose steroids started immediately on clinical suspicion — do not wait for biopsy confirmation, given the risk of rapid, irreversible bilateral visual loss.
- **Chronic/long-term:** taper over 4–6 weeks (for GCA-related AION) — though note that GCA more broadly often requires a much longer overall steroid course than this specific taper figure suggests; see [[04_Neurology]] Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis) for the broader GCA Mx context.
- **Non-arteritic AION:** no proven effective treatment beyond addressing vascular risk factors — the emphasis is on cardiovascular risk factor management rather than a specific ophthalmic intervention, given the ischaemic/vascular mechanism.

## Retinal Migraine

**D:** transient monocular scotoma or loss of vision that is accompanied or followed by a headache within 60 minutes of visual symptom onset.

**S/Smx:** flashing lights; scintillating scotoma with fortification spectrum (expanding zigzag lines).

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis, but genuinely a **diagnosis of exclusion** — given retinal migraine is rare and its presentation overlaps with more dangerous causes of transient monocular visual loss (amaurosis fugax/TIA, GCA), these must be actively excluded first (carotid imaging, cardiac source screening, ESR/CRP for GCA) rather than assuming migraine in a patient with vascular risk factors or age >50.

**Mx:** as for migraine generally (see [[04_Neurology]] Migraine for the full Mx detail, not repeated here) — the distinguishing clinical challenge is diagnostic (excluding vascular causes), not therapeutic.

## Papilloedema

**D:** optic disc swelling caused by increased intracranial pressure.

**A:** space-occupying lesions (neoplastic, vascular), malignant hypertension, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, hydrocephalus, hypercapnia, hypoparathyroidism, hypocalcaemia, vitamin A toxicity

**S/Smx:** almost always bilateral — venous engorgement (usually the first sign), loss of venous pulsation (though many normal patients lack pulsation normally), blurring of the optic disc margin, elevation of the optic disc, loss of the optic cup, Paton's lines (concentric/radial retinal lines cascading from the optic disc).

**Ix:** fundoscopy is the key examination finding papilloedema itself, but the essential next step is identifying the cause — urgent neuroimaging (CT/MRI) to exclude a space-occupying lesion, and LP (only once imaging has excluded a mass lesion, given the herniation risk) if idiopathic intracranial hypertension is suspected — see [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP and Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension for the full Ix/Mx pathway, not repeated here, since papilloedema is a sign of that underlying process rather than a standalone disease.

**Mx:** entirely dependent on the underlying cause identified above — this section describes the sign, not a separate treatment pathway; see [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP for the Mx of the underlying process (treating the cause, head elevation, osmotic therapy, CSF diversion as appropriate).

## Optic Neuritis

**D:** inflammation of the optic nerve — most commonly idiopathic, a primary demyelinating disease in isolation or secondary to multiple sclerosis.

**S/Smx:** unilateral decreased visual acuity over hours-days; poor colour discrimination ('red desaturation'); pain worse on eye movement; RAPD; central scotoma.

**Ix:** MRI brain, orbits with contrast (*why:* the key diagnostic and prognostic test — confirms the diagnosis and, critically, the number of white-matter lesions present is the strongest predictor of subsequent MS risk (see Prognosis below); *what:* may show optic nerve enhancement and white-matter lesions elsewhere in the brain, feeding directly into the MS-risk stratification below).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** high-dose steroids, taper over 4–6 weeks — shortens the duration of the acute episode though doesn't clearly alter long-term visual outcome (a similar pattern to the general principle noted for MS relapse Mx in [[04_Neurology]] Multiple Sclerosis (MS)).
- **Chronic/long-term:** given the significant MS-conversion risk with multiple white-matter lesions (below), ongoing neurology follow-up and monitoring is warranted rather than treating this as a purely ophthalmic, self-contained event — see [[04_Neurology]] Multiple Sclerosis (MS) for the disease-level content this may evolve into.

**P:** if >3 white-matter lesions present, 5-year risk of developing multiple sclerosis is ~50%.

## Colour Vision and Blurred Vision — DDx

> [!info] DDx of loss of colour vision (non-congenital)
> Optic neuritis/MS, optic nerve ischaemia, glaucoma, ARMD, retinitis pigmentosa, thyroid eye disease, DM (diabetic retinopathy), drugs (chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, phenytoin, sildenafil, digoxin)

> [!info] DDx of blurred vision
> Refractive error (e.g. myopia), cataracts, retinal detachment, ARMD, acute angle closure glaucoma, optic neuritis, amaurosis fugax

**Assessment:** Snellen chart — use pinhole occluders (if blurring improves, likely a refractive error); visual fields; fundoscopy.

## Glaucoma

**D:** group of conditions causing optic nerve damage and vision loss.

### Angle-Closure Glaucoma

**D:** condition resulting from closure of the anterior-chamber angle, resulting in elevation of intraocular pressure.
- Acute ACG — relatively sudden blockage of the trabecular meshwork by the iris, via the pupillary block mechanism (iris pushed upward, blocking outflow)
- Chronic ACG — adhesional blockage

> [!note] For open-angle glaucoma, see the Age-Related Conditions section below.

**R:** hypermetropia (far-sighted), pupillary dilatation, lens growth associated with age, F>M, previous ACG

**S/Smx:** severe pain (ocular or headache); ↓visual acuity; symptoms worse with mydriasis (e.g. watching TV in a dark room); hard, red eye; haloes around lights; semi-dilated non-reacting pupil; corneal oedema → dull/hazy cornea; ± systemic upset (nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain)

**Dx:** clinical — immediate referral for a same-day ophthalmology appointment.

**Ix:** gonioscopy (*why:* directly visualises the anterior chamber angle, the definitive test confirming angle closure as the mechanism; *what:* unable to see the trabecular meshwork, confirming a closed angle). Slit-lamp exam (*why:* assesses the anterior segment findings supporting the diagnosis; *what:* corneal oedema, shallow anterior chamber, semi-dilated pupil). Automatic static perimetry (*why:* assesses for visual field loss from optic nerve damage, relevant to monitoring and to distinguishing acute from chronic/pre-existing damage; *what:* may show field loss if damage has already occurred).

**Mx — medical:**
> [!info] Verified — acetazolamide 500mg stat dosing for acute angle-closure glaucoma is consistent across international sources (US, UK, and general ophthalmic emergency literature) and reflects the drug's pharmacology rather than a jurisdiction-specific protocol; no correction needed. The follow-on maintenance dosing varies somewhat by source (250mg q6–8h is a reasonable range) — treat the exact interval as illustrative rather than a single fixed number.
- Combination eye drops: pilocarpine + timolol + apraclonidine
- IV acetazolamide 500mg stat, then 250mg/8h PO/IV
- ± topical steroids

**Mx — surgical (definitive):**
- Laser peripheral iridotomy — tiny hole in the iris to allow aqueous humour to flow out
- The unaffected eye is also treated (prophylactically)

## Anterior Uveitis

**D:** irritation of the anterior uvea (iris and ciliary body).

**R:** other inflammatory diseases, HLA-B27 positivity, ocular trauma

**Associated with:** ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, IBD (UC, Crohn's), Behçet's disease, sarcoidosis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, HSV, VZV, TB, syphilis, HIV, tarantula hairs (literally).

**S/Smx:** acute onset (hours-days) red eye; ocular discomfort and pain; pupil may be small ± oval/irregular (sphincter muscle contraction); photophobia (often intense); blurred vision; lacrimation; ciliary flush (ring of red spreading outwards); hypopyon (pus and inflammatory cells in the anterior chamber → visible fluid level); visual acuity initially normal, then impaired.

**Hx:** ask about systemic disease.

**Dx:** clinical.

**Ix:** slit-lamp exam (*why:* the key diagnostic test — directly visualises the anterior chamber inflammation (cells and flare), confirming the diagnosis and grading severity; *what:* cells and flare in the anterior chamber, hypopyon if severe; monitored serially since inflammation can recur, so follow-up is critical, not just a one-off diagnostic test).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** urgent review by ophthalmology; cycloplegics (dilate the pupil, relieving pain and photophobia, and importantly preventing posterior synechiae — adhesions between the iris and lens that can form if the pupil is left constricted during active inflammation — e.g. atropine, cyclopentolate); steroid eye drops to control the inflammation itself.
- **Chronic/long-term:** given the strong systemic disease associations (see above), a first presentation — especially if recurrent or bilateral — warrants screening for an underlying systemic cause (HLA-B27-associated spondyloarthropathy, IBD, sarcoidosis) rather than treating each episode as an isolated ophthalmic event; recurrence risk means patients need clear safety-netting on symptoms to watch for.

### Other types of uveitis
- **Intermediate:** vitreous chamber
- **Posterior:** choroid ± retina
- **Panuveitis:** all
- **S/Smx:** painless, blurry vision/floaters, eye not red
- **Ix:** fundus fluorescein, indocyanine green angiography (*why:* these posterior segments aren't visible on slit-lamp alone the way the anterior chamber is, so angiography is needed to characterise inflammation and any associated vascular changes; *what:* identifies patterns of choroidal/retinal inflammation and leakage)
- **Mx:** systemic or intraocular therapies (e.g. adalimumab, steroid implants) — note the shift from topical therapy (sufficient for anterior uveitis) to systemic/intraocular therapy here, reflecting that topical drops don't penetrate adequately to treat posterior segment inflammation
- **Complications:** vision loss

## Episcleritis and Scleritis

**D:** inflammation of the episclera/sclera.

**Associated with:** IBD (UC, Crohn's), RA. F>M.

**S/Smx:** red eye (~30–50% bilateral). Pain — episcleritis is not very painful; scleritis causes a constant, severe dull ache that "bores" into the eye, can wake the patient at night, with painful eye movements ± headache. ± watering and photophobia. Scleritis causes gradual vision loss.

**Ix:** slit-lamp exam (*why:* the primary diagnostic examination for both conditions, and enables the phenylephrine blanching test above; *what:* assesses vessel pattern/mobility and depth of inflammation). Given the systemic disease association, first presentation of scleritis specifically (more so than episcleritis, given its stronger systemic association and worse prognosis) warrants screening for an underlying cause (IBD, RA, vasculitis) rather than treating as an isolated ophthalmic event.

**Mx of scleritis:**
- **Immediate/acute:** urgent referral to an eye emergency department / ophthalmology unit.
- **Definitive:** non-necrotising anterior scleritis — PO NSAIDs ± PO high-dose prednisolone; posterior/necrotising scleritis — systemic immunosuppression (cyclophosphamide or rituximab), reflecting the more severe, sight-threatening nature of this subtype; recalcitrant disease — infliximab; globe perforation — emergency surgery.
- **Chronic/long-term:** investigate and manage the underlying systemic disease if identified, since scleritis often reflects active systemic autoimmune disease rather than being a purely localised eye problem.

**Mx of episcleritis:** conservative ± artificial tears — reflecting its generally benign, self-limiting course compared to scleritis.

**P:** vision loss highly likely in necrotising scleritis or globe perforation.

## Keratitis

**D:** inflammation of the cornea. Infectious keratitis refers to microbial invasion of the cornea causing inflammation and damage to the corneal epithelium, stroma, or endothelium.

**R:** contact lenses, corneal trauma, abrasion/erosion (+ recurrent), PMH of autoimmune disease, immunosuppression

**Aetiology — infectious:**
- Bacterial — typically *S. aureus*; *P. aeruginosa* in contact lens wearers
- Fungal
- Amoebic (acanthamoebic keratitis) — 5% of cases, increased risk with soil or contaminated water exposure; pain out of proportion to findings
- Parasitic: onchocercal keratitis ('river blindness')
- Viral — herpes simplex keratitis (HSV1)

**Aetiology — others (rare):** environmental (photokeratitis/"welder's arc eye"), exposure keratitis, contact lens acute red eye (CLARE)

**S/Smx:** red eye — pain and erythema; photophobia; ± hypopyon (white area on the cornea indicating a collection of white cells in corneal tissue); foreign body/gritty sensation.

**Ix:** corneal scraping — microscope slide, MC&S (*why:* identifies the causative organism, essential given the very different treatment for bacterial vs fungal vs amoebic vs viral causes above, and given the significant sight-threatening potential of getting empirical therapy wrong (e.g. missing acanthamoeba); *what:* identifies the specific organism, guiding targeted rather than purely empirical therapy). Slit lamp exam (*why:* assesses the depth and extent of corneal involvement, particularly important for contact lens wearers given the higher risk of severe/atypical organisms in this group; *what:* characterises the corneal lesion — depth, size, associated anterior chamber reaction).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** same-day referral to eye specialist; stop wearing contact lenses; topical antibiotics (typically quinolones) started empirically pending culture results, given the sight-threatening potential of delayed treatment; cycloplegic for pain relief (e.g. cyclopentolate).
- **Definitive:** targeted therapy once the causative organism is identified from corneal scraping — antifungals for fungal keratitis, specific anti-amoebic therapy for acanthamoeba (a notably difficult-to-treat organism, hence the emphasis on prevention via contact lens hygiene above), aciclovir-based therapy for HSV keratitis.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address modifiable risk factors (contact lens hygiene — see Contact Lens Care below) to prevent recurrence, particularly relevant given how much of the risk factor profile is behaviour-modifiable.

**P:** corneal scarring, perforation, endophthalmitis, visual loss.

## Conjunctivitis

**D:** inflammation of the lining of the eyelids and eyeball.

**Aetiology:** allergic; infectious (bacterial or viral)

**R:** exposure to an infected person (or public areas with increased risk), environmental irritants or allergen exposure, other infection (+ prior HSV1 infection), atopy, contact lens use, trauma, oculogenital spread, vaginal delivery in neonates

**S/Smx of infectious conjunctivitis:** bacterial — purulent discharge, eyes stuck together; viral — serous discharge, recent URTI, preauricular lymphadenopathy

**Ix:** cultures only needed if gonorrhoea/chlamydia suspected, neonatal, or recurrent/refractory (*why:* most infectious conjunctivitis is a clinical diagnosis, and culture is reserved for specific higher-risk scenarios where identifying the organism changes Mx — gonococcal/chlamydial conjunctivitis carries a risk of rapid corneal perforation and needs specific systemic (not just topical) treatment, and neonatal conjunctivitis needs urgent identification given the risk of severe complications; *what:* identifies the causative organism in these specific higher-risk presentations, not routinely needed otherwise).

> [!info] Verified against Australian Therapeutic Guidelines (eTG) and a cross-sectional analysis of Australian GP prescribing (Journal of Primary Health Care), Aug 2026 — **chloramphenicol is confirmed as the correct first-line agent in Australia too** (95.8% of surveyed Australian GP registrars prescribe it, consistent with eTG recommendations — this is genuinely one of the few areas where AU and UK practice converge rather than diverge), but the alternative choice needs correcting.
> **Mx of infectious conjunctivitis:** conservative — watch and wait 1–2 weeks; offer topical antibiotics (e.g. chloramphenicol eye drops q2–3h; ointment qds) — **fusidic acid eye drops are not available in Australia**, so this cannot be used as the pregnancy-safe alternative the way UK sources suggest; check current eTG for the Australian-specific alternative in pregnancy if chloramphenicol is contraindicated/declined, rather than reaching for fusidic acid. For contact lens users: do not wear lenses, topical fluorescein to identify corneal staining. Advise not sharing towels, proper handwashing. School exclusion not necessary.

**S/Smx of allergic conjunctivitis:** bilateral conjunctival erythema, chemosis, and itch; ± swollen eyelids; generally in a patient with PMH of atopy, spring/summer timing, or environmental exposure.

**Mx of allergic conjunctivitis:** topical or PO antihistamines first-line; topical mast-cell stabilisers (e.g. sodium cromoglicate, nedocromil) second-line. Advice: remove allergens, cold compresses, artificial tears, PO antihistamines, nasal steroid sprays.

## Blepharitis

**D:** inflammation of the eyelid margin.
- Posterior blepharitis: meibomian gland dysfunction (common)
- Anterior: seborrhoeic dermatitis or staphylococcal infection

**S/Smx:** typically bilateral; grittiness and discomfort especially around eyelid margins; ± sticky in the morning; ± red and swollen eyelid margins (especially in staph infection); styes and chalazions common; secondary conjunctivitis may occur.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (symptomatic relief):** hot compresses 10 min bd; lid hygiene — cotton wool buds dipped in boiled water + baby shampoo, or a teaspoon of sodium bicarbonate in a cup of cooled water; ± artificial tears if dry eye or abnormal tear film.
- **Chronic/long-term:** since blepharitis is typically a chronic, relapsing-remitting condition rather than a one-off infection, ongoing lid hygiene as a maintenance routine (not just during flares) is usually needed to keep symptoms controlled, particularly for the meibomian-gland-dysfunction (posterior) subtype.

## Blepharospasm

**D:** dystonia of the upper eyelids — involuntary contraction of the orbicularis oculi, often in response to ocular pain. Focal dystonia = repetitive blepharospasm.

**A:** idiopathic, drugs (neuroleptics, PD, progressive supranuclear palsy, paraneoplastic)

**S/Smx:** F>M (3:1); often preceded by exaggerated blinking ± other dystonias; starts unilateral → then bilateral; patients may develop coping mechanisms (e.g. pulling eyelids).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (symptomatic control):** botulinum neurotoxin injection every 3 months (MOA: flaccid paralysis by inhibiting ACh release) — the mainstay treatment, providing temporary relief that needs repeating given the reversible mechanism.
- **Adjunctive:** anticholinergics (e.g. trihexyphenidyl); dopamine agonists (e.g. levodopa); supportive treatment — wearing goggles (protects the eyes and can reduce triggering stimuli in photophobic patients).
- **Chronic/long-term:** since this is typically a chronic condition without a cure, ongoing repeat botulinum toxin injections form the backbone of long-term management rather than a one-off treatment.

## Stye (Hordeolum) and Chalazion

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Eyelid disorders," Ophthalmology category, Low yield) — the **only** MISSING candidate the Ophthalmology cross-check produced, and a genuine one: blepharitis and entropion/ectropion are both built below and above, but the two commonest eyelid lumps were absent entirely. Low yield, so built compactly.

| | **Stye (hordeolum)** | **Chalazion** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | **Acute infection** of an eyelash follicle (external) or a meibomian gland (internal) | **Sterile granuloma** from a blocked meibomian gland — the blockage, not an infection |
| **S/Smx** | Painful, red, tender lump at the lid margin; points and often discharges | **Painless** (or mildly uncomfortable), firm, rubbery lump in the lid *body* rather than at the margin |
| **Mx** | Warm compresses; lid hygiene. Antibiotics are **not** routinely needed — topical only if there is associated conjunctivitis or spreading infection | Warm compresses and massage; most resolve over weeks to months. Persistent or cosmetically troubling lesions → ophthalmology for incision and curettage |

> [!tip] **The distinction that decides management is pain and position.** Painful and at the margin → stye, infective, settles quickly. Painless and in the lid body → chalazion, a blocked gland, slow. The common error is treating a chalazion with antibiotics, which does nothing because there is no infection to treat.

> [!danger] **The one that is not either of these.** A recurrent lesion **at the same site**, one that distorts the lid margin, or one with **loss of eyelashes**, needs specialist assessment for **sebaceous gland carcinoma**, which characteristically masquerades as a recurrent chalazion. Recurrence at the same spot is the flag — not the first episode.

**Cross-reference:** see Blepharitis above for the chronic lid-margin inflammation that predisposes to both, not repeated here.

**Ectropion:** out-turning of (usually the lower) eyelid → watering ± exposure keratitis. Associated with old age, facial palsy. Mx: plastic surgery, surgical correction with implant.

## Hordeolum (Stye)

**Hordeolum externum:** abscess/infection in a lash follicle — glands of Moll (sweat gland) or glands of Zeis (sebum-producing, attached directly to follicles). Points outwards ("externum"). Mx: warm compress 5–10 min several times daily until it resolves.

**Hordeolum internum:** abscess/infection in a Meibomian gland. Leaves a residual swelling called a chalazion (Meibomian cyst) — firm, painless lump in the eyelid. Points inward and opens to the conjunctiva; less local reaction. Mx: chalazion needs incision and curettage under local anaesthesia.

## Ophthalmic Shingles

**D:** Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO). VZV usually affects children (chickenpox), then lies dormant, usually reactivated >60yo. Most common in thoracic nerves (50%), then CN V1 (20%).

**S/Smx:** pain and neuralgia in V1 distribution; vesicular rash (blistering, inflamed) around the eye ± eye involvement. Hutchinson's sign — rash on the tip or side of the nose, a strong risk factor for ocular involvement. Ocular involvement: corneal signs ± iritis.

**Ix/referral:** normal visual acuity and corneal appearance are reassuring. Refer same-day if pain, redness, change in vision, or Hutchinson's sign.

**Mx:** oral antiviral for 7–10 days (e.g. famciclovir, aciclovir) — start within 72h; IV if severe infection or immunosuppressed (reduces complications but will not prevent post-herpetic neuralgia); steroid eye drops for secondary inflammation; pregnant women with no VZV immunity — isolate until vesicles dry up.

**Complications:** conjunctivitis, keratitis, episcleritis, anterior uveitis, ptosis; post-herpetic neuralgia (up to 45%, months to years, associated with ↑age, ocular involvement, severe rashes — Mx with TCAs, gabapentinoids; cited as a common cause of suicide in patients with chronic pain >70yo); Ramsay Hunt syndrome.

> [!info] General (non-ophthalmic) varicella zoster/chickenpox and shingles content is now built in [[09_07_Dermatology_-_Chickenpox__Shingles__Pityriasis_Rosea__Hidradenitis_Suppurativa]] Varicella zoster virus (VZV) / chickenpox and Herpes zoster / shingles, not repeated here — includes the AU-verified antiviral timing, VZIG post-exposure prophylaxis, and pregnancy-specific management this ophthalmic entry's Mx above draws on.

## Other Issues with the External Eye

**Ptosis:** abnormally low upper lid when the patient looks forward. A: congenital (absent nerve to levator muscle or poorly developed levator), myogenic, CNS. Mx: surgical if the pupil is covered in congenital ptosis.

**Pseudoptosis:** due to abnormal position or size of the globe.

**Dermatochalasis:** excess eyelid skin hanging over the eye; may obstruct sight.

**Lagophthalmos:** inability to close the eyelids. A: exophthalmos (e.g. thyroid eye disease), mechanical impairment of lid movement (e.g. injury, leprosy, CN VII palsy). Can lead to exposure keratopathy and corneal ulcers. Mx: lubricate eyes regularly, tape eyes shut at night ± tarsorrhaphy (stitching eyelids together).

## Open-Angle Glaucoma

**D:** condition resulting from an anatomically open angle but obstructed and slowed drainage system outflow. The trabecular network functionally causes increased resistance to aqueous humour outflow → increased IOP.

**R:** IOP >23, >50yo, family history, Afro-Caribbean ethnicity, myopia, HTN, DM, steroid use

**S/Smx:** peripheral visual field loss (nasal scotomas progressing to tunnel vision); decreased visual acuity.

> [!info] Fundoscopy signs
> Optic disc cupping (cup-to-disc ratio >0.7; loss of disc substance widens/deepens the optic cup); optic disc pallor (secondary to optic atrophy); bayonetting of vessels (vessels appear to break as they disappear into the deep cup and re-appear at the base); cup notching (usually inferior, where vessels enter the disc); disc haemorrhages

**Dx:** usually found via screening in patients with risk factors.

**Ix:** automated perimetry (*why:* assesses visual field loss, the functional consequence of the optic nerve damage and a key monitoring tool for disease progression; *what:* peripheral field loss progressing to tunnel vision in established disease). Slit-lamp exam with pupil dilatation (*why:* allows detailed assessment of the optic disc for the specific fundoscopy signs above; *what:* cupping, pallor, notching as described). Applanation tonometry (*why:* directly measures IOP, the modifiable risk factor and treatment target; *what:* typically elevated, though open-angle glaucoma can occur at "normal" IOP — normal-tension glaucoma — so a normal IOP doesn't exclude the diagnosis). Central corneal thickness measurement (*why:* thinner corneas can cause tonometry to underestimate true IOP, and corneal thickness is itself an independent risk factor for progression; *what:* used to correct/interpret the tonometry reading and to stratify future risk). Gonioscopy (*why:* confirms the angle is genuinely open, distinguishing this from angle-closure glaucoma, since Mx differs significantly; *what:* open angle with visible trabecular meshwork — the opposite finding to angle-closure glaucoma above).

**Mx:**
> [!info] Verified against Optometry Australia's Glaucoma Clinical Practice Guide, Aug 2026 — the stepwise drug protocol is essentially internationally standard and not meaningfully UK-specific; the Australian guide confirms the same first-choice/second-line structure, with one AU-specific process detail added below.
- **Definitive (no acute tier — chronic progressive disease managed via stepwise drug escalation, not an emergency):** PGA eye drops (e.g. latanoprost) or β-blockers as first choice; switch (rather than simply add) if the first agent alone is inadequate or poorly tolerated; second-line — acetazolamide, sympathomimetic eye drops, or add a second topical agent + surgical/laser (trabeculectomy). **Australian-specific process point:** if target IOP is not achieved, referral to an ophthalmologist for formal management is recommended within 4 months — a specific, guideline-stated timeframe worth knowing rather than an open-ended "refer if not controlled."
- **Chronic/long-term:** regular reassessment to check progression (visual fields, IOP, optic disc appearance over time) — since glaucomatous damage is irreversible, the goal of treatment is halting further progression rather than reversing existing damage, which shapes how "success" is assessed and communicated to patients.

## Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)

**D:** a potentially progressive maculopathy.

**Dry AMD (90%):** drusen — yellow round spots in Bruch's membrane.

**Wet AMD (10%):** exudative or neovascular macular degeneration — 'late' AMD; choroidal neovascularisation; leakage of serous fluid and blood can result in rapid vision loss; worst prognosis.

**R:** ↑age, smoking, family history

**S/Smx:** subacute reduction in visual acuity, especially for near-field objects; difficulties in dark adaptation with overall night vision deterioration; fluctuations in visual disturbance day-to-day; photopsia (flickering/flashing lights) + glare around objects; distortion of line perception on Amsler grid testing; fundoscopy — drusen, macular scar, well-demarcated red patches (intra-retinal or sub-retinal fluid leakage or haemorrhage).

**Ix:** OCT to visualise the macula (*why:* the key modern imaging test, directly visualising retinal layers and identifying fluid/oedema characteristic of wet AMD; *what:* identifies sub-/intra-retinal fluid in wet AMD, drusen and retinal thinning in dry AMD). Slit-lamp microscopy (*why:* assesses for pigmentary, exudative, or haemorrhagic retinal changes as part of the clinical exam; *what:* may show these changes directly). Colour fundus photography (*why:* provides an objective baseline to track progression over time, particularly useful for monitoring dry AMD; *what:* documents drusen distribution/macular appearance for future comparison). Fluorescein angiography if neovascular AMD suspected (*why:* identifies and characterises the choroidal neovascular membrane, directly guiding anti-VEGF treatment decisions; *what:* leakage pattern confirms and localises active neovascularisation). Indocyanine green angiography (*why:* visualises the choroidal circulation specifically, complementing fluorescein angiography particularly for certain neovascular patterns; *what:* identifies choroidal vascular abnormalities not always well seen on fluorescein angiography alone).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (wet AMD specifically):** **anti-VEGF** started within 2 months of diagnosis, given by **intravitreal injection** roughly every 4 weeks initially — genuinely time-sensitive given the rapid vision loss wet AMD can cause if untreated.

> [!danger] **Route correction (M9, 2026-08-29): anti-VEGF for wet AMD is an intravitreal *injection*, not an infusion.** The entry previously said "administered every 4 weeks via infusion". Bevacizumab is given intravenously in oncology, which is likely where the error came from — but in the eye the drug is injected directly into the vitreous, and describing it as an infusion would have a candidate explain the wrong procedure to a patient facing a needle into their eye.
>
> **VEGF = vascular endothelial growth factor**, and the mechanism is the whole reason this treatment works: wet AMD *is* abnormal choroidal neovascularisation, VEGF is the signal driving that new vessel growth, so blocking VEGF addresses the cause rather than the consequence. That is also why it must be repeated — the drug is cleared and the stimulus returns.
>
> **In Australian practice, ranibizumab and aflibercept are the PBS-listed intravitreal agents; bevacizumab is used off-label** and is not the default example to quote. See `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B40**.
- **Definitive/chronic (dry AMD):** zinc + multivitamins (decreases progression) — a disease-modifying rather than curative intervention, aimed at slowing rather than reversing the degenerative process.
- **Chronic/long-term:** laser photocoagulation as an alternative/adjunct option in select cases.

> [!danger] Warn patients that laser photocoagulation can cause acute visual loss.

## Ametropia (Refractive Error)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Ametropia," Low yield) — genuinely absent from the source notes despite being fundamental background for understanding several other conditions in this file (amblyopia, strabismus, presbyopia).

**D:** a refractive error — the eye's optical system fails to focus light precisely on the retina, so vision is blurred without correction. "Ametropia" is the umbrella term for any refractive error; "emmetropia" is the normal, correctly-focusing state.

**Types:**
- **Myopia (short-sightedness):** the eye is too long (or the cornea/lens too powerful) for its focal length, so light focuses in front of the retina — distant objects appear blurred, near objects remain clear. Onset typically in childhood/adolescence, often progressive during growth.
- **Hyperopia (long-sightedness):** the eye is too short (or the cornea/lens too weak), so light focuses behind the retina — near objects are more blurred than distant ones, though the eye's accommodation (lens-shape adjustment) can compensate for mild degrees, especially in younger patients, meaning hyperopia can be under-recognised until accommodation reserve declines with age.
- **Astigmatism:** the cornea (or occasionally lens) is not perfectly spherical, so light focuses at multiple points rather than one — vision is blurred or distorted at all distances, often with specific difficulty on fine detail. Frequently coexists with myopia or hyperopia rather than occurring in isolation.
- **Presbyopia:** an age-related decline in the lens's ability to accommodate (change shape to focus on near objects), typically becoming symptomatic from the mid-40s onward — this is a distinct, near-universal process from the other refractive errors above, reflecting lens stiffening with age rather than a mismatch in eye length/corneal power, and it affects everyone eventually regardless of their baseline refractive status.

**S/Smx:** blurred vision at a characteristic distance per the type above; squinting (which narrows the aperture and can transiently sharpen focus, a common compensatory behaviour, especially in myopia); headaches or eye strain, particularly with prolonged near work in uncorrected hyperopia or presbyopia; in children, uncorrected significant refractive error (especially if asymmetric between the two eyes) is a recognised cause of amblyopia — see Amblyopia elsewhere in this file for the fuller detail on this important paediatric consequence, not repeated here.

**Ix:** visual acuity testing (Snellen chart) (*why:* the basic screening test quantifying the degree of uncorrected vision impairment; *what:* reduced acuity that improves with pinhole testing supports a refractive cause rather than a structural/pathological one — a useful bedside distinguishing test, since a pinhole improves refractive blur but doesn't meaningfully improve vision loss from retinal, optic nerve, or media opacity pathology). Refraction (subjective or autorefraction) (*why:* the definitive test quantifying the specific type and degree of refractive error, performed by an optometrist/ophthalmologist, directly prescribing the correction needed; *what:* expressed in dioptres — the specific myopic, hyperopic, or astigmatic correction required).

**Mx:**
- **Corrective (first-line for all types):** spectacles or contact lenses, prescribed to the specific refractive error identified above.
- **Definitive/elective:** refractive surgery (e.g. LASIK, PRK) is an option for suitable adult candidates with stable refraction who wish to reduce dependence on glasses/contacts — not a first-line necessity, but a recognised elective option.
- **Presbyopia specifically:** reading glasses, bifocals/varifocals, or multifocal contact lenses — since presbyopia is a progressive, universal ageing change rather than a fixed error, the correction typically needs periodic updating as accommodation continues to decline.

## Retinitis Pigmentosa

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Retinitis pigmentosa," Low yield) — previously only a one-line mention within the chronic vision loss differential; given a brief dedicated entry given its status as a classic, distinctive exam topic despite being individually rare.

**D:** a group of inherited retinal dystrophies causing progressive degeneration of photoreceptors, predominantly rods initially, then cones — the most common inherited retinal disease.

**A:** genetically heterogeneous — can be inherited in autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked patterns (X-linked tends to be the most severe); may occur in isolation or as part of a recognised syndrome (e.g. Usher syndrome — retinitis pigmentosa with sensorineural hearing loss, a combination worth specifically knowing given how often it's tested).

**S/Smx:** the classic pattern reflects the rod-before-cone degeneration sequence —
- **Early:** night blindness (nyctalopia) — often the first symptom, since rods (responsible for low-light vision) are affected first, sometimes noticed years before other symptoms
- **Progressive:** peripheral visual field loss ("tunnel vision") — as peripheral rod-dominant retina degenerates before the central cone-dominant macula
- **Late:** central vision loss — once cone degeneration also becomes significant, central/reading vision is eventually affected too, though this typically occurs later in the disease course than the peripheral/night-vision symptoms above

**Ix:** fundoscopy (*why:* the classic bedside finding directly visualises the retinal changes, though presentation can be subtle in early disease; *what:* the classic triad — **bone-spicule pigmentation** (dark, spiky pigment deposits in the peripheral retina, the hallmark finding giving the disease its name), **attenuated (narrowed) retinal blood vessels**, and **waxy pallor of the optic disc**). Electroretinogram (ERG) (*why:* the definitive functional test, directly measuring the retina's electrical response to light and detecting rod/cone dysfunction even before visible fundoscopic changes or symptoms develop — particularly useful for early or atypical presentations; *what:* reduced or absent rod (and later cone) responses). Visual field testing (*why:* objectively quantifies and tracks the progressive peripheral field loss described above; *what:* progressive peripheral constriction over time). Genetic testing and counselling (*why:* given the heterogeneous inheritance patterns above, genetic testing can clarify the specific subtype, inform family planning/counselling for relatives, and is increasingly relevant given emerging gene-specific therapies; *what:* identifies the specific causative gene where possible).

**Mx:**
- **Currently, no cure exists for most forms** — Mx is predominantly supportive and adaptive rather than disease-modifying for the majority of patients.
- **Supportive/adaptive:** low-vision aids and rehabilitation services; mobility training given the progressive visual field constriction; genetic counselling for the patient and at-risk relatives; regular monitoring for and management of complications (e.g. cystoid macular oedema, cataracts — both recognised associated complications worth screening for, since they may be independently treatable even when the underlying retinitis pigmentosa isn't).
- **Emerging/disease-specific:** gene therapy is now available for a small number of specific genetic subtypes (e.g. *RPE65*-associated retinitis pigmentosa) — a genuine and relatively recent therapeutic development worth being aware of, though it applies only to a minority of patients with the specific confirmed genetic cause the therapy targets, reinforcing the value of genetic testing above.

## Cataracts

**D:** opacification of the crystalline lens resulting from the normal ageing process. Leading cause of blindness worldwide; can be due to normal ageing or other causes.

**S/Smx:** clouded, blurred vision (painless); decreased distance judgment (reduced stereopsis); ± dazzle, ± monocular diplopia; increased difficulty driving in adults; in children — squinting, white pupil, nystagmus, amblyopia.

> [!info] Classification
> **Nuclear:** change in lens refractive index ('2nd sight' — patient becomes more myopic and regains reading vision); common with increasing age.
> **Cortical:** wedge-shaped opacities.
> **Posterior subcapsular:** progresses faster; causes the classic glare from bright sunlight and lights while driving at night.

**Ix:** slit-lamp exam (*why:* the key diagnostic examination, directly visualising the lens opacity and its pattern, which distinguishes the classification subtypes above; *what:* decreased red reflex, cataract visible — the specific pattern (nuclear, cortical, posterior subcapsular) is identified on this exam). Normal IOP (*why:* helps distinguish cataract from other causes of visual loss/red reflex change, particularly excluding a coexisting glaucoma process before surgical planning; *what:* IOP is not typically elevated by the cataract itself, so an elevated IOP should prompt consideration of a separate/coexisting glaucoma diagnosis).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute tier — this is a gradually progressive condition managed electively, not as an emergency):** glasses, brighter lights for mild/early disease not yet warranting surgery.
- **Surgical (definitive treatment):** if severe visual impairment, decreased QoL, or patient choice — lens removal by phacoemulsification (ultrasound to break up the cataract, then aspirated), followed by artificial lens insertion into the empty capsule. Usually a day procedure under local anaesthesia; 90–95% successful, 2% serious risk of complications.
- **Chronic/long-term:** monitor for and manage surgical complications as they arise (see below); patients should be counselled that some residual visual imperfection (e.g. glare) often remains even after otherwise successful surgery, so expectations should be set accordingly.

**Complications of surgery:** posterior capsule opacification (treat with YAG laser capsulotomy), retinal detachment, posterior capsule rupture, endophthalmitis (inflammation of aqueous and/or vitreous humour, can cause blindness). Astigmatism — toric lens. Eyes may not be completely normal after (glare often remains).

**Prevention:** sunglasses, reduce oxidative stress, stop smoking.

## Diabetic Retinopathy

**D:** chronic, progressive retinal disease secondary to hyperglycaemic microvascular damage and neurodegenerative changes. Most common cause of blindness in 35–65yo patients.

**R:** longer duration of DM, poor glycaemic control, ↑lipids, HTN. Proliferative retinopathy more common in T1DM.

**A/P:** hyperglycaemia → increased retinal blood flow and abnormal metabolism in retinal vessel walls → damage to endothelium and pericytes.
- Endothelial damage → increased vascular permeability → exudates
- Pericyte damage → microaneurysms
- Retinal ischaemia → increased growth factors → neovascularisation

**S/Smx:** asymptomatic initially; maculopathy causes decreased visual acuity; once proliferative retinopathy develops, blindness can come rapidly (50% blind within 5 years).

**Ix:** fundoscopy (*why:* the key screening and diagnostic examination — the basis for the staging system below, and the reason regular diabetic eye screening exists, since retinopathy is frequently asymptomatic until advanced; *what:* identifies the specific features (microaneurysms, haemorrhages, exudates, neovascularisation) that determine staging below).

> [!info] Non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) staging
> **Mild:** ≥1 microaneurysm
> **Moderate:** + blot haemorrhages + hard exudates (≈ maculopathy) + cotton wool spots (retinal infarcts)
> **Severe:** above findings in all 4 quadrants + venous beading in >2 quadrants + intraretinal microvascular abnormality (IRMA)
>
> **Proliferative:** retinal neovascularisation (can lead to vitreous haemorrhage), fibrous tissue anterior to retinal discs

**Mx:**
- **Chronic/long-term (all patients, regardless of retinopathy stage):** optimise glycaemic control, BP, and lipids + regular ophthalmology review — this systemic risk-factor control is the single most important intervention, since it addresses the underlying driver rather than just the ocular consequence.
- **Definitive (stage-specific):** maculopathy (decreased visual acuity) — intravitreal VEGF inhibitors; NPDR — if severe, consider panretinal laser photocoagulation; proliferative retinopathy — panretinal laser photocoagulation (warn patient 50% experience decreased visual fields due to scarring, decreased night vision), intravitreal VEGF inhibitors, vitreoretinal surgery if severe.
- **Immediate/acute:** patients presenting with sudden vision loss/floaters in the context of known proliferative retinopathy should be assessed urgently for vitreous haemorrhage (see Vitreous Haemorrhage above) or tractional retinal detachment, both recognised complications requiring prompt Mx.

## Hypertensive Retinopathy

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Ophthalmology category, Low yield) — not covered in the source notes despite being a natural pairing with Diabetic Retinopathy above, and a common OSCE fundoscopy-interpretation topic given how frequently hypertension coexists with diabetes.

**D:** retinal microvascular damage secondary to chronic and/or acute severe hypertension.

**A/P:** chronically elevated BP → arteriolar vasoconstriction and wall thickening (hyaline sclerosis) → progressive narrowing and, in severe/malignant hypertension, breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier → exudation, haemorrhage, and (in the most severe cases) disc swelling.

**S/Smx:** typically asymptomatic in the chronic, low-grade stages — found incidentally on fundoscopy; more severe/accelerated (malignant) hypertension can cause visual disturbance, and the retinal findings themselves are often a marker of the severity/duration of the underlying hypertension rather than a symptomatic process in their own right.

> [!info] Keith-Wagener-Barker grading (the classic staging system)
> **Grade 1:** mild generalised arteriolar narrowing/tortuosity — often subtle, may be within normal variation.
> **Grade 2:** more marked narrowing + arteriovenous nicking (AV nipping — the vein appears to be compressed where an arteriole crosses over it, a sign of the arteriole's thickened wall).
> **Grade 3:** grade 2 + flame haemorrhages, cotton wool spots, hard exudates (features overlapping in appearance with diabetic retinopathy above, though the distribution and clinical context differ).
> **Grade 4:** grade 3 + papilloedema — a marker of malignant/accelerated hypertension and a genuine emergency (see [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP and [[01_Cardiovascular]] for the hypertensive emergency Mx this finding should prompt, not repeated here).

**Ix:** fundoscopy (*why:* the key diagnostic examination, and the grading above is determined directly from the fundoscopic appearance; *what:* the specific findings by grade as above). Blood pressure measurement (*why:* confirms and quantifies the underlying hypertension driving the retinal changes; *what:* correlates with retinopathy severity, particularly for the higher grades).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (Grade 4/malignant hypertension with papilloedema):** this is a hypertensive emergency — urgent BP control per the malignant hypertension pathway (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] for the full Mx of hypertensive emergency, not repeated here) rather than a purely ophthalmic issue.
- **Definitive/chronic (Grades 1–3):** BP control is the treatment — there is no separate ophthalmic intervention for the retinal changes themselves, which regress (to varying degrees) with adequate long-term BP management.
- **Chronic/long-term:** the presence and grade of retinopathy is itself useful prognostic information about cumulative hypertensive end-organ damage, and can prompt closer cardiovascular risk assessment more broadly.

## Orbital and Peri-Orbital Cellulitis

**D:** infection within the orbital soft tissues with associated ocular dysfunction. Peri-orbital cellulitis (aka preseptal) — inflammation and infection of the superficial eyelid.

**R:** sinusitis, young age (7–12yo), M>F, lack of Hib vaccination in children, facial infection

**A:**
- Orbital — local spread of URTI, usually sinusitis; less often from injury/fracture, dental infection
- Peri-orbital — superficial spread from an inoculated site (e.g. insect bite)

> [!note] The bony orbit is thin with many nerves, vessels, and valveless veins — makes it easier for infection to spread.

**S/Smx of orbital cellulitis:** redness, swelling around the eye; severe ocular pain; visual disturbances; proptosis; ophthalmoplegia or pain with eye movements; eyelid oedema and ptosis; meningeal involvement (rare) — drowsiness ± nausea/vomiting.

**S/Smx of peri-orbital cellulitis:** redness, swelling around the eye only ± fever.

**Ix:** FBC (*why:* supports the inflammatory/infective diagnosis; *what:* ↑WCC, ↑CRP). CT with contrast (*why:* the key test distinguishing orbital from peri-orbital cellulitis — a distinction that fundamentally changes urgency and Mx, since orbital cellulitis risks vision loss and intracranial spread while peri-orbital does not; *what:* identifies orbital involvement (postseptal extension, abscess formation) vs disease confined to the preseptal soft tissues). Blood cultures and swabs (*why:* identifies the causative organism, particularly important if IV antibiotics and admission are required; *what:* may identify the causative organism, guiding antibiotic rationalisation).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** ophthalmology review ASAP — orbital cellulitis is a genuine emergency given the risk of vision loss, cavernous sinus thrombosis, and intracranial spread via the valveless venous system noted above; admit for IV antibiotics if orbital cellulitis.
- **Definitive:** peri-orbital cellulitis can be managed with PO antibiotics as an outpatient in most cases, reflecting its lower risk of serious complications compared to true orbital involvement.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address the underlying source where identifiable (e.g. sinusitis management) to prevent recurrence; ensure age-appropriate Hib vaccination status is reviewed given its role as a modifiable risk factor.

## Strabismus (Squint)

**D:** misalignment of the eyes; divided into concomitant and paralytic.

### Concomitant (Non-Paralytic) Squint

**R:** family history, prematurity, low birth weight, maternal smoking during pregnancy

**A/P:** poorly understood; arises due to imbalance in extraocular muscles — divided into convergent and divergent.

**S/Smx:**
- Convergent squint (esotropia) — one or both eyes turn inward
- Divergent squint (exotropia) — one eye turns outward ± intermittent

**Ix (screening):**
- Corneal reflection (*why:* a simple bedside screening test suitable for young children; *what:* bright light will not fall symmetrically on each cornea due to misalignment)
- Cover test (*why:* the definitive clinical test for confirming and characterising a squint; *what:* ask the child to focus on an object; cover one eye and observe movement of the uncovered eye; cover the other eye and repeat. If neither eye moves, both are fixating normally. If either eye moves, a squint is present — when the fixating eye is covered, the non-fixating (squinting) eye moves to fixate on the object)

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute tier — this is a developmental condition addressed on an elective, though time-sensitive, basis given the amblyopia risk below):** optical (spectacles); orthoptic (patching the good eye encourages use of the squinting eye); operation (resection and recession of rectus muscles — helps alignment and cosmesis; botulinum toxin in some patients).
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing orthoptic/ophthalmology follow-up given the genuine time-sensitivity of treatment during the critical period of visual development — this isn't a "treat whenever convenient" condition, since delayed treatment risks permanent amblyopia (see Complications below).

**Best results with:** early detection, conscientious and compliant treatment, optimal glasses.

**C:** if untreated, can lead to permanently damaged vision (amblyopia).

### Paralytic Squint

**D:** paralysis of extraocular muscles resulting in diplopia.

**S/Smx depends on the affected cranial nerve:**
- CN III, IV, and VI palsies cause diplopia, especially looking in the direction the affected muscle would have pulled
- **CN III palsy:** ptosis, proptosis, fixed pupil dilation, eye looks "down and out"
- **CN IV palsy** (superior oblique paralysed): ocular torticollis (head tilted to correct diplopia), slight upward eye deviation, difficulty looking down and in (difficulty walking down stairs)
- **CN VI palsy** (lateral rectus paralysed): diplopia in the horizontal plane, eye medially deviated, cannot look sideways on the affected side

**Causes of CN palsies:**
- All: trauma, stroke (ischaemic or haemorrhagic), tumour, congenital, DM
- CN III: cavernous sinus lesions, superior orbital fissure syndrome, posterior communicating artery aneurysm
- CN VI: raised ICP, trauma to skull base, multiple sclerosis

**Ix:** given the broad and sometimes serious differential above (particularly a posterior communicating artery aneurysm as a cause of CN III palsy — a genuine neurosurgical emergency, especially if the pupil is involved/dilated, as pupil-sparing vs pupil-involving CN III palsy is a critical distinguishing feature worth specifically assessing), urgent neuroimaging (CT/MRI ± angiography) is generally warranted for a new, acute paralytic squint rather than treating it as a purely ophthalmic finding — see [[04_Neurology]] Brain Bleeds (Subarachnoid Haemorrhage — posterior communicating artery aneurysm) and Brain Lesion Localisation for the fuller neurological context.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** urgent investigation of the underlying cause given the differential above (especially excluding an aneurysmal or other urgent structural cause) — this is not primarily an ophthalmic-only assessment.
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause where identified; temporary prism glasses or an eye patch can manage diplopia symptomatically while awaiting recovery or definitive Mx.
- **Chronic/long-term:** if recovery is incomplete, strabismus surgery may be considered once the underlying condition has stabilised and no further spontaneous recovery is expected.

### Amblyopia ("Lazy Eye")

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Ophthalmology category, Low yield) — previously only mentioned as a one-line complication of untreated squint above; given a proper entry here since it's a distinct diagnostic/management concept in its own right, not just a squint complication, and can arise from several different causes beyond strabismus.

**D:** reduced visual acuity in one (occasionally both) eyes caused by abnormal visual development in early childhood, **not correctable by glasses alone** and not explained by any structural eye abnormality on examination — the brain effectively "learns" to suppress or ignore input from the affected eye during the critical period of visual development.

**A/P:** any condition disrupting clear, equal visual input to both eyes during the critical period (roughly birth to age 7–8, with the earliest years being the most sensitive) can cause amblyopia via three main mechanisms:
- **Strabismic amblyopia** — from squint (see above) — the brain suppresses input from the misaligned eye to avoid diplopia, and that eye's visual pathway fails to develop normally.
- **Refractive/anisometropic amblyopia** — a significant difference in refractive error between the two eyes (or bilateral high refractive error) means one or both retinal images are chronically blurred, and the brain again fails to develop that pathway normally; this can occur with normally-aligned eyes, so a normal-looking eye exam does not exclude amblyopia.
- **Deprivation amblyopia** — anything physically blocking a clear image reaching the retina during the critical period (congenital cataract, ptosis covering the pupil, corneal opacity) — the most severe form, since the visual pathway receives essentially no useful input at all.

**S/Smx:** often asymptomatic to the child/parent, particularly if unilateral (the better eye compensates, so the child doesn't complain) — this is precisely why population vision screening programs exist, since amblyopia is frequently only detected on screening rather than presenting complaint. May present with squint (if strabismic), squinting/head-tilting to see better, or poor performance on a school vision screen.

**Ix:** visual acuity testing (age-appropriate — e.g. Snellen for older children, picture/symbol charts for younger children) with each eye tested separately (*why:* this is the key diagnostic step — a difference in acuity between the two eyes that isn't correctable by refraction, in an eye with no structural abnormality, defines amblyopia; *what:* reduced acuity in the affected eye, not improved by pinhole/refractive correction). Full eye examination (*why:* to identify and characterise the underlying cause — strabismus (see Cover Test above), refractive error, or a structural cause requiring its own urgent Mx (e.g. congenital cataract — see Cataracts above); *what:* identifies the specific mechanism driving the amblyopia).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (genuinely time-critical, unlike most of the conditions in this file — treatment effectiveness declines sharply with age and is minimal past the critical period):** treat the underlying cause first — correct refractive error with glasses, treat strabismus (see above), remove any obstruction (e.g. congenital cataract surgery, ptosis correction) as early as possible.
- **Definitive (amblyopia-specific treatment, once the underlying cause is addressed):** occlusion therapy — patching the better/dominant eye to force use of and visual development in the amblyopic eye; atropine penalisation (blurring the good eye pharmacologically) as an alternative to patching in some cases, particularly where compliance with patching is difficult.
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing orthoptic/ophthalmology follow-up throughout the treatment period, given the need to titrate patching duration/intensity against response and to stop before overcorrection (amblyopia can be induced in the previously-good eye with excessive patching) — this requires genuine specialist oversight, not a one-off prescription.

**P:** outcomes are strongly age-dependent — treatment started earlier in the critical period achieves substantially better outcomes than treatment started later; amblyopia identified and treated after the critical period closes (roughly age 7–8, though some plasticity persists somewhat beyond this) has a much poorer prognosis for visual recovery, which is the entire rationale for population-based childhood vision screening programs.

## Pupil Problems

Divided into afferent (light going in — CN II problem) or efferent (eye reacts — CN III problem), or both.

### Afferent Pupil Problems
No direct response in the affected eye, but consensual response intact (swinging flashlight test). Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) = when light swings to the affected eye, the pupil dilates slightly. Compression of the optic nerve can also cause decreased colour vision (test with Ishihara plates). Causes: optic neuritis, optic atrophy, retinal disease.

> [!info] Gap-filled — RAPD's paradoxical dilation was described procedurally (what you see on testing) without explaining why it happens, or why it's specifically called a "**relative**" defect rather than an absent one. **Mechanism:** normally, light shone into either eye produces equal bilateral pupillary constriction — both the direct response (same eye) and consensual response (other eye) — because the pretectal nucleus receives input from both optic nerves and sends output bilaterally to both Edinger-Westphal nuclei. **In RAPD, the affected optic nerve is only partially damaged**, so it conducts a genuinely weaker (not absent) signal than the healthy nerve. During the swinging light test: light in the healthy eye produces a normal, strong bilateral constriction. When the light then swings **to** the damaged eye, that eye's weaker afferent signal produces a weaker pupillary stimulus than the consensual constriction the healthy eye had just achieved a moment before — so **relative to** that just-established constriction, the pupil appears to dilate, even though it isn't dilating from a resting baseline in absolute terms. This is precisely why it's called a *relative* defect: it reflects partial, comparative nerve damage detectable by contrasting the two eyes against each other, not complete afferent loss (which would instead show no direct response with a fully intact consensual response, and no comparative "swing" phenomenon at all).

### Efferent Pupil Problems (CN III)
With complete palsy: complete ptosis, fixed dilated pupil, eye looks "down and out" (see CN III palsy causes above). Other causes of a fixed dilated pupil: acute glaucoma, coning (uncal herniation).

### Tonic (Adie) Pupil
**D:** parasympathetic denervation → poor pupil constriction to light. Prevalence 2:1000, F>M, mid-30s.
**S/Smx:** blurry vision, difficulty reading up close.
**Ix:** slit-lamp exam may show wormy movements ("iris streaming").
**Mx:** no specific treatment needed for the pupil abnormality itself given its generally benign course; pilocarpine (low-dose) can be used if symptomatic accommodation difficulty is troublesome; reassurance that this is a benign condition is often the main intervention required.

### Horner Syndrome

> [!note] Cross-file duplicate resolved — a full entry for Horner Syndrome (the three-neuron localisation framework, Pancoast tumour, carotid artery dissection, and the associated Ix/Mx) is already built in [[04_Neurology]] Horner's Syndrome, in more depth than warranted here. Not repeated — see that entry for the complete disease-level content.

**D:** partial ptosis + miosis + facial anhidrosis due to disruption of sympathetic fibres — see [[04_Neurology]] Horner's Syndrome for the full three-neuron localisation (central/1st-order, preganglionic/2nd-order, postganglionic/3rd-order) and the associated causes (Pancoast tumour, carotid dissection, brainstem stroke) this localises to.

**Ophthalmology-specific note:** congenital Horner's syndrome specifically includes iris heterochromia (the affected iris appears lighter-coloured, since sympathetic innervation influences iris melanocyte development) — a finding not typically seen in acquired Horner's syndrome, and a useful clue to a longstanding/congenital rather than new/acquired cause.

### Argyll Robertson Pupil
Bilateral miosis, poor pupillary dilation, pupil irregularity. Light-near dissociation — pupil does not respond to light but can still accommodate to near objects. Causes: neurosyphilis, diabetes. See [[04_Neurology]] Tabes Dorsalis for the fuller neurosyphilis disease context this sign is classically associated with.

> [!info] The mechanism behind light-near dissociation — genuinely worth understanding, not just memorising as an isolated fact: the pupillary light-reflex pathway and the near/accommodation-reflex pathway are **anatomically distinct** in the midbrain, despite both ultimately driving pupillary constriction. The light-reflex fibres run through the dorsal pretectal nuclei before reaching the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, while the near-reflex fibres take a more **ventral** route — a lesion affecting the dorsal/pretectal region can therefore disrupt the light reflex while sparing the near reflex, producing exactly the dissociation seen clinically. **Worth being honest about the limits of current knowledge here**: despite over 150 years since the sign was first described, the precise lesion location and mechanism in neurosyphilis specifically remains somewhat unsettled in the literature — several sources note the exact site hasn't been reliably demonstrated on imaging or pathology, so this is the best current anatomical explanation rather than a fully proven mechanism.

### Anisocoria (Unequal Pupils)
**Causes:** physiological (20% of the population); Horner's syndrome; CN III palsy; migraine headache; drugs (cholinergics, cycloplegics, sympathomimetics); Adie tonic pupil; trauma.

**Assessment approach:** determine whether the abnormal pupil is the larger or the smaller one, and whether the anisocoria is more pronounced in bright or dim light — greater difference in dim light points to a problem with the smaller pupil failing to dilate (e.g. Horner's); greater difference in bright light points to a problem with the larger pupil failing to constrict (e.g. CN III palsy, pharmacological mydriasis) — this single distinction substantially narrows the differential above before further testing.

## Thyroid Eye Disease

**D:** eye condition resulting from Graves' disease; affects 20–30% of Graves' patients.

**A/P:** autoimmune response to the TSH receptor, causing retro-orbital inflammation → glycosaminoglycan and collagen deposition in muscles.

**S/Smx:** exophthalmos; conjunctival oedema; optic disc swelling; ophthalmoplegia; sore, dry eyes (secondary to inability to close eyelids) → can lead to exposure keratopathy. Symptoms have no relation to thyroid hormone status.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis based on the characteristic exam findings in a patient with known/suspected Graves' disease; thyroid function tests and TSH-receptor antibodies support the underlying diagnosis (though note the S/Smx above are explicitly independent of current thyroid hormone status — a euthyroid patient on treatment can still have active or progressive eye disease); orbital imaging (CT/MRI) if optic neuropathy is suspected, to assess the degree of extraocular muscle enlargement causing optic nerve compression.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** topical lubricants for symptomatic dry eye relief; urgent ophthalmology review if any of the red flags below are present, given the risk of optic neuropathy — a genuine sight-threatening emergency requiring prompt escalation, not routine follow-up.
- **Definitive:** treating the underlying Graves' disease (e.g. radiotherapy or surgery) — though note this doesn't always improve established eye disease, since the orbital inflammatory process can run a somewhat independent course from the systemic thyroid disease; prednisolone specifically for active, inflammatory-phase eye disease.
- **Chronic/long-term:** prevention — stop smoking (a significant, modifiable risk factor for both development and progression of thyroid eye disease specifically, beyond its general health effects); ?radioiodine treatment (note: radioiodine can itself worsen eye disease in some patients, so timing/choice of Graves' treatment modality should factor in eye disease status); advise patients about red flag signs so they know when to seek urgent review rather than waiting for a routine appointment.

> [!danger] Ophthalmology review if red flags: unexplained decreased vision, change in colour vision, sudden globe subluxation (or history of this), obvious corneal opacity, cornea still visible when eyes closed, disc swelling.

**Complications:**
- Exposure keratopathy — foreign body sensation, pain, photophobia; can cause corneal scarring and blindness
- Optic neuropathy — due to compression of the optic nerve by extraocular muscles; manifests as decreased acuity, colour vision deficits, visual field defects; requires urgent medical attention
- Strabismus and diplopia — due to enlargement of extraocular muscles restricting movement

## Tropical Eye Diseases

### Xerophthalmia

**D:** dry eyes associated with vitamin A deficiency; affects 40 million children worldwide.

**S/Smx:** night blindness (nyctalopia), tunnel vision, poor acuity, dry eyes.

**Progression:** cornea becomes unwettable → cornea loses transparency → small foamy plaques occur → keratomalacia (cornea softens, thins, and eventually ulcerates) → can lead to blindness.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis based on the characteristic history/exam findings in a patient with vitamin A deficiency risk factors (malnutrition, malabsorption, alcoholism); serum retinol level can confirm deficiency where available, though Mx is typically started on clinical grounds given the urgency of preventing progression to keratomalacia.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** vitamin A supplementation — genuinely time-sensitive, since established keratomalacia (corneal thinning/ulceration) may not be reversible even with treatment, unlike earlier-stage changes.
- **Definitive:** improve nutrition; address associated causes (e.g. alcoholism, malabsorptive conditions) driving the deficiency, rather than treating this as a purely nutritional-supplement problem in isolation.
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing nutritional support and monitoring, particularly relevant in populations/settings with ongoing food insecurity where recurrence risk remains.

### Trachoma

**D:** blindness secondary to *Chlamydia trachomatis* (serotypes A, B, C), spread by flies in hot, dry places where people live near cattle.

**P:** causes scarring on the inner eyelids, damaging the cornea → causes the eyelid to roll inwards so lashes rub directly on the cornea, exacerbating damage → corneal scarring and ulceration.

> [!info] Verified — this is a much more directly Australia-relevant topic than the generic "international guideline, verify AU relevance" note previously here suggested. Australia was, until very recently, **the only developed country in the world with endemic trachoma** — exclusively affecting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities in remote parts of the NT, SA, WA, and Qld. This makes trachoma a genuine, actively-examined Australian public/Indigenous health topic, not just a "tropical disease" footnote.
> **Australian history and current status:** the National Trachoma Management Programme began in 2006, implementing the WHO SAFE strategy with Australian-specific adaptations — most notably **targeted treatment based on community-level prevalence data rather than mass drug administration**, delivered through partnerships with Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations (ACCHOs). In April 2026, the WHO formally recognised Australia as having eliminated trachoma as a public health problem — the 30th country globally to do so, and the outcome of a ~20-year targeted program. This does not mean trachoma is gone entirely — ongoing surveillance continues, since environmental risk factors (overcrowding, limited access to water/sanitation) persist in many communities and recrudescence remains possible without continued facial cleanliness and environmental improvement efforts.
> **Australian antibiotic approach specifically:** azithromycin is given to household members of a diagnosed case, or to everyone in an affected community, every 6–12 months (rather than the broader mass drug administration model used in some other endemic countries) — targeted at the community/household level based on local prevalence surveillance data, not given empirically to individuals in isolation the way the general **S**urgery/**A**ntibiotics/**F**acial cleanliness/**E**nvironmental improvement framework might otherwise imply.
> **Screening:** WHO grading criteria are used; a community is classified as "at-risk" and eligible for the program based on trachoma prevalence ≥5% in children aged 5–9; facial cleanliness (proportion of children with a "clean face") is itself a tracked and reported public health indicator, reflecting how central hygiene promotion is to the whole strategy.

**Mx (WHO SAFE strategy, delivered via the Australian program described above):**
**S**urgery (for trichiasis — correcting the inward-turned eyelashes before they cause further corneal damage), **A**ntibiotics (azithromycin, dosed/targeted as above), **F**acial cleanliness (promotion of face-washing, particularly in children — the single most emphasised behavioural intervention in the Australian program), **E**nvironmental improvement (addressing overcrowding, water access, and sanitation — the structural drivers of transmission).

### Onchocerciasis (River Blindness)

**D:** blindness secondary to *Onchocerca volvulus* (nematode parasite), transmitted between humans by black flies; infection causes blindness in 40% of cases.

**P:** parasite infects (or travels to) the eye, causing inflammation → fibrosis + corneal opacities. Chronic iritis causes adhesions (synechiae) ± cataracts and a fixed pupil. Also causes skin symptoms where the parasite lodges in subcutaneous tissue.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis in an appropriate exposure context (endemic region, black fly exposure); skin snip microscopy can identify microfilariae; slit-lamp exam may directly visualise motile microfilariae in the anterior chamber in active ocular disease.

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute tier — this is a chronic parasitic infection managed with repeated treatment courses rather than a single acute intervention):** ivermectin (kills microfilariae, though not the adult worms, hence the need for repeated dosing over the parasite's lifespan) + steroids (controls the inflammatory response to dying microfilariae, since the inflammatory reaction itself — not just direct parasitic damage — drives much of the ocular pathology).
- **Chronic/long-term:** repeated ivermectin dosing (typically annually or more frequently in mass drug administration programs) over years, since a single course doesn't clear adult worms; community-level vector control (reducing black fly breeding sites) as a public health measure alongside individual treatment.

## Ocular Manifestations of Systemic Disease — Consolidated Reference

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Ocular manifestations of systemic disease," Medium yield, filed under Cardiology in the source spreadsheet but genuinely an Ophthalmology topic) — the individual disease entries this draws together (Diabetic Retinopathy, Hypertensive Retinopathy, Thyroid Eye Disease, and others throughout this file) are already thoroughly built; this entry provides the reverse lookup — given a known systemic disease, what should the eye be examined for — which is a genuinely different retrieval direction than reading each disease entry individually, and is exactly what this CSV item is testing.

- **Diabetes mellitus:** diabetic retinopathy (see Diabetic Retinopathy above, not repeated here) — the leading cause of blindness in the working-age population, making systematic screening genuinely important rather than only examining symptomatically; also cranial nerve palsies (classically CN III with pupillary sparing, given diabetic microvascular disease preferentially affects the nerve's central fibres while sparing the peripherally-located pupillomotor fibres — see [[04_Neurology]] for the broader cranial nerve palsy differential this fits into, not repeated here), and an increased risk of cataracts.
- **Hypertension:** hypertensive retinopathy (see Hypertensive Retinopathy above, not repeated here); also a risk factor for retinal vein and artery occlusion (see Retinal Vein Occlusion and Retinal Artery Occlusion above, not repeated here).
- **Thyroid disease:** thyroid eye disease specifically associated with Graves' hyperthyroidism (see Thyroid Eye Disease above, not repeated here) — proptosis, lid retraction/lag, diplopia from extraocular muscle involvement.
- **Rheumatological/autoimmune disease:** scleritis and episcleritis (see the relevant entries above, not repeated here) — scleritis in particular is a genuinely useful clue to active systemic autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and other vasculitides) rather than a purely localised eye problem, per the note already established in the Scleritis entry; anterior uveitis (see the relevant entry above, not repeated here) associated with the seronegative spondyloarthropathies (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, IBD-associated arthritis), sarcoidosis, and Behçet's disease; dry eye (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) as a feature of Sjögren's syndrome specifically — see [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]] and [[12_03_Rheum_-_Connective_Tissue_Diseases__SLE__Systemic_Sclerosis__Dermatomyositis__Polymyositis__Sjogren_]] for the disease-level detail these associations connect to, not repeated here.
- **Giant cell arteritis:** sudden, often profound and irreversible vision loss (via anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy or, less commonly, central retinal artery occlusion) — a genuine ophthalmic emergency given the visual loss can become bilateral if untreated; see [[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] for the full disease-level Ix/Mx (including the urgent high-dose steroid principle already established there), not repeated here.
- **HIV/immunosuppression:** CMV retinitis and other opportunistic ocular infections in advanced immunosuppression — see [[04_Neurology]] CNS Infections Associated with Immunosuppression for the broader immunosuppression-related infection context, not repeated here.
- **Malignancy/haematological disease:** retinal haemorrhages and other fundoscopic changes can occur in severe anaemia, leukaemia (via hyperviscosity or direct infiltration), and other haematological disease — see the relevant Haematology/Oncology entries elsewhere in this project for disease-level detail, not repeated here.
- **Multiple sclerosis:** optic neuritis (see the relevant entry above, not repeated here) — often the presenting feature of MS, making eye examination findings genuinely relevant to a much broader neurological diagnosis, not just an isolated ophthalmic concern.

**The genuinely important clinical principle this entry represents:** the eye is frequently a visible, examinable "window" onto systemic disease — a careful fundoscopic and general ocular examination can provide diagnostic clues to a systemic condition that hasn't yet been otherwise identified, which is precisely why ocular findings appear so often as a presenting or supporting feature across many of the systemic diseases built elsewhere throughout this project, not only as isolated primary eye disease.
