## Migraine

**D:** chronic, episodic neurological disorder characterised chiefly by severe headaches ± aura.

**R:** family history, F>M (~3:1), young to middle age, obesity (BMI >30), stress, sleep disorder

**A:** uncertain; ?hyperexcitable brain, strong genetic component

**P:** neurogenic inflammation of trigeminal sensory neurons → vasodilation of blood vessels → headache. Aura relates to neuronal dysfunction.

**S/Smx:** headache lasting 4–72h, unilateral location, pulsatile quality, moderate-severe pain, aggravated by (or causes avoidance of) usual activity, ± nausea/vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, ± association with menses in women, ± aura (25% — visual symptoms e.g. scotoma).

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* migraine is diagnosed on history alone using established criteria — Ix is reserved for excluding a secondary cause when red flags are present, not for confirming migraine itself; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above meeting **ICHD (International Classification of Headache Disorders)** criteria — the formal diagnostic classification, applied to the symptom pattern rather than requiring any test). Exclude red flag symptoms (see "Other Headache Causes" section below for the full SNOOP4 red-flag list) — imaging (CT/MRI) only if red flags present, not for typical migraine.

**Mx:**

> [!info] Verified against Australian migraine management literature (Pain Management Today, an Australian GP-focused source), Aug 2026 — acute Mx is essentially internationally standard and matches the content below; the main AU-specific point is a difference in prophylaxis prescribing pattern.
> **Immediate/acute:** sumatriptan + NSAID/paracetamol (in 12–17yo, consider nasal triptan instead of PO); non-PO metoclopramide or prochlorperazine, consider non-PO NSAID or triptan if vomiting limits oral absorption.
>
> Chronic migraine = ≥8 days/month for ≥3 months.
>
> **Chronic/long-term (prophylaxis):** propranolol; topiramate (NOT in women of childbearing age — teratogenic); amitriptyline. **In Australian general practice specifically, there has been a strong historical bias toward pizotifen or propranolol as first-choice prophylaxis** — pizotifen does not appear in the standard UK-sourced prophylaxis list this entry began from (propranolol/topiramate/amitriptyline, named at the start of this line) but is a genuinely common Australian first-line choice; note, however, that current specialist guidance cautions this bias doesn't always reflect the most appropriate individual choice, and agent selection should be guided by comorbidities and side-effect profile rather than habit. For women with predictable menses-related migraines, frovatriptan or zolmitriptan may be given ± mefenamic acid, caffeine. In pregnancy, paracetamol + NSAIDs (2nd line); avoid aspirin and opioids.

> [!danger] Migraine with aura is an absolute contraindication to the COCP due to markedly increased stroke risk. HRT might worsen migraines.

## Trigeminal Neuralgia

**D:** facial pain syndrome in the distribution of ≥1 divisions of the trigeminal nerve (CN V).

**R:** ↑age, multiple sclerosis (20x risk), ?F>M (3:2), ?HTN

**A:** focal compression of the CN V root by an aberrant vascular loop; idiopathic/other causes

**P:** focal demyelination → conduction dysfunction → pain

**S/Smx:** brief (seconds), sudden onset and cessation; electric shock-like pain limited to the CN V distribution; triggered by any movement/sensation in that distribution (touching the face, shaving, brushing teeth).

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* trigeminal neuralgia is a clinical diagnosis based on the characteristic pain pattern; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above). Exclude red flags — MRI if atypical features (younger age, bilateral, sensory deficit, other neurological signs) since these raise suspicion for an underlying cause such as MS or a compressive lesion rather than idiopathic/vascular-loop compression (*why:* MRI screens for a structural cause needing separate management, particularly relevant given the MS association noted above; *what:* may show an aberrant vascular loop, or an alternative structural cause such as a demyelinating plaque or tumour).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute/emergency tier — this is a chronic pain condition managed pharmacologically first):** carbamazepine first-line; if no response, refer to neurology.
- **Chronic/long-term:** neurosurgical options (e.g. microvascular decompression, ablative procedures) considered for medically refractory pain once specialist-assessed.

## Trigeminal Autonomic Cephalalgias

**D:** group of primary headache disorders characterised by unilateral head pain associated with generally prominent ipsilateral cranial autonomic features. Includes: cluster headache, paroxysmal hemicrania, SUNCT (short-lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache attacks with conjunctival injection and tearing), SUNA (with cranial autonomic symptoms).

### Cluster Headache

**D:** a trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia causing headaches occurring in clusters of weeks, around once a year.

**R:** M>F (3:1), family history, head injury, smoking, alcohol use (can trigger)

**A:** unknown

**P:** trigeminal distribution, ipsilateral cranial autonomic symptoms, occurring a few weeks per year.

**S/Smx:** headache lasting 15 min–2h; clusters typically ~4–12 weeks; intense, sharp, stabbing pain around one eye (described as one of the worst kinds of pain); associated with lacrimation, redness, lid swelling; ± miosis, ptosis. Patient is often restless during attacks (as opposed to migraine, where patients often want to lie down).

**Ix:** MRI head with gadolinium (*why:* excludes a secondary structural cause — cluster headache is a clinical diagnosis, but first presentations warrant imaging to exclude a pituitary or other structural lesion given some overlap in presentation; *what:* usually normal in primary cluster headache).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** 100% oxygen and subcutaneous triptan (80% and 75% respond within 15 min for each treatment respectively) — refer to neurology for diagnosis confirmation and ongoing management.
- **Chronic/long-term (prophylaxis, started early in a cluster to shorten its course):** verapamil ± tapering dose of prednisolone.

## Tension Headache

**D:** headache characterised by generalised distribution, like a "tight band around the head". Also known as tension-type headache.

**R:** mental tension/stress, fatigue, missing meals, dehydration

**A:** unknown; ?muscle tension

**P:** release and activation of inflammatory agents → pain

**S/Smx:** "tight band around head"; can coexist with migraine but lacks migraine features.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* tension headache is diagnosed on the characteristic history alone; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above, with exclusion of red flags as for other primary headaches).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** paracetamol, NSAIDs, aspirin.
- **Chronic/long-term:** avoid known triggers (stress, missed meals, dehydration, fatigue) — lifestyle-focused prevention rather than pharmacological prophylaxis for most patients, given tension headache doesn't typically require the disease-modifying prophylaxis used in migraine/cluster headache.

## Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis)

> [!note] For further detail, see [[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] Giant cell arteritis (GCA) — this section covers only the headache aspect.

**R:** >50–60yo, F>M, ?family history

**S/Smx:** headache occurs in 85% of GCA patients, with a tender, palpable temporal artery. Rapid onset, history <1 month, unilateral. Jaw claudication in 65%. ± ocular complications.

**Ix:** ↑ESR (*why:* a sensitive but non-specific inflammatory marker, and the key first-line screening test given how time-critical this diagnosis is — visual loss can be sudden and irreversible; *what:* typically markedly elevated). Temporal artery biopsy to confirm (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test; *what:* may require >1 biopsy due to skip lesions, so a single negative biopsy doesn't fully exclude the diagnosis if clinical suspicion remains high).

> [!warning] **[[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] Giant cell arteritis (GCA) owns the full Mx and the steroid doses** — urgent high-dose steroids should not be delayed for biopsy confirmation, given the risk of irreversible visual loss, and biopsy stays positive for 1–2 weeks after steroids are started. Not repeated here.

## Medication Overuse Headache

**D:** chronic headache secondary to overuse of analgesics, ≥15 days/month. Common.

**A/P:** not fully understood — ?overuse of analgesics leads to decreased receptor sensitivity to analgesia → paradoxical pain ("rebound effect") when the analgesic is withdrawn.

**Dx:** pre-existing headache disorder requiring analgesia; headache ≥15 days/month; regular analgesic overuse for >3 months (people taking triptans or opioids at higher risk); not better accounted for by another headache syndrome.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** if on paracetamol, NSAID, or triptan — withdraw abruptly (warn patient headache will get worse before it improves; persist). If on opioids — gradual reduction. Withdrawal symptoms: nausea/vomiting, hypotension, ↑HR, restlessness, anxiety, sleep disturbance — persist through these.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address the underlying primary headache disorder driving the analgesic overuse (e.g. appropriate migraine prophylaxis) once withdrawal is complete, to prevent relapse into overuse.

## Other Headache Causes

> [!danger] Headache red flags — mnemonic "SNOOP4"
> **S**ystemic symptoms (incl. fever), **N**eoplasm in PMH/FHx, **N**eurologic deficit or change, **O**nset — sudden/abrupt, **O**lder age >50yo, **P**attern change or recent onset, **P**ositional headache, **P**recipitated by sneezing/coughing/exercise, **P**apilloedema, **P**rogressive headache + atypical symptoms, **P**regnancy or puerperium, **P**ainful eye with autonomic features, **P**ost-traumatic onset, **P**athology of immune system (e.g. HIV), **P**ainkiller overuse or new drug at headache onset

**Acute, single episodes:**
- Subarachnoid haemorrhage — occipital location, "thunderclap" sudden onset, "worst headache of my life"
- Acute closed-angle glaucoma — severe pain (ocular or generalised), ↓visual acuity, hard red eye, semi-dilated non-reacting pupil
- Meningitis/encephalitis — non-specific symptoms
- Head injury
- Sinusitis — associated with rhinorrhoea, allergic-type symptoms
- Tropical illness (e.g. malaria)

**Chronic:**
- Idiopathic intracranial hypertension — risk factors: obesity, F>M, pregnancy, drugs (COCP, steroids, tetracyclines, retinoids, lithium); symptoms include blurred vision, papilloedema, enlarged blind spot ± CN VI palsy (see ICP section)
- Chronically raised ICP
- Paget's disease of bone — secondary to mechanical compression or reduced blood flow to neural tissue; occipital headache, worse on coughing
- Psychological

## Dementias

### Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Cognitive screening tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS) & MCI progression to dementia," High yield). The tools half of that row is fully built in [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Cognitive Screening Tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS), but **MCI itself appeared exactly once in the entire corpus** — as an acronym inside a sentence about MoCA's sensitivity — with no definition, no boundary against normal ageing or dementia, and no management. Placed here at the head of the Dementias section rather than in the Geriatrics file because MCI is the prodromal state of the conditions below, and belongs with them. Verified against Dementia Australia's MCI guidance and healthdirect (Australian Government), Aug 2026.

**D:** objective cognitive impairment in one or more domains that is **greater than expected for age but does not impair independent function in daily life** — the functional criterion is the whole distinction. Once cognitive impairment causes loss of independence in everyday activities, the diagnosis is dementia, not MCI.

**The three-way boundary an intern is expected to hold:**
- **Subjective cognitive decline** — the person notices a change; formal testing is normal.
- **MCI** — testing is objectively abnormal; **function is preserved**.
- **Dementia** — testing is abnormal **and** function is impaired.

**Subtypes:** *amnestic* MCI (memory predominantly affected — the pattern most associated with progression to Alzheimer's disease) and *non-amnestic* MCI (executive, language or visuospatial domains predominantly).

> [!info] **Progression to dementia — represent this honestly, because the commonly-quoted single figure is misleading.** Annual conversion rates vary substantially with the population studied: around **8% per year in clinical trial cohorts and roughly 13% in large registries**, with higher rates in specialist memory-clinic populations, which are enriched for people already more likely to progress. Amnestic MCI converts faster than non-amnestic.
>
> The genuinely important counterpoint, and the one patients most need to hear: **MCI is not a guaranteed pre-dementia state.** It may remain stable for years, and a meaningful proportion of people **revert to normal cognition** — often those whose impairment had a reversible contributor in the first place. Presenting MCI as "early dementia" is both inaccurate and needlessly distressing.

**Ix — the point of investigating MCI is to find the reversible contributors**, because that is where the clinical value lies:
- Cognitive assessment with a validated tool (see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Cognitive Screening Tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS)) — MoCA is preferred over MMSE here specifically because of its greater sensitivity at the mild end.
- **Collateral history**, which does more work than any test in establishing whether function is genuinely preserved.
- Bloods: **B12 and folate** (*why:* deficiency is a treatable cause of cognitive impairment; *what:* low — see Subacute Combined Degeneration of the Spinal Cord for the fuller mechanism, not repeated here), **TFTs** (*why:* hypothyroidism is treatable and commonly presents with cognitive slowing in older people; *what:* raised TSH), U&Es, calcium, LFTs, glucose/HbA1c.
- **Screen for depression** — depressive pseudodementia is the single most important reversible mimic (see Delirium vs Dementia vs Depression — the "3 Ds" in Older People below).
- **Medication review** — anticholinergic and sedative burden is a genuinely common and fully reversible contributor (see [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Polypharmacy and Deprescribing, not repeated here).
- Neuroimaging (CT or MRI brain) where there are atypical features, focal signs, rapid progression, or a young age at onset — not routinely in every case.

> [!danger] **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people develop dementia earlier and more often, and the age at which you should be thinking about it is correspondingly younger.** Age-specific prevalence ratios are most pronounced at the *younger* age bands — of the order of 3.5-fold at 45–49 and 3.8-fold at 50–54 — so cognitive symptoms in a 50-year-old Aboriginal patient warrant assessment rather than reassurance that they are too young. This is also why aged care eligibility begins at 50 rather than 65 — a threshold that applies to people who are homeless or at risk of homelessness as well (see [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Falls in Older People, not repeated here). **A further practical caution: the standard screening tools are not culturally neutral** — MMSE and MoCA depend on formal schooling, literacy and Western cultural content, and applying them unadjusted will over-diagnose impairment. Validated alternatives designed for this population exist (the KICA — Kimberley Indigenous Cognitive Assessment — being the best known) and should be used instead. Verified against AIHW dementia reporting for First Nations Australians and Australian rurality/dementia prevalence research, Aug 2026.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** identify and treat every reversible contributor found above — this is the highest-value intervention and the reason the work-up matters.
- **Definitive:** **there is no approved drug treatment for MCI**, and cholinesterase inhibitors are **not** indicated at this stage — a common error, given they are used in Alzheimer's disease below. What has evidence is: **regular physical exercise** (any level better than none), **treating cardiovascular risk factors** — hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia — as modifiable dementia risk factors, cognitive and social engagement, addressing hearing and vision impairment, and treating depression where present.
- **Chronic/long-term:** **planned review over time** rather than a single assessment, since the trajectory is the diagnostic information and a one-off score cannot distinguish stable MCI from early dementia. Refer to a geriatrician, memory clinic or neurologist where the presentation is atypical, progression is rapid, or onset is young. Use the period of preserved capacity to raise **advance care planning and enduring power of attorney** while the person can genuinely participate (see [[Clinical-Process-EBM-Consent-Capacity]] Capacity assessment — the general framework) — a conversation that is far easier now than after progression, and a specific reason not to defer the discussion simply because the diagnosis is "only" MCI.

### Vascular Dementia

**D:** chronic progressive cerebrovascular disease of the brain causing cognitive impairment. 2nd most common dementia after Alzheimer's.

**R:** >60yo, obesity, HTN, smoking, stroke risk factors (e.g. AF)

**A/P:** infarction (secondary to emboli, thrombi, lacunar infarction, hypoxia), leukoaraiosis (subcortical leukoencephalopathy), haemorrhage. Vascular changes exhaust the brain's compensatory mechanisms and lead to dementia.

**S/Smx:** history of stroke strongly suggestive. Frontal cognitive syndrome — difficulty solving problems, apathy, disinhibition, slowed processing, poor attention, retrieval memory deficit, frontal release reflexes (grasp, glabella tap, jaw jerk). ± focal neurological signs, impaired gait and balance.

**Dx/Ix:** cognitive testing (e.g. MMSE) (*why:* quantifies the cognitive impairment and its pattern, feeding into the NINDS-AIREN criteria below; *what:* impairment across multiple domains, often with a frontal-subcortical pattern in VD specifically — see the frontal cognitive syndrome features above). Imaging — MRI (best) or CT (*why:* directly demonstrates the cerebrovascular disease burden required for diagnosis (criterion 2 below) and its temporal relationship to symptom onset; *what:* infarcts, leukoaraiosis, or haemorrhage — the specific findings needed to fulfil the NINDS-AIREN criteria). Baseline bloods — FBC, vitamin B12 (*why:* screens for reversible/contributing causes of cognitive impairment that could mimic or compound VD; *what:* anaemia or B12 deficiency would be alternative/additional explanations), consider syphilis serology (*why:* neurosyphilis is a rare but treatable mimic of dementia, worth excluding especially with an atypical picture; *what:* positive serology would redirect Mx entirely).

> [!info] NINDS-AIREN criteria for diagnosis of VD
> (1) Dementia — impairment in memory + ≥2 other domains (orientation, attention, language, executive function)
> (2) Cerebrovascular disease — focal signs on neuro exam (e.g. hemiparesis)
> (3) Time relationship — step-wise decline or dementia within 3 months after stroke
> (4) Clinical symptoms consistent, e.g. gait disturbance, urinary incontinence

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute tier — this is a chronic, progressive condition, though acute strokes contributing to it are managed per the Strokes section above):** consider referral to specialist dementia clinic/neuro services; secondary prevention of stroke (antiplatelets, antihypertensives — the mainstay of Mx, since preventing further vascular injury is the main lever available, unlike AD where disease-modifying drugs exist); no specific drugs for VD itself.
- **Chronic/long-term:** non-pharmacological — cognitive stimulation programmes, music/art therapy; ensure social support for family/carers; ongoing cardiovascular risk factor management (the same risk factors driving the vascular disease itself).

### Alzheimer's Disease (AD)

**D:** chronic neurodegenerative disease with insidious onset and progressive (generally slow) decline.

**R:** ↑age, family history, genetics, Down's syndrome (early onset), CVD, smoking, midlife obesity, ↑saturated fats (linked to apolipoprotein E allele E4), alcohol >14 units/week or 0 units/week

**A/P:** increased interneuronal amyloid peptides due to decreased clearance of β-amyloid → amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles → microglial activation, cytokine and complement activation → neuritic plaques → synaptic/neuritic injury and cell death.
- Presenilin 1 and 2 gene missense mutations → early-onset AD
- Cerebral cortical and hippocampal atrophy

**S/Smx:** cognitive deficits (loss of recent memory, then decreased executive function, nominal dysphasia). Insidious onset, gradual progression.

> [!info] Early warning signs (10)
> Memory loss disrupting daily life; challenges in planning/problem-solving; difficulty completing familiar tasks; confusion with time/place; trouble with visual images and spatial relationships; new problems with words in speaking/writing; misplacing things and losing ability to retrace steps; decreased judgment; withdrawal from work/social activities; change in mood and personality

**Ix:** cognitive testing (e.g. MMSE) (*why:* establishes the pattern and severity of cognitive impairment, and provides a baseline to track progression; *what:* typically shows a memory-predominant deficit pattern early on, distinguishing it from the frontal-subcortical pattern of VD or the executive/visuospatial pattern of LBD). Baseline bloods (*why:* excludes reversible/contributing causes as for VD above — B12/folate deficiency, hypothyroidism, and other metabolic causes of cognitive impairment should always be screened before attributing decline to AD; *what:* identifies any reversible contributor).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — chronic progressive neurodegenerative disease):** non-pharmacological (reminiscence therapy, cognitive rehab); acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g. donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) for mild-moderate AD; memantine (NMDA receptor antagonist) as add-on or monotherapy for more advanced disease.
- **Behavioural symptoms:** antipsychotics ONLY for patients at risk of self-harm or severe distress (associated with increased mortality in dementia — a genuine risk-benefit trade-off, not a routine option).

**P:** 8–10 years of deterioration typically.

### Lewy Body Dementia (LBD)

**D:** neurodegenerative disorder with parkinsonism, progressive cognitive decline, prominent executive dysfunction, and visuospatial impairment.

**R:** ↑age

**A:** unknown

**P:** accumulation of Lewy bodies in vulnerable sites; course mimics idiopathic Parkinson's disease. Lewy bodies contain α-synuclein (a cytoplasmic protein associated with synaptic vesicles), neurofilament, ubiquitin. Marked ↓ ACh and dopamine.

**S/Smx:** cognitive fluctuations, visual hallucinations (80%), parkinsonian features, REM sleep disturbance; autonomic dysfunction (falls, syncope, urinary incontinence, constipation), delusions, altered sleep.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* LBD is primarily diagnosed on the characteristic clinical triad of fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, and parkinsonism; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above). Imaging — SPECT (*why:* dopamine transporter (DaT) SPECT imaging supports the diagnosis when the clinical picture is ambiguous, by demonstrating reduced striatal dopaminergic activity similar to idiopathic Parkinson's; *what:* reduced striatal uptake supports LBD/PD-spectrum pathology over AD).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic:** as per AD — cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine (often particularly effective for the cognitive/hallucination symptoms in LBD specifically).

> [!danger] Avoid antipsychotics (especially first-generation) due to extrapyramidal side effect risk, which can worsen parkinsonian symptoms — this is a specific, important LBD prescribing hazard, more pronounced than in other dementias.

**P:** average survival 5–7 years from symptom onset. In LBD, neuropsychiatric and motor symptoms occur within 1 year of each other. Dementia can also develop in Parkinson's disease, but typically >1 year after motor symptoms.

### Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (FTLD)

**D:** spectrum of primary neurodegenerative brain diseases affecting the frontal and/or temporal lobes.
- Behavioural variant ≈ Pick's disease
- Progressive nonfluent aphasia ≈ chronic progressive aphasia (CPA)
- Semantic dementia

**R:** mutations in some genes, ?traumatic brain injury

**A:** genetics (~30%), traumatic brain injury

**P:** neuronal loss, gliosis, microvascular changes of frontotemporal lobes. Abnormal neuronal/glial inclusions: FTLD-tau (hyperphosphorylated tau), TDP, FET.

**S/Smx:**
- All variants: onset ≤65yo, insidious onset, relatively preserved memory, changes in personality/social conduct
- Behavioural variant: disinhibition, apathy/inertia, increased appetite, perseveration
- CPA: effortful language output, loss of grammar, motor speech deficits
- Semantic dementia: progressive loss of knowledge about words and objects

**Ix:** MMSE (*why:* baseline cognitive assessment, though may under-detect FTLD early since memory — the domain MMSE weights heavily — is relatively preserved in FTLD, unlike AD; *what:* may be relatively normal early despite significant functional/behavioural impairment — a key diagnostic pitfall). MRI (*why:* demonstrates the characteristic regional atrophy pattern distinguishing FTLD from AD's more diffuse/hippocampal pattern; *what:* frontal/temporal lobe atrophy; behavioural variant shows focal gyral atrophy). Post-mortem biopsy (*why:* the definitive histological diagnosis, obviously not useful for antemortem management but relevant to eventual diagnostic confirmation/family counselling; *what:* may show Pick bodies (tau protein bodies on silver staining), gliosis, neurofibrillary tangles and senile plaques).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic:** supportive care; cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine not recommended (unlike AD/LBD — a genuine treatment distinction worth knowing, since prescribing these in FTLD offers no benefit and may worsen behavioural symptoms in some cases).

**P:** median survival from diagnosis 7–13 years — generally shorter survival and faster decline than AD.

### Cognitive-enhancing drug classes
**Cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEI)** (e.g. rivastigmine, donepezil, galantamine) — for mild-moderate AD. MOA: increased cholinergic activity. SE: GI upset, GU changes, sleep disorders. Note: donepezil relatively contraindicated with bradycardia.

**NMDA receptor antagonists** (e.g. memantine) — for severe AD, better tolerated. SE: GI upset, balance issues.

### Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus

**D:** condition with clinical features of hydrocephalus but without significant increased CSF pressure.

**R:** >65yo, vascular disease

**A/P:** not well understood; ?decreased blood flow to basal ganglia

**S/Smx:** "wet, wobbly, wacky" — gait apraxia not responding to levodopa; cognitive impairment; urinary incontinence.

**Ix:** CT head or MRI head (*why:* demonstrates the characteristic imaging pattern required for diagnosis; *what:* hydrocephalus with ventriculomegaly out of proportion to sulcal enlargement — this disproportion is the key distinguishing feature from simple age-related atrophy). Levodopa challenge (*why:* helps distinguish NPH's gait apraxia from Parkinson's disease, since NPH gait doesn't improve with levodopa; *what:* lack of response supports NPH over PD). LP (*why:* a large-volume "tap test" (removing a significant CSF volume) can predict response to shunting, used as a prognostic test before committing to surgery; *what:* symptomatic improvement after CSF removal supports a good shunt-response likelihood).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive:** ventriculoperitoneal shunting — the only effective treatment, and often produces meaningful improvement given the condition's mechanism is fundamentally a CSF dynamics problem.
- **Chronic/long-term:** shunting is lifelong, with ongoing monitoring for complications — seizures, infection, bleeding (ICH), stroke.

**P:** shunting is lifelong.

## Delirium

**D:** acute, fluctuating changes in mental status — inattention, disorganised thinking, altered level of consciousness. Divided into hyperactive, hypoactive, mixed.

**R:** >65yo, past/present cognitive impairment or dementia, current hip fracture, severe illness

**A/P:** reduced functional reserve + precipitating insult = delirium. Insults include: drugs and withdrawal, primary neurological injury, acute illness (infection, hypoxia, shock, dehydration, fever, constipation), metabolic abnormalities, surgery, pain, sleep deprivation, environmental factors (physical restraints, catheters, ICU stay).

**S/Smx:** acute onset (hours to days); decreased cognition (often fluctuating); impaired consciousness (more drowsy than normal); agitation, fear, delusions; abnormal perceptions (e.g. hallucinating); symptoms usually worse at night, interspersed with periods of normality.

**Ix:** cognitive testing (e.g. MMSE) (*why:* establishes a baseline and severity, though delirium's fluctuating course means a single assessment can under- or over-estimate impairment; *what:* fluctuating deficits, often with impaired attention as the most sensitive finding). Look for the source of delirium (*why:* delirium is always a symptom of an underlying precipitant — Ix should be directed at finding the reversible cause(s) rather than confirming delirium itself, which is diagnosed clinically; *what:* U&Es for AKI/electrolyte disturbance, FBC for infection (↑WCC), plus other targeted Ix per the precipitants listed above — e.g. blood glucose, urinalysis/culture, CXR, medication review).

> [!tip] Organic vs psychiatric causes of memory loss can be assessed with serial 7s — usually intact in psychiatric causes.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** remove precipitating insult, manage risk factors (e.g. medication review), give clear instructions, signage, visits — non-pharmacological measures remain first-line even in acute agitation, with medication reserved for when these are insufficient (see the box below for AU-specific antipsychotic dosing considerations, flagged as needing direct eTG/AMH confirmation).
- **Chronic/long-term (prevention):** assess for risk factors, ensure familiarity of environment — proactive prevention in at-risk patients (elderly, known cognitive impairment, hip fracture, severe illness) reduces incidence, given how much easier delirium is to prevent than to treat once established.

> [!info] Resolved — non-pharmacological management remains first-line in Australia as elsewhere (per eTG/AMH), and haloperidol is confirmed as the standard first-choice antipsychotic when medication is needed. CareSearch (an Australian palliative care clinical resource) gives the directly-matching, PBS-referenced Australian starting dose: **haloperidol 0.5–1mg (SC in the palliative/end-of-life setting specifically, though PO is also used more generally) twice daily PRN**, confirming the file's existing starting figure is essentially correct — the "0.5mg BD" starting point matches, though route (SC vs PO) depends on clinical context and swallowing ability rather than being fixed. For ongoing/persistent delirium, dosing is typically given regularly rather than PRN-only (either as a standing BD dose or via continuous subcutaneous infusion in the palliative setting), with additional PRN doses as needed on top. As before: QTc prolongation risk means baseline/repeat ECG monitoring is prudent, particularly in frail/elderly patients or with escalating doses, and doses should be titrated cautiously with more frequent review in this population.
> If patient becomes aggressive: haloperidol 0.5 mg PO bd → increase to 2.5 mg PO if no effect; IM haloperidol if no other choice; if secondary to alcohol withdrawal — diazepam is the Australian first-line choice, not chlordiazepoxide (which isn't registered in Australia — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ArLD) → Alcohol withdrawal for the fuller AU-verified regimen).

## Delirium vs Dementia vs Depression — the "3 Ds" in Older People

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Delirium vs dementia vs depression (differentiating in the elderly)," High yield, Older Persons Health / Geriatrics category). Each of the three conditions was already built — Delirium immediately above, Dementias earlier in this file, and [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Depression (Major depressive disorder) — but the *differential between them* existed only as a one-line tip about serial 7s. Built here rather than in the new Geriatrics file because both structural anchors (Dementias, Delirium) already live in this file, and separating the comparison from the things being compared would make it harder to find, not easier. Verified against the RACGP aged care clinical guide (Silver Book, 5th edition), which carries the same comparison, Aug 2026.

This is the single most commonly tested geriatric discriminator, and the single most common source of avoidable harm in an older inpatient — because the default assumption when an older person is confused is usually "dementia", and the answer is usually delirium.

| | **Delirium** | **Dementia** | **Depression** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Acute — hours to days, and usually datable | Insidious — months to years, no clear start | Subacute — weeks to months |
| **Course** | **Fluctuating**, characteristically worse in the evening and at night | Progressive; stable within any given day | Relatively stable day to day, often with diurnal variation (worse in the morning) |
| **Attention** | **Markedly impaired — this is the cardinal feature** | Preserved until late disease | May be reduced by poor concentration, but the patient can be engaged and redirected |
| **Level of consciousness** | Altered — drowsy, or hypervigilant | Clear until very late | Clear |
| **Psychomotor activity** | Hyperactive, **hypoactive**, or mixed | Usually normal early | Retardation, or sometimes agitation |
| **Perception** | Hallucinations common, characteristically **visual**; fleeting delusions | Possible, especially in Lewy body dementia (see Lewy Body Dementia above) | Uncommon unless psychotic depression |
| **Sleep–wake cycle** | Disrupted, often reversed | Fragmented | Early morning waking |
| **The patient's own account** | Cannot sustain attention well enough to give one | Often **minimises or confabulates**; typically unaware of the deficit | **Complains prominently** of poor memory; answers "I don't know" rather than guessing |
| **Reversibility** | Usually reversible once the precipitant is treated | Not reversible | Treatable |

> [!danger] Three traps, in order of how often they cause harm
> 1. **Hypoactive delirium mistaken for depression, or dismissed as "just their dementia."** The quiet, withdrawn, drowsy patient is the one who gets missed — and hypoactive delirium carries a *worse* prognosis than the hyperactive form that attracts attention. Being difficult to rouse is not being depressed.
> 2. **Delirium superimposed on dementia — the commonest scenario of all, not an edge case.** Dementia is the single largest risk factor for delirium. So the two are not alternatives to choose between: **any acute change in cognition or behaviour in a person with known dementia is delirium until proven otherwise**, and needs the precipitant hunt in the Delirium entry above, not a shrug and a note saying "known dementia".
> 3. **Assuming the three are mutually exclusive.** They coexist frequently — depression is common in early dementia, and both predispose to delirium.

> [!info] **Two of the table's rows are usually memorised when they are actually derivable, and both come from the same mechanism.** Delirium is a global disturbance of the brain's arousal and attentional networks rather than a failure of memory *storage* — which is why **attention is the first and most severely affected domain**, and why memory appears impaired mainly because nothing was attended to well enough to be encoded in the first place. The same network dependence explains the **fluctuation and the evening worsening**: attention is the most effort-dependent cognitive function, so it degrades first as the day's reserve is spent, and it degrades further once the external cues that support orientation — daylight, routine, staff presence, visitors — are withdrawn at night. Sundowning is therefore not a separate phenomenon to remember; it is the same attentional vulnerability under worse conditions.

**The practical bedside discriminators:**
- **Test attention, not memory.** Attention is what delirium impairs first and most, for the reason above; memory testing is slower and less specific. Serial 7s, months of the year backwards, or digit span will separate delirium from dementia far more efficiently than a full cognitive battery — and the serial-7s point already noted in the Delirium entry above is really this principle in miniature.
- **Get a collateral history with a timeline.** "How was she two weeks ago?" does more diagnostic work than any bedside test. An acute change over days is delirium; a slow decline over years is dementia; a change over weeks with low mood and anhedonia is depression.
- **Screening tools are for screening.** The tools in [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Cognitive Screening Tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS) measure impairment, not its cause — a low MMSE is equally consistent with all three, and in delirium the fluctuating course means a single score can mislead in either direction.

> [!info] **Depressive pseudodementia** — depression in an older person presenting as cognitive impairment, and a genuine diagnostic trap because the cognitive deficits are real, not feigned. Suggestive features: the patient complains of memory loss more than their family does (the reverse of dementia), the onset is relatively abrupt and datable, effort on testing is poor with "I don't know" answers rather than confabulated wrong ones, and low mood and anhedonia are prominent. **The cognitive impairment improves with treatment of the depression** — which is precisely why it must not be labelled dementia and left. The honest caveat: late-life depression can also be an early manifestation of an emerging dementia rather than a purely reversible mimic, so treat the depression and then **reassess cognition once mood has responded**, rather than treating a single improved score as having settled the question permanently.

**Why this matters beyond the diagnosis:** delirium is a marker of acute illness and demands a search for the precipitant (see Delirium above); it also predicts falls, longer admission, functional decline and institutionalisation (see [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Falls in Older People). Getting the label wrong sends the patient down the wrong pathway entirely — a missed delirium means a missed sepsis, a missed drug effect, or a missed urinary retention.

## Serotonin Syndrome and Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)

> [!note] Gap-filled — genuinely absent from any file despite being one of the classic contrasting-pair topics in medicine (both cause hyperthermia + altered mental status + muscle rigidity from different drug mechanisms, and distinguishing them is a frequently-tested skill), and despite haloperidol/antipsychotic use for delirium being covered immediately above, making NMS a directly relevant differential. Built together as a comparison, in the same spirit as the Warm vs Cold AIHA and Pemphigus vs Bullous Pemphigoid comparisons elsewhere in this project.

Both are toxidromes causing the triad of **hyperthermia, altered mental status, and neuromuscular abnormality** — but with different causative drug classes, timing, and specific neuromuscular findings, which is exactly what makes distinguishing them a classic exam skill.

| | Serotonin syndrome | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) |
|---|---|---|
| **A/P** | Excess serotonergic activity — typically from combining serotonergic drugs (e.g. an SSRI/SNRI plus an MAOI, tramadol, or another serotonergic agent), or from a single-drug overdose | Dopamine receptor blockade — typically from antipsychotics (both typical and atypical, including haloperidol used for delirium above), or from abrupt withdrawal of a dopaminergic drug (e.g. levodopa in Parkinson's disease) |
| **Onset** | Rapid — typically within 24h of starting/increasing the causative drug or combination | Slower — typically days, sometimes 1–2 weeks after starting/increasing the causative antipsychotic |
| **Neuromuscular findings** | **Hyperreflexia and clonus** (particularly lower-limb, and particularly inducible/spontaneous clonus is a relatively specific finding), tremor, myoclonus | **"Lead-pipe" rigidity** (uniform, non-cogwheel rigidity throughout range of motion) with **reduced or normal reflexes** (a genuinely important distinguishing point from serotonin syndrome's hyperreflexia) |
| **Other features** | Autonomic instability (tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis, diarrhoea), agitation/confusion, dilated pupils | Autonomic instability (similar pattern), altered mental status ranging from confusion to stupor, diaphoresis |
| **Ix** | Clinical diagnosis (Hunter Serotonin Toxicity Criteria are sometimes used) — no specific confirmatory test; bloods may show raised CK if significant rigidity/agitation present, though typically less markedly elevated than in NMS | Clinical diagnosis; **CK is typically markedly elevated** (often >1000, sometimes much higher, reflecting more sustained rigidity) — a useful supporting distinguishing feature from serotonin syndrome's more modest CK elevation; raised WCC is also common, sometimes prompting an incorrect initial diagnosis of infection |
| **Mx** | **Immediate:** stop the causative drug(s) — the single most important step for both conditions; supportive care (cooling for hyperthermia, IV fluids, benzodiazepines for agitation/to reduce muscle activity and associated hyperthermia). **Definitive:** cyproheptadine (a serotonin antagonist) for moderate-severe cases not responding to supportive care alone. **Course:** typically resolves within 24–72h of stopping the causative drug, given the shorter onset also reflects a shorter, more self-limiting course once the drug is stopped | **Immediate:** stop the causative antipsychotic (or reinstate the dopaminergic drug if NMS was precipitated by abrupt withdrawal) — again the single most important step; supportive care (cooling, IV fluids, benzodiazepines). **Definitive:** dantrolene (a direct-acting skeletal muscle relaxant) or bromocriptine (a dopamine agonist, replacing the blocked dopaminergic tone) for severe cases. **Course:** typically slower to resolve than serotonin syndrome (days to over a week), consistent with its slower onset |
| **Complications if untreated** | Rhabdomyolysis, DIC, multi-organ failure — both conditions can be fatal if unrecognised and untreated, which is the underlying reason distinguishing and promptly treating either matters | Rhabdomyolysis (often more pronounced given the higher CK), acute kidney injury from myoglobin release, DIC, multi-organ failure |

> [!tip] The reflex/tone pattern is the single most useful bedside discriminator once the trigger-drug history has narrowed the differential: **hyperreflexia and clonus point toward serotonin syndrome; reduced reflexes with lead-pipe rigidity point toward NMS** — genuinely opposite findings despite both conditions sharing the hyperthermia-plus-altered-mental-status-plus-rigidity picture at a glance.

## Opioid Toxicity

> [!note] Gap-filled — mentioned in passing as a cause of Type II respiratory failure in [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] and as a naloxone-reversible cause of reduced consciousness in [[Examination]] ABCDE Assessment (Acutely Unwell / Deteriorating Patient), but never built as its own entry, despite the extensive opioid content already established in [[10_11c_Oncology_-_Palliative_Care_Prescribing]] (opioid conversion, dosing) that this connects to directly.

- **A/P:** excess opioid receptor agonism (μ-receptor predominantly) causing CNS and respiratory depression — can occur from prescribed opioid accumulation (e.g. in renal impairment, or rapid dose escalation without adequate tolerance), accidental or intentional overdose, or opioid use disorder-related overdose (particularly with illicit opioids of variable/unpredictable potency).
- **S/Smx — the classic opioid toxidrome triad:** **respiratory depression** (reduced rate and/or depth of breathing — the feature most directly responsible for mortality), **pinpoint (miotic) pupils**, and **reduced conscious level** — the combination of all three is highly suggestive, though any one feature alone has a broader differential. Other features: reduced bowel sounds/constipation, hypotension and bradycardia in severe toxicity, hypothermia.
- **Ix:** primarily a clinical diagnosis based on the toxidrome above and history/collateral information (empty pill packets, known opioid prescription, drug paraphernalia) — **do not delay treatment (naloxone) to await any test result** in a patient with respiratory depression, given this is a time-critical, reversible cause of death. Blood glucose (*why:* hypoglycaemia is a differential for reduced consciousness and should always be excluded alongside opioid toxicity, per the same general principle in [[Examination]] ABCDE Assessment (Acutely Unwell / Deteriorating Patient), not repeated here; *what:* excludes hypoglycaemia as a co-existing or alternative cause). ABG (*why:* confirms and quantifies the respiratory depression/hypercapnia — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] for the Type II respiratory failure pattern this produces, not repeated here; *what:* hypoxaemia with hypercapnia in significant toxicity).
- **Mx:**
  - **Immediate/acute:** **naloxone** (IV, IM, subcutaneous, or intranasal depending on setting and access) — a competitive opioid receptor antagonist that rapidly reverses respiratory depression; supportive airway/breathing management (per [[Examination]] ABCDE Assessment (Acutely Unwell / Deteriorating Patient)) while naloxone takes effect or if inadequately responsive.
  - **Definitive:** **naloxone's duration of action is often shorter than that of the causative opioid**, particularly long-acting or modified-release opioids — meaning **re-sedation after an initial response is a genuine, well-recognised risk**, and patients require a period of monitoring/observation after naloxone reversal, not single-dose treatment and immediate discharge; repeat naloxone dosing or an infusion may be needed for longer-acting opioids.
  - **Chronic/long-term:** for opioid use disorder-related overdose specifically, this is an opportunity for harm-reduction discussion and referral (opioid substitution therapy, take-home naloxone programs for the patient/family, drug and alcohol service referral) — genuinely important given overdose survivors remain at high risk of a future, potentially fatal, event without intervention.

> [!tip] Naloxone reversal in a patient physically dependent on opioids can precipitate acute, uncomfortable (though not typically life-threatening) opioid withdrawal — titrate the dose to reverse dangerous respiratory depression rather than to fully "wake the patient up," particularly in a known opioid-dependent patient, to avoid unnecessarily severe precipitated withdrawal while still achieving the safety-critical goal.

## Movement Disorders

### Parkinson's Disease (PD)

**D:** chronic progressive neurological disorder characterised by motor symptoms of resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability.

**R:** ↑age, M>F, family history, genetics, MPTP exposure

**A:** considered a sporadic disorder, with some genetic forms; environmental factors, oxidative stress.

**P:** selective loss of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta + Lewy bodies and neurites (composed of synuclein).

> [!info] S/Smx — mnemonic "TRAP"
> **T**remor (resting), **R**igidity (cogwheel), **A**kinesia/bradykinesia, **P**ostural instability. Others: mask-like facies, micrographia, depression (40%), sleep disorder. Drug-induced parkinsonism tends to be more symmetrical than idiopathic PD.

> [!tip] Clinical features
> Parkinsonian gait — slowed movement, ↓arm swing, rigidity, freezing, shuffling steps, postural instability; asymmetric resting tremor that improves on voluntary movements; mask-like face; glabella tap — patient cannot stop blinking.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis based on history and examination (*why:* PD remains a clinical diagnosis — no blood test or routine imaging confirms it; Ix is used to exclude alternative causes (e.g. drug-induced parkinsonism, Parkinson-plus syndromes — see below) rather than to positively diagnose PD; *what:* the TRAP features above, with asymmetry and a good initial levodopa response supporting idiopathic PD over the alternatives).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — chronic progressive disease managed via specialist titration over time):** referral to specialist. If motor symptoms affect QoL → levodopa. If motor symptoms don't affect QoL → dopamine agonist, levodopa, or MAO-B inhibitor. Risk of impulse control disorders with these medications — ensure patients know warning signs. Medication review to remove drug-drug interactions.

> [!warning] Risk factors for faster progression: older age at symptom onset, rigidity/bradykinesia as presenting symptoms, associated comorbidities, decreased response to dopaminergic medications.

**P:** after 5 years of levodopa treatment, motor complications develop; eventually symptoms such as freezing, falling, and dementia develop. Cognitive dysfunction and non-dopaminergic symptoms are the main determinants of increased disability in the first 5 years of disease.

### PD drug classes

**Levodopa:** precursor to dopamine. Usually combined with a decarboxylase inhibitor (e.g. carbidopa) to decrease peripheral metabolism → fewer side effects.
- SE: end-of-dose wearing off, on-off phenomenon (large variations in motor performance), dyskinesias at peak dose (dystonia, chorea, athetosis)

> [!danger] Do NOT abruptly stop levodopa. If a patient cannot take PO medications, a dopamine agonist (e.g. cabergoline) can be given as rescue medication.

**MAO-B inhibitors** (e.g. selegiline): not often used first-line. MOA — inhibits dopamine breakdown. SE: arrhythmias, anticholinergic effects, can potentiate levodopa effects (may need to reduce levodopa dose 10–30%). Avoid with cheese (tyramine reaction).

**Dopamine agonists** (e.g. ropinirole, apomorphine, cabergoline, bromocriptine): MOA — activate dopamine receptors. SE: increased risk of impulse control disorders; excessive daytime somnolence (do not drive/operate machinery); hallucinations in older patients; increased risk of pulmonary, retroperitoneal, and cardiac fibrosis with cabergoline and bromocriptine (baseline echo, CXR, ESR, creatinine; monitor closely).

**COMT inhibitors** (e.g. entacapone, tolcapone): MOA — inhibits dopamine breakdown; often used as adjunct to levodopa.

**Antimuscarinics** (e.g. procyclidine, benzatropine): MOA — blocks cholinergic receptors; often used to treat drug-induced parkinsonism.

> [!warning] Dopaminergic drug adverse effects: dyskinesia, on-off phenomenon, psychosis, ↓arterial BP, mouth dryness, insomnia, nausea/vomiting, excessive daytime sleepiness.

### Parkinson-Plus Syndromes

**D:** disorders with classic features of PD, plus distinguishing features.

**Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP):** rigidity and frequent falls; problems with balance and mobility; difficulty looking up/down and blinking (staring expression, double vision); behaviour change (apathy, impulsivity); dysarthria and dysphagia ("chaotic dysarthrophonia", less spontaneous speech); slowness of thought, irritability, mood changes. Named for the supranuclear gaze palsy causing restriction of voluntary eye movements in the vertical plane.

**Corticobasal Degeneration (CBD):** limb rigidity or akinesia, limb dystonia, limb myoclonus, orobuccal or limb apraxia, cortical sensory deficit, alien limb phenomena — usually asymmetrical, with cortical signs.

**Multiple System Atrophy (MSA):** autonomic failure (urinary incontinence, orthostatic hypotension within 3 min of standing — SBP drop ≥30 or DBP drop ≥15 mmHg); parkinsonian symptoms not responsive to levodopa; cerebellar syndrome (gait ± limb ataxia, dysarthria, oculomotor dysfunction).

### Motor Neuron Disease (MND)

**D:** group of rare neurodegenerative disorders that selectively affect motor neurons. Divided into 4 subtypes:
- ALS — 50% (see below)
- Primary lateral sclerosis: only UMN signs first; slower progression than ALS
- Progressive muscular atrophy: only LMN signs first; best prognosis
- Progressive bulbar palsy: worst prognosis

### Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

**D:** progressive disease characterised by degeneration of motor neurons with cortical, brainstem, and ventral cord locations.

**R:** genetics (≤7%), >40yo

**A:** unknown; various theories

**P:** progressive loss of cortical (frontotemporal), bulbar (pons, medulla), and ventral cord motor neurons → retrograde axonal degeneration → denervation and reinnervation in affected muscles.

**S/Smx:** UMN + LMN signs; clinical diagnosis. Asymmetrical limb involvement (70%), then spreads to a contiguous motor neuron segment (e.g. cervical → thoracic). UMN signs: weakness, spasticity, ↑reflexes (Babinski's, jaw jerk). LMN signs: weakness, atrophy, fasciculations (tongue fasciculations = bulbar onset MND). No remissions — progressive disease.

**Ix:** nerve conduction studies (*why:* demonstrates the LMN component and excludes mimics such as peripheral neuropathy or a treatable neuromuscular junction disorder; *what:* normal motor conduction velocity despite clinical weakness — a key distinguishing feature, since the pathology is at the anterior horn cell/motor neuron level, not the peripheral nerve itself). MRI (*why:* excludes structural mimics — cord compression or myelopathy can produce a similar UMN+LMN picture and must be ruled out before accepting an ALS diagnosis; *what:* typically normal in ALS itself, used to exclude alternative causes). ± LP (*why:* excludes an infective/inflammatory mimic when the diagnosis is less certain; *what:* normal in ALS, used to exclude infection).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — this is a progressive neurodegenerative disease managed via multidisciplinary care over time):** riluzole (reduces glutamate receptor stimulation — prolongs life ~3 months); BiPAP at night once respiratory function declines (survival benefit ~7 months); nutrition via PEG once swallowing is significantly affected — each of these interventions targets a different domain of progressive decline (pharmacological, respiratory, nutritional) rather than being alternatives to each other.
- **Chronic/long-term:** multidisciplinary care (physio, OT, speech pathology, palliative care involvement early given the progressive, ultimately terminal course); advance care planning discussions given the eventual respiratory failure that characterises disease progression.

**P:** mean survival 3–5 years. Prolonged survival with NIV (BiPAP), enteral nutrition, younger age at diagnosis, limb onset, baseline FVC >75%. Negative prognostic factors: older age, bulbar onset, frontotemporal dementia, FVC <75%.

### Huntington Disease

**D:** slowly progressive neurodegenerative disorder with specific symptoms.

**R:** expansion of the CAG repeat length at the N-terminal end of the huntingtin gene (≥40 copies), family history

**A:** autosomal dominant, trinucleotide repeat disorder

**P:** expanded CAG repeat generates an elongated polyglutamine tail on the huntingtin protein, leading to cleavage and generation of toxic fragments — primarily affects the striatum.

**S/Smx:** earliest symptoms relate to loss of executive function (↓concentration, increased errors in complex tasks, slips, misjudgments, needing more help). Behavioural symptoms (irritability, impulsivity, personality changes). Motor symptoms (chorea, twitching, rigidity, bradykinesia, saccadic eye movements). "Anticipation" — disease presents earlier in successive generations.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* the clinical picture in the context of a positive family history is often highly suggestive; *what:* the S/Smx pattern above). CAG testing (*why:* the definitive genetic diagnostic test, and central to genetic counselling for at-risk relatives given the autosomal dominant inheritance and anticipation phenomenon; *what:* confirms the pathogenic CAG repeat expansion, with repeat length correlating loosely with age of onset). MRI/CT (*why:* supports the diagnosis and excludes alternative causes of chorea; *what:* may show caudate/striatal atrophy in established disease, though normal early on).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — a chronic progressive disease):** counselling and supportive care (ensure good support system, including genetic counselling for at-risk family members given the inheritance pattern); behavioural symptoms — SSRIs, CBT; motor symptoms (specialist Mx) — tetrabenazine, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines.
- **Chronic/long-term:** staged care planning as the disease progresses through the stages below, with increasing focus on safety, dependency care, and eventually end-of-life planning.

**P:** ~20 years from diagnosis to death. Stage I: disability. Stage II: managing behaviour. Stage III: dependence. Stage IV: placement, end of life.

## CNS Infections

### Bacterial Meningitis

**D:** inflammation of the meninges by various microbes.

**R:** ↑age, crowding, exposure history, ↓immunity, brain defects (e.g. AV defects), cochlear implants, sickle cell disease

> [!info] Causative organisms by age
> 0–3mo: **Group B Streptococcus** (*S. agalactiae* — note this file also uses **GBS** for **Guillain-Barré syndrome**, so the organism is spelled out here), *E. coli*, *L. monocytogenes* | 3mo–6y: *N. meningitidis*, *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae* | 6–60y: *N. meningitidis*, *S. pneumoniae* | >60y: *S. pneumoniae*, *N. meningitidis*, *L. monocytogenes* | Immunosuppressed: *L. monocytogenes*

**P:** haematogenous or direct contiguous spread → rapid multiplication in the subarachnoid space → bacterial components in CSF trigger inflammation → influx of WCC → cerebral oedema, ↑ICP → damage, death.

**S/Smx:**
- Early: headache, neck stiffness, fever, altered consciousness
- Late (red flags): shock, sepsis/rash, respiratory or cardiac compromise, bleeding, raised ICP (focal neuro signs, papilloedema, seizures, ↓GCS)

> [!tip] Kernig's sign (11%): pain in lower back/thigh on knee extension when hip is flexed to 90°. Brudzinski's sign (9%): forced neck flexion elicits reflex hip flexion.

> [!info] Verified against an Australian-authored review (Young et al., Internal Medicine Journal — "Meningitis in adults: diagnosis and management"), Aug 2026 — the overall pathway and drug classes below are essentially consistent with Australian practice (Australian, UK, and European guidelines share the same broad approach, in contrast to North American guidelines which add vancomycin routinely for all patients). Adding specific AU-sourced doses not in the original UK-style note.
> **Ix + Mx pathway:**
> - If patient presents at GP, send to hospital ASAP; give IM benzylpenicillin if this does not delay transfer — **1200mg for anyone ≥10 years**, per the age-banded table in [[15_02_Paeds_-_Ill_and_Feverish_Child__Meningitis__Encephalitis]] Giving IM benzylpenicillin before hospital admission, which is the corpus's owner for this dose and includes the adult band. **This entry named the drug with no dose** until the G22 round (2026-08-29).
> - ABCDE + senior review if red flags (e.g. GCS <12, RR <8 or >30)
> - Decide on LP: delay if rapidly deteriorating, very unstable (e.g. severe sepsis), or signs of raised ICP (papilloedema, GCS ≤12, focal neuro signs, seizures); if LP cannot be done within 1 hour, give IV antibiotics after blood cultures
> - **Antibiotics + IV dexamethasone + fluids** (for the criteria that justify dexamethasone — frankly purulent CSF, CSF WBC >1000, protein >1g/L, or bacteria on Gram stain — see [[15_02_Paeds_-_Ill_and_Feverish_Child__Meningitis__Encephalitis]] Meningitis, which states them and this entry did not)**:** empirical ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h (base regimen — consistent with the age range already in the UK-sourced note); **vancomycin is only added if there's a higher suspicion of pneumococcal meningitis or reduced ceftriaxone susceptibility** (known/suspected otitis media or sinusitis, Gram-positive cocci on CSF Gram stain, positive pneumococcal antigen test) — not routinely added for every patient; **benzylpenicillin 2.4g IV q4h is added for Listeria coverage** in patients with Listeria risk factors (age <3mo or >50yo, immunocompromise) — consistent with the UK-sourced age thresholds already here. Dexamethasone 10mg IV q6h is a routine, guideline-supported component given its proven mortality/morbidity benefit specifically in pneumococcal meningitis.
> - Contacts exposed within 7 days before symptom onset: PO ciprofloxacin (alternative: rifampicin); offer meningococcal vaccination if confirmed; no prophylaxis for pneumococcal meningitis unless a cluster of cases — not individually re-verified against an Australian public health unit source this session; the general principle is standard, but check current state/territory public health guidance for the specific first-line prophylactic agent.

**Ix (why + what, expanding on the pathway above):** LP with CSF analysis (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test, identifying the causative organism and guiding antibiotic rationalisation once results return — see the CSF Interpretation table further down this file for the bacterial pattern; *what:* neutrophilic pleocytosis, raised protein, reduced glucose/CSF:plasma glucose ratio, positive Gram stain/culture). Blood cultures (*why:* identifies the organism when LP is delayed or contraindicated, and supports diagnosis even without CSF culture; *what:* may be positive, particularly for meningococcal/pneumococcal disease). FBC, CRP (*why:* supports the inflammatory/infective picture and can be trended; *what:* leucocytosis, elevated CRP). Coagulation profile (*why:* screens for DIC, a recognised complication especially of meningococcal disease; *what:* may show coagulopathy in severe/fulminant disease).

**Complications:** sensorineural hearing loss (25–35% after pneumococcal meningitis, 5–10% after Hib), seizures, focal neurological deficit, cognitive/behavioural problems, sepsis/abscess, raised pressure (herniation, hydrocephalus), Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome (adrenal insufficiency secondary to adrenal haemorrhage, especially with meningococcal meningitis).

> [!note] *Cryptococcus neoformans* stains with India ink.

### Viral Meningitis

**D:** inflammation of the meninges by a virus.

**R:** very young or old, immunocompromised, summer/autumn, unvaccinated for mumps, IVDU

**A:** non-polio enteroviruses (e.g. coxsackievirus, echovirus), mumps, HSV, CMV, HZV/VZV, HIV, measles

**S/Smx:** presents similarly to bacterial meningitis, possibly less severe.

**Ix:** exclude bacterial meningitis — LP (*why:* distinguishes viral from bacterial meningitis, since the two are clinically similar but require very different Mx; *what:* lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal/mildly elevated protein, normal glucose — see CSF Interpretation table further down this file for the full comparison), PCR (*why:* identifies the specific viral cause, particularly important to identify/exclude HSV given the specific antiviral implications; *what:* may identify enterovirus, HSV, VZV, or other causative virus).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** supportive generally; if HSV meningitis suspected, consider aciclovir; if bacterial meningitis or encephalitis suspected clinically, treat until proven otherwise (IV ceftriaxone and aciclovir) — don't wait for CSF results to start empirical treatment when the clinical picture doesn't clearly distinguish viral from bacterial disease.
- **Chronic/long-term:** none typically required — most cases are self-limiting.

**P:** generally good, but post-viral syndrome (headache, malaise) may persist for weeks. May recur.

### Encephalitis

**D:** inflammation of the brain parenchyma associated with neurological dysfunction.

**R:** <1yo or >65yo, immunocompromised, post-infection, blood/fluid exposure, organ transplant, animal/insect bites, geographic location, season, swimming in certain waters

**A:** causative agent found in only 50%; viruses, bacteria, immune-mediated causes.

**P:** similar to meningitis but involving the brain parenchyma.

> [!info] Diagnostic criteria (1 major + ≥3 minor for probable encephalitis)
> Major: altered mental status lasting ≥24h with no alternative cause identified. Minor: fever ≥38°C within 72h before/after presentation; partial/generalised seizures; new-onset focal neurological findings; CSF WCC ≥5; positive neuroimaging findings; positive EEG findings

**Ix:** LP (*why:* essential for diagnosis and to identify the causative organism via PCR, which directly guides antiviral therapy; *what:* CSF — ↑WCC, ↑protein + PCR for HSV, VZV, enteroviruses). Neuroimaging — MRI better than CT (*why:* identifies the pattern of parenchymal involvement, supporting the diagnosis and sometimes suggesting a specific cause (e.g. temporal lobe changes in HSV encephalitis — see the note below); *what:* normal in 1/3 of patients; may show medial temporal and inferior frontal changes). EEG (*why:* supports the diagnosis (one of the minor criteria above) and screens for subclinical seizure activity; *what:* lateralised periodic discharges at 2Hz — a pattern particularly associated with HSV encephalitis). Bloods + blood cultures (*why:* screens for a systemic infective source and baseline; *what:* may be unremarkable or show evidence of the underlying infective/systemic process).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** hospitalisation + isolation; **IV aciclovir 10mg/kg every 8 hours if suspected encephalitis — start empirically without waiting for confirmatory PCR**, given HSV encephalitis causes irreversible damage rapidly and aciclovir is safe to start presumptively; supportive care (airway, fluids, analgesia). **Continue for 14–21 days if HSV is confirmed**, then repeat the LP to confirm the CSF is HSV-negative before stopping.

> [!note] **The dose and duration were absent from both encephalitis entries** — this one gave neither, and [[15_02_Paeds_-_Ill_and_Feverish_Child__Meningitis__Encephalitis]] Encephalitis gave the duration but no dose. **No IV aciclovir dose existed anywhere in the corpus**, which carried only oral regimens for cold sores, shingles and suppression. Added by the pairs audit (2026-08-29); see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B56**. That paediatric entry also owns the **encephalitis-vs-meningitis distinction** (meningeal irritation usually absent; seizures and focal signs more common) and the empirical **triple therapy** — ceftriaxone, clarithromycin and aciclovir — given while the cause is unknown.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation for any residual neurological deficit; ongoing seizure management if seizures occurred during the acute illness.

**P:** depends on aetiology; 12% mortality for infectious aetiology in England.

> [!note] Herpes simplex encephalitis — HSV-1 causes 95% of cases; characteristically affects temporal and inferior frontal lobes. Focal features of temporal lobe infection, e.g. aphasia. CT may show bilateral temporal lobe changes.

### Brain Abscess

**D:** suppurative collection of microbes within a gliotic capsule occurring within brain parenchyma — single or multiple.

**R:** infection elsewhere (especially sinusitis, otitis, dental procedure/infection, endocarditis), brain surgery, AVM, immunocompromise, DM

**A:** bacterial, fungal, or parasitic organisms (possibly polymicrobial)

**P:** organisms enter the brain via haematogenous spread or direct inoculation; most often originates in ischaemic white matter adjacent to the cortex.
1. Early cerebritis (~3 days): local inflammation, necrosis, neutrophilic infiltration, microglial/astrocyte activation
2. Late cerebritis (days 4–9): lymphocytic and microglial infiltration
3. Frank abscess formation (after day 10): encapsulation and suppuration

**S/Smx:** headache, signs of raised ICP, cranial nerve palsy, fever, focal neurological deficit.

**Ix:** bloods (FBC, CRP/ESR) (*why:* supports the inflammatory diagnosis, and the degree of elevation can help distinguish abscess from tumour, which can otherwise look similar on imaging; *what:* elevated inflammatory markers — ↑CRP/ESR favours abscess over tumour). Blood cultures (*why:* may identify the causative organism, particularly if haematogenous spread is the source; *what:* may be positive, especially with endocarditis-related seeding). Neuroimaging — MRI better than CT (*why:* the key diagnostic test, characterises the lesion and its stage; *what:* ring-enhancing lesion with surrounding oedema, evolving through the cerebritis-to-abscess stages described above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone and metronidazole) ASAP — empirical broad cover pending culture/drainage results; anticonvulsants if seizures occur; steroids only if patient unstable (raised ICP causing mass effect) — not routine, since steroids can theoretically reduce antibiotic penetration and are reserved for genuine clinical need.
- **Definitive:** surgical drainage once confirmed and the collection has organised enough to be amenable to drainage (early cerebritis is typically treated medically, since there's no discrete collection yet to drain).
- **Chronic/long-term:** prolonged antibiotic course (often several weeks) with imaging follow-up to confirm resolution; address the underlying source (e.g. dental/sinus/ear infection) to prevent recurrence.

**P:** permanent hemiparesis and long-term seizures in <50%; mortality <13%. Main prognostic factor is the patient's neurological status at presentation.

### Spinal Epidural Abscess

**D:** inflammation with pus within the epidural space.

**R:** IVDU, recent surgery/trauma, indwelling catheter, immunocompromise, DM, concomitant bacteraemia or endocarditis

**A:** haematogenous spread (up to 50% of cases), contiguous spread (30%), direct inoculation (surgery, trauma)

**P:** the epidural venous plexus (valveless veins running from foramen magnum to sacrum) and lymphatics allow bacterial spread. Neurological impairment secondary to vascular compromise of the spinal cord/roots and direct pressure on tissue.

> [!info] Clinical stages
> (1) Localised muscle/bone pain (2) Radicular pain and paraesthesias (3) Muscle weakness, sensory loss, sphincter dysfunction (4) Paralysis

**S/Smx:** classic triad of focal back pain, fever, and neurological deficit present in only 10%. **The usual organism is *S. aureus*** — named in [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Spinal epidural abscess and absent here until the pairs audit (2026-08-29); that entry owns the antimicrobial approach.

**Ix:** bloods (FBC, CRP/ESR) (*why:* supports the inflammatory diagnosis and can be trended; *what:* elevated inflammatory markers). Blood cultures (*why:* identifies the causative organism, especially relevant given the frequent haematogenous source; *what:* often positive, most commonly *S. aureus*). MRI spine (*why:* the definitive diagnostic and localising test — essential for surgical planning and to assess the degree of cord/root compression; *what:* epidural collection with cord/root compression, extent defining the urgency of surgical intervention).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics started promptly (don't delay for culture results given the risk of rapidly progressive paralysis) → culture-directed once results available; supportive therapy (manage hypotension, DVT prevention given reduced mobility).
- **Definitive:** surgical decompression for patients with neurological deficit or failure to improve on antibiotics alone — timing is critical, since paralysis lasting ≥24–36h is likely to persist (see Prognosis below), making this a genuine surgical emergency once deficit appears.
- **Chronic/long-term:** prolonged antibiotic course (typically several weeks) with clinical/imaging follow-up.

**P:** ~5% mortality; most important prognostic factor is the patient's neurological status at presentation. Paralysis lasting ≥24–36h is likely to persist.

> [!tip] Not all neck stiffness is meningitis
> **Meningeal** — meningitis (§CNS Infections above), **subarachnoid haemorrhage** developing over hours (§Strokes below), meningeal malignancy.
> **Raised intracranial pressure with tonsillar herniation** — neck stiffness with reduced conscious state and abnormal posturing. **Lumbar puncture here is dangerous**, per the CT-before-LP rule in §CNS Infections.
> **Cervical spine** — trauma with fracture or ligamentous injury; **atlantoaxial instability**, which matters in **rheumatoid arthritis and Down syndrome** (see [[15_20a_Paeds_-_Trisomies_and_Sex_Chromosome_Disorders]]); discitis or vertebral osteomyelitis; spondylosis; malignancy.
> **Deep neck space infection** — **retropharyngeal abscess** (a child who will not move the neck, drooling, unwell) and **Lemierre syndrome** (see [[13_05a_ENT_-_Sore_Throat_and_Tonsillitis]] and [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]]).
> **Muscular** — torticollis, myositis, cervical muscle spasm.
> **Acute dystonic reaction** — from **metoclopramide, prochlorperazine or antipsychotics**, with torticollis, oculogyric crisis and trismus. **Responds within minutes to an anticholinergic**, and is frequently misread as a psychiatric or neurological event. See [[NEW_Drugs_12_Gastrointestinal]] §0.2.2.
> **Tetanus** — trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonos, with a wound and incomplete immunisation.
> **Systemic** — tonsillitis and cervical lymphadenitis in children, and severe pneumonia (particularly upper lobe) causing **meningism without meningitis**.

> [!danger] In a child with neck stiffness, two must be excluded urgently
> **Meningitis** and **retropharyngeal abscess.** A child who holds the neck rigidly, refuses to move it, drools, or has a muffled voice needs urgent assessment of the **airway and deep neck spaces**, not simply a lumbar puncture. See [[15_02_Paeds_-_Ill_and_Feverish_Child__Meningitis__Encephalitis]].

**Ix:** directed by the differential — **LP where meningitis is suspected and it is safe**, **CT brain where raised pressure or subarachnoid haemorrhage is possible**, and **cervical spine imaging where there is trauma or focal spinal tenderness**. The order matters more than the list: imaging precedes the LP whenever raised intracranial pressure is a possibility.


## CNS Infections Associated with Immunosuppression

### Toxoplasmosis

**D:** infection by the protozoan parasite *Toxoplasma gondii*, usually symptomatic only in immunocompromised patients.

**R:** immunocompromise (HIV etc.), exposure during pregnancy (risk to child), ingestion of raw/undercooked meat, exposure to cat faeces

**A:** *Toxoplasma gondii* found in cat faeces → ingested → spreads haematogenously throughout the body.

**P:** 90% asymptomatic if immunocompetent. In immunocompromised patients, increased dissemination (especially neural and muscular tissues) → inflammation → symptoms.

**S/Smx:**
- Immunocompetent: resembles infectious mononucleosis — fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy; rarely meningoencephalitis and myocarditis
- Immunocompromised: headache, confusion, drowsiness, vision issues
- Toxoplasmic chorioretinitis: blurry vision, fundoscopic lesions
- Congenital: cerebral calcification, hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis, retinopathy, cataracts

**Ix:** serology (*why:* screens for exposure/infection, though a positive result mainly indicates past exposure and doesn't confirm active CNS disease in an immunocompromised patient — often used alongside imaging rather than alone; *what:* positive IgG indicates prior exposure, with reactivation being the mechanism of disease in immunocompromise rather than new infection). CT (*why:* the key diagnostic imaging test in a patient with new neurological symptoms and known immunocompromise/HIV risk; *what:* single or multiple ring-enhancing brain lesions, often involving basal ganglia ± mass effect — a pattern overlapping with primary CNS lymphoma below, sometimes distinguished by a therapeutic trial of anti-toxoplasma treatment with repeat imaging).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (immunosuppressed/congenital cases):** pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + calcium folinate (≥6-week course) — folinic acid co-administered to reduce pyrimethamine's marrow toxicity. Eye disease — steroids to reduce inflammation.
- **Definitive:** not needed in immunocompetent patients (self-limiting).
- **Chronic/long-term:** treat until CD4 >200 for ≥6 months (immune reconstitution allows safe cessation); prophylaxis for HIV patients with CD4 <100: cotrimoxazole (primary prevention before infection occurs, distinct from the treatment regimen above).

**P:** in immunocompromised patients, infection is lifelong; chronic suppressive therapy needed unless CD4 >200.

### Primary CNS Lymphoma

**D:** extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphoma confined to the brain, eyes, and CSF without evidence of systemic spread.

**R:** HIV + EBV, usually >60yo

**A/P:** various mutations; usually diffuse large B-cell lymphoma histology.

**S/Smx:** focal neurological deficits, altered mental status, behavioural changes, raised ICP (headache, nausea/vomiting, papilloedema), seizures.

**Ix:** CT/MRI (*why:* the initial imaging test, though the appearance can overlap with toxoplasmosis (see above) in an HIV-positive patient — this overlap is a genuine diagnostic challenge worth knowing; *what:* homogeneously enhancing mass lesion, most commonly brain, less frequently eyes/spine). PET scan (*why:* screens for systemic (non-CNS) disease, which would reclassify this as secondary rather than primary CNS lymphoma and change Mx; *what:* absence of systemic disease supports the primary CNS diagnosis). Diagnostic procedure — stereotactic biopsy, vitrectomy, or CSF cytology (*why:* tissue/cytological diagnosis is required for definitive confirmation, since imaging alone can't reliably distinguish lymphoma from toxoplasmosis or other mass lesions; *what:* confirms diffuse large B-cell lymphoma histology).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — this is managed as an oncological diagnosis via a treatment protocol, not an acute emergency Mx pathway):** high-dose methotrexate + rituximab ± other chemotherapy ± radiation. Surgery of limited use (unlike many other brain tumours, since lymphoma is chemosensitive and surgical resection doesn't improve outcomes the way it might for other lesions).
- **Chronic/long-term:** close monitoring for relapse given the significant relapse rate (see Prognosis).

**P:** 10–15% primary refractory, 50% of patients relapse → poor prognosis, median survival of 2 months without added treatment. Median time to relapse is 10–18 months; most relapses occur within 2 years of initial diagnosis.

### Cryptococcosis

**D:** opportunistic fungal infection caused by *Cryptococcus* species.

**R:** HIV (80% of cases; CD4 <100), immunosuppression, comorbidities, smoking

**A/P:** in immunosuppressed patients, majority caused by *Cryptococcus neoformans*, usually starting in the lungs.
1. Dry yeast cells inhaled and deposited in lungs
2. Alveolar macrophages release cytokines/chemokines to recruit inflammatory cells
3. Th1 response → granulomatous inflammation; haematogenous dissemination to multiple organs

**S/Smx:** symptom onset 1–2 weeks in HIV. Pulmonary infection: fever, productive cough, dyspnoea, chest pain, weight loss. CNS involvement: headache, fever, cranial nerve palsies, change in mental status over days.

**Ix:** cryptococcal polysaccharide antigen in serum/CSF/pleural fluid (*why:* a highly sensitive and specific rapid test, often the first confirmatory test used given its speed compared to culture; *what:* positive antigen confirms the diagnosis). Cultures (*why:* confirms the diagnosis and allows susceptibility testing; *what:* may take longer to grow than the antigen test takes to return, hence the antigen test's practical importance). HIV antibodies (*why:* cryptococcal CNS disease is strongly associated with HIV, so testing is essential both diagnostically and for planning concurrent ART; *what:* screens for the underlying immunosuppressive cause). CXR (*why:* screens for the pulmonary source/co-existing pulmonary disease; *what:* may show pulmonary infiltrates/nodules). LP (*why:* essential to assess for CNS involvement and, critically, to measure opening pressure — raised ICP is a major driver of morbidity/mortality in cryptococcal meningitis specifically and needs active management; *what:* often markedly raised opening pressure, positive India ink stain/antigen — see the note on this above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** antifungal therapy (fluconazole for milder disease; amphotericin B for CNS/severe disease, often combined with flucytosine per current international practice); if relapse/refractory, amphotericin B. Manage raised ICP actively (repeated therapeutic LPs to reduce opening pressure — a distinct and important part of Mx given how much raised ICP contributes to mortality in this specific infection).
- **Definitive/chronic:** if HIV-positive, start ART — but timing matters, since starting ART too early relative to antifungal treatment risks immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), so this is usually deferred by some weeks after antifungal treatment begins.

**P:** 70% fatal if untreated; 20–30% fatal in high-income countries.

### Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML)

**D:** devastating CNS infection caused by the JC virus (JCV).

**R:** immunocompromise (especially HIV, but also drug-induced)

**A/P:** JCV establishes an asymptomatic, lifelong persistent/latent infection in the general population. In immunocompromise, JCV can reactivate and cause lytic infection of CNS glial cells (demyelination) → PML.

> [!note] "Classic PML" triad: multifocal demyelination, oligodendroglia with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei, and enlarged bizarre astrocytes with irregularly lobulated nuclei.

**S/Smx:** multifocal neurological symptoms, altered mental status, blindness, aphasia, progressive cranial nerve dysfunction, motor or sensory decline, ultimately coma. Rarely, seizures.

**Ix:** brain biopsy is definitive (*why:* the gold-standard diagnostic test, though invasive and not always performed if the clinical/imaging picture plus a positive CSF PCR is sufficiently convincing; *what:* the classic triad described above). PCR from CSF (*why:* a less invasive alternative first-line test, though sensitivity is imperfect so a negative result doesn't fully exclude PML; *what:* detects JCV DNA in CSF, supporting the diagnosis when positive).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no specific antiviral cure exists — Mx centres on restoring immune function):** direct antiviral therapies or boosting antiviral immune response — the mainstay is actually reversing the underlying immunosuppression where possible (e.g. ART in HIV, or stopping/reducing an implicated immunosuppressive drug) since there's no reliably effective direct anti-JCV therapy; watch for immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) as immune function recovers, which can paradoxically worsen symptoms initially.

**P:** if ART started early, better chance of improvement, but 70% have residual neurological disability. JCV persists in the host.

A simpler, faster alternative to GCS used in rapid initial assessment (e.g. primary survey/triage) rather than detailed ongoing neurological monitoring: **A**lert, responds to **V**oice, responds to **P**ain, **U**nresponsive. > [!info] Gap-filled from CSV ("Loss or altered conscious state," Medium yield) — the GCS/AVPU tools above score conscious state, but nothing covered the actual differential for *why* it's reduced. **Causes of an altered/reduced conscious state — mnemonic "AEIOU-TIPS":**
> - **A**lcohol (intoxication or withdrawal)
> - **E**pilepsy (post-ictal state) / **E**lectrolytes (hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, and others)
> - **I**nsulin (hypo- or hyperglycaemia)
> - **O**pioids/**O**verdose (any sedating drug or toxin)
> - **U**raemia (and other metabolic causes — hepatic encephalopathy, hypothyroidism)
> - **T**rauma / **T**emperature (hypo- or hyperthermia)
> - **I**nfection (meningitis, encephalitis, sepsis-associated encephalopathy)
> - **P**sychiatric (rarely a true cause of reduced GCS — consider once organic causes are reasonably excluded, not as a default explanation)
> - **S**troke/**S**AH/**S**pace-occupying lesion (and other structural causes — raised ICP, hydrocephalus)
>
> **Practical approach:** a bedside glucose and this differential should be run through systematically for any patient with reduced conscious state of unclear cause — hypoglycaemia specifically is rapidly reversible and must never be missed, which is precisely why capillary glucose is checked immediately in any collapsed or confused patient regardless of what else seems obvious. Also always consider the **specific reversal agents** available for two treatable causes: naloxone for suspected opioid toxicity, and IV thiamine before/with glucose in a patient with possible alcohol-related nutritional deficiency (to avoid precipitating Wernicke's encephalopathy — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ArLD) for the specific AU-verified thiamine dosing, not repeated here).

### Added from unverified layer — structural versus metabolic, and two transient amnesias
`SRC:D2_Altered_Consciousness_and_Cognition §0.1` `SRC:D2_Altered_Consciousness_and_Cognition §0.5` `UNVERIFIED — model knowledge, not source-checked.`

> [!tip] Structural versus metabolic — the pattern that separates them
> The GCS above records **how impaired** consciousness is. This is the question of **why**, and it splits before any investigation returns.
> **Structural** — **focal** and **asymmetrical**. A lateralising motor deficit, asymmetric pupils, gaze deviation, or a progressive deterioration following an anatomical sequence. Imaging is urgent.
> **Metabolic and toxic** — **symmetrical**, with **pupils preserved and reactive** (the striking exception being opioids, which constrict them), often with tremor, myoclonus or asterixis, and a level of consciousness that **fluctuates** rather than marching downward.
> **Preserved, symmetrical, reactive pupils in a deeply unconscious patient point away from a structural cause** — which is the single most useful bedside observation in this assessment.

> [!warning] Transient global amnesia
> **Abrupt, isolated anterograde amnesia lasting hours and resolving completely**, in a patient who is otherwise alert, oriented to self, and neurologically normal. The characteristic feature is **repetitive questioning** — asking the same question every few minutes, with no recollection of the answer.
> There is **no focal deficit and no alteration of consciousness**; if either is present this is not the diagnosis. Recovery is complete, and recurrence is uncommon.
> `UNVERIFIED — the diagnostic criteria, the recurrence rate, and what imaging is warranted before accepting the diagnosis; RACGP or a neurology reference.`

> [!warning] Transient epileptic amnesia
> **Brief, recurrent amnestic episodes**, often on waking, in an older patient — and, unlike transient global amnesia, **they repeat**. It is a temporal lobe epilepsy presenting as memory loss rather than as a recognisable seizure, and it is **treatable**, which is why the distinction matters.
> **Recurrent amnestic episodes should prompt an EEG rather than reassurance.** See §Seizures and Epilepsy below and §Focal Seizures.
> `UNVERIFIED — diagnostic criteria and the yield of EEG in this setting; neurology guidance.`

> [!note] Not repeated here
> **Wernicke-Korsakoff** is the other major cause of an amnestic presentation and is covered in §Serotonin Syndrome and NMS above, in `03_Gastrointestinal` §0.6.1, and in `14a-1_Psych_-_Substance_Misuse`. **`TGA` in this vault usually means transposition of the great arteries, and sometimes the Therapeutic Goods Administration** — the abbreviation is deliberately not used above.


## Seizures and Epilepsy

**Seizure:** a transient occurrence of signs/symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain.

> [!info] Epilepsy diagnosis — must fulfil any of:
> 1. ≥2 unprovoked seizures occurring >24h apart
> 2. 1 unprovoked seizure and probability of further seizures after 2 unprovoked seizures occurring over 10 years, similar to general recurrence risk
> 3. Diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with both an incidence gap and a distinct, actionable treatment-quality gap — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **Epilepsy prevalence in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians is roughly double that of non-Indigenous Australians** (11 per 1,000 vs approximately half that rate, per national health survey data), and **hospital admission rates for seizures/epilepsy are around 5.6 times higher**. The disparity is even starker in acute presentations specifically: a major Far North Queensland study found Aboriginal patients comprised only ~13% of the hospital's catchment population, but **30% of ED presentations for seizure, 31% of epilepsy-related inpatient admissions, and 44% of status epilepticus admissions** — status epilepticus being the most acute, life-threatening seizure presentation, making this a genuinely severe over-representation in the highest-acuity category specifically.
> - **A genuinely distinct treatment-quality gap, not just an incidence/access gap**: research has specifically found **suboptimal anti-epileptic drug use is common among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients presenting to the emergency department with seizures** — this mirrors the same pattern already established elsewhere in this project (lung cancer surgery rates, renal transplant access, joint replacement access): Indigenous Australians are both more likely to develop a condition and less likely to receive optimal treatment for it once they do, two compounding gaps rather than one.
> - **Under-recognition and misdiagnosis are specifically flagged as contributing factors** — seizures may go unrecognised or be misdiagnosed, leading to inappropriate treatment, and unstable living circumstances can further compromise ongoing treatment adherence and follow-up. This is a genuine clinical vigilance point: a lower index of suspicion or a less thorough seizure history in this population risks compounding the disparity rather than closing it.
> - Elderly Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients specifically are a growing, high-risk group — epilepsy onset is generally more common in older age, and this population segment is projected to grow, meaning medication regimen complexity and lifestyle adjustment considerations in older patients matter increasingly rather than being a niche concern.

**Classification based on:** (1) where the seizure began in the brain, (2) level of awareness during the seizure, (3) other features of the seizure.

### Generalised Tonic-Clonic Seizures (GTCS)
1. Loss of consciousness
2. Tonic stiffening phase
3. Repetitive clonic jerking

Other symptoms: tongue biting, incontinence, irregular breathing + post-ictal confusion.

> [!info] Verified against TGA safety guidance and Epilepsy Action Australia, Aug 2026 — the underlying clinical rationale (valproate teratogenicity driving sex-specific first-line choice) is the same in Australia as in the UK, but **Australia has explicitly not adopted the UK's 2024-tightened regulatory mechanism** (mandatory pregnancy prevention programme with two-specialist sign-off before initiating/continuing valproate in women of childbearing potential) — this is a genuine, deliberate AU/UK divergence, not an oversight. Australian guidance (TGA Product Information) advises valproate should be avoided in women/people who can bear children unless alternatives are ineffective/not tolerated, with contraception advised while on treatment — but without the UK's mandatory formal programme and dual specialist sign-off. Some Australian epileptologists have specifically pushed back against importing the UK's stricter approach, citing cases of harm from inappropriately switching stable patients off valproate. The clinical drug-choice logic below (avoid valproate first-line in women of childbearing potential) still broadly holds and doesn't need correcting — but don't describe Australia as following "the same rules" as the UK's 2024 update; the underlying principle is shared, the regulatory process is not.
> **Mx (GTCS):** males — sodium valproate; females of childbearing potential — lamotrigine or levetiracetam (valproate avoided as first-line here due to teratogenicity/neurodevelopmental risk, per the above — not because of a mandatory programme requirement as in the UK)

### Generalised Seizure Subtypes
Tonic-clonic (= grand mal), tonic, clonic, typical absence (= petit mal), myoclonic (brief, rapid jerks), atonic (drop).

**Absence seizures:** involve loss of consciousness + patient stares into space (10–20s) and abruptly returns to normal.
> [!info] Mx: ethosuximide first-line regardless of sex (2nd line carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, zonisamide) — the same regardless-of-sex choice applies in Australia; ethosuximide isn't a valproate-alternative-driven choice the way GTCS/atonic Mx is, so the sex-based caveat doesn't materially apply here — no change needed from the source note beyond removing the unnecessary "verify" flag.

**Atonic seizures:** loss of consciousness + brief lapses in muscle tone (≤3 min) — patient drops to the ground.
> [!info] Mx: males — sodium valproate; females of childbearing potential — lamotrigine — same underlying valproate-avoidance rationale as GTCS above (see the box there for the AU-specific regulatory nuance); the drug choice itself is consistent with Australian practice.

### Focal Seizures
Start in a specific area of the brain, typically the temporal lobe; further divided by awareness and by motor vs non-motor features.

- **Temporal lobe seizures:** usually have an aura (e.g. rising epigastric sensation, experiential phenomena — deja vu/jamais vu, rarely hallucinations); seizures are typically automatisms lasting ~1 min (e.g. lip smacking, grabbing, plucking)
- **Frontal lobe seizures:** motor seizures — e.g. Jacksonian march (twitching starts on one side of the body and spreads proximally); post-ictal weakness (Todd's paresis = focal unilateral weakness, usually of the limbs)
- **Parietal lobe:** sensory seizures
- **Occipital lobe:** visual seizures (e.g. flashers)

> [!info] Mx (focal seizures): lamotrigine or levetiracetam first-line; carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, or zonisamide second-line — these drug class choices are internationally standard, including in Australia; no valproate-specific sex caveat applies here since valproate isn't the first-line choice for focal seizures in the first place.

### Added from unverified layer — current terminology, and two counselling points

> [!info] The current ILAE terms — the concept above, named
> §Focal Seizures already says focal seizures are divided **by awareness and by motor versus non-motor features**. The current names for that division:
> - **Focal aware** — awareness retained throughout. (Formerly *simple partial*.)
> - **Focal with impaired awareness** — awareness lost or reduced at any point. (Formerly *complex partial*.)
> - **Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic** — a focal onset that spreads. (Formerly *secondary generalised*.)
>

> [!danger] SUDEP — sudden unexpected death in epilepsy
> **Patients and families have a right to know it exists.** The main modifiable factors are **seizure control and medication adherence**, and the risk concentrates in **nocturnal generalised tonic-clonic seizures**.
> Avoiding the conversation does not protect anyone: it removes the strongest reason a patient has to take medication reliably.
> `UNVERIFIED — the incidence, and how Australian guidance recommends the conversation be framed and timed; Epilepsy Action Australia, or RACGP.`

- **Drug-resistant epilepsy is defined as failure of two appropriately chosen and tolerated antiseizure medications.** It matters because it is the trigger for **epilepsy surgery assessment**, referral for which is frequently delayed by years — see [[NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological]], which covers the referral itself.


### Febrile Convulsions
Children 6 months to 5 years old, ~3% of children; occurs early in a viral infection when temperature rises rapidly; brief, generalised tonic-clonic or tonic.

> [!warning] **This is a one-line definition and was the whole entry.** [[15_12a_Paeds_-_Epilepsy_Syndromes_and_Status_Epilepticus]] Febrile seizures/convulsions owns it, and carries the three things missing here that change what you do:
> - **Simple vs complex.** A *simple* febrile seizure is generalised, lasts **<15 minutes**, and occurs **once in 24 hours**. Focal onset, duration >15 minutes, or recurrence within 24 hours make it **complex** — which changes the risk and the threshold for investigation.
> - **Meningitis must be actively excluded** — the fever is the presenting feature of both, and this entry named neither the differential nor the work-up (source of infection, MSU, CXR, ENT swabs; avoid LP in the postictal period).
> - **Management**, including that a seizure lasting **>5 minutes is treated as status epilepticus** — see Status Epilepticus below in this file.
>
> Linked and summarised by the G27 round (2026-08-29). Both entries agree on the ~3% incidence and the seizure description.

### Alcohol Withdrawal Seizures
Peak incidence ~36h after last drink — **one point on a withdrawal timeline, not an isolated event.** **Delirium tremens peaks later, at 48–72h**, and is the phase that carries the mortality, so a patient who has had a withdrawal seizure at 36h is heading into the higher-risk window rather than past it. The full timeline, the CIWA-Ar/AWS severity scoring and the AU-verified regimen are owned by [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol withdrawal. Carried here by the G21 round (2026-08-29): this entry gave the seizure timing alone.

### Status Epilepticus

**D:** (1) a single seizure lasting >5 min, OR (2) ≥2 seizures within a 5-min period without the individual returning to baseline in between.

> [!danger] Medical emergency.

**Mx:**
- Airway: secure airway
- Breathing: high-flow oxygen
- Circulation: IV access if possible
- Disability: check blood glucose

> [!info] Verified — the overall structure (benzodiazepine first-line → second-line agent if ongoing at 5–10min → general anaesthesia if refractory beyond ~45min) reflects standard international practice, including the drug classes used in Australia (levetiracetam, phenytoin, and sodium valproate are all standard eTG/AMH second-line choices here) — no major structural correction needed, but note: buccal midazolam and PR diazepam (no-IV-access options) and IV lorazepam (IV-access option) are all used in Australia in the same roles as below; the specific repeat-timing and second-line agent choice are standard practice rather than a UK-specific protocol.
> Benzodiazepines: no IV access — PR diazepam or buccal midazolam; IV access — IV lorazepam; repeat once after 5–10 min. If >5–10 min, start second-line agent (e.g. IV levetiracetam, phenytoin infusion, or sodium valproate). If >45 min (refractory), general anaesthesia or phenobarbital.

## Anticonvulsants / Antiepileptics

> [!info] Verified — the adverse effect profiles, mechanisms, and monitoring principles below (LFT/FBC monitoring for valproate/carbamazepine, trough-level monitoring for phenytoin, Stevens-Johnson risk for lamotrigine/carbamazepine, CYP450 induction effects) are pharmacological properties of the drugs themselves, not jurisdiction-specific prescribing conventions — no change needed. The drug names/classes are all standard AMH/eTG choices in Australian practice. See the Seizures and Epilepsy section above for the AU-specific nuance on sodium valproate prescribing in women of childbearing potential (Australia has not adopted the UK's 2024 mandatory pregnancy-prevention-programme requirement, though the same underlying teratogenicity caution applies) — not repeated here.

**Sodium valproate:** modulates voltage-gated Na channels (binds inactivated Na channel, use-dependent blocking action).
- AE: CYP450 inhibitor, teratogenic (neural tube defects), liver damage, pancreatitis, GI upset, weight gain, hair loss (may regrow curly), tremor, ataxia, thrombocytopenia, hyponatraemia
- To stop, reduce dose over 4 weeks
- Monitoring: LFT and FBC (baseline and during first 6 months)
- Pregnancy: females on sodium valproate should be on contraception

**Lamotrigine:** Na channel blocker. AE: Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Monitoring: no specific requirements.

**Levetiracetam:** ?Ca channel blocker. AE: blood dyscrasias, QT prolongation. Monitoring: no specific requirements.

**Carbamazepine:** binds voltage-gated Na channels, increases refractory period.
- AE: P450 enzyme inducer (auto-inducer — may increase clearance of itself; seizures may return 3–4 weeks after starting/increasing dose, needing titration until this stops), dizziness, ataxia, drowsiness, headache, visual disturbances (especially diplopia), Stevens-Johnson syndrome, leukopenia, agranulocytosis, hyponatraemia (secondary to SIADH)
- Monitoring: LFT and FBC (no clear guidance on frequency)

**Phenytoin:** MOA similar to carbamazepine.
- AE: dizziness, ataxia, diplopia, nystagmus, slurred speech, dyskinesia, gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, coarsening facial features, megaloblastic anaemia (secondary to folate metabolism dysfunction), peripheral neuropathy, toxic epidermal necrolysis, hepatitis/liver failure, aplastic anaemia, teratogenic (cleft palate, congenital heart disease), CYP450 inducer
- Monitoring: check if starting/adjusting dose, suspected toxicity, or suspected non-adherence — measure trough (lowest) levels immediately before dose

## Psychogenic Non-Epileptic Seizures (PNES)

**D:** episodic events that appear similar to epileptic seizures but without abnormal EEG findings. Do not refer to as "pseudoseizures".

**R:** trauma (including PTSD), acute stressors, mood and personality disorders; can occur alongside epilepsy

**A/P:** poorly understood; previously thought of as a conversion disorder. Patients have actual structural neurological changes and should not be thought of as "making up" their seizures.

**S/Smx:** PNES appears similar to epileptic events, but some features point toward PNES: pelvic thrusting, family member with epilepsy, occurring only with others around, gradual onset, prolonged event (>10 min), forced eye closure during the seizure, crying during/after the seizure, remembering events during the seizure, lack of post-ictal confusion. PNES can still have features resembling epileptic seizures, including urinary incontinence and tongue biting.

**Ix:** EEG (*why:* the key distinguishing test — a normal EEG during a clinical event strongly supports PNES over epileptic seizure, given epileptic seizures should show ictal EEG abnormalities; *what:* normal EEG during the event itself, ideally captured on video telemetry). Video telemetry (*why:* combines EEG with video recording, allowing direct correlation between the clinical event and EEG trace — the most definitive diagnostic approach; *what:* captures a typical event with no EEG correlate). Serum prolactin (*why:* a supportive adjunct test — prolactin characteristically rises after a true epileptic (especially generalised tonic-clonic) seizure but not after PNES; *what:* ↑prolactin favours epileptic seizures, though a normal level doesn't exclude epilepsy since the rise can be missed if not timed correctly).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute** (at diagnosis): explain the diagnosis empathetically — how this is communicated matters significantly, given patients have often been previously told (incorrectly) they are "faking" their seizures; emphasise this is a real, involuntary condition with a different mechanism from epilepsy, not a fabrication.
- **Chronic/long-term:** no consensus on best treatment; CBT + treat comorbid psychiatric conditions (trauma, mood/personality disorders) — addressing the underlying psychological drivers rather than anti-epileptic medication, which has no role here unless genuine comorbid epilepsy is also present.

## Syncope

**D:** sudden and transient loss of consciousness associated with loss of postural tone, resolving spontaneously and completely without intervention.

**A:** divided into cardiac and non-cardiac causes

**P:** common end-point in syncopal syndromes is transient insufficiency of cerebral blood flow.

**Ix:** cardiovascular examination (*why:* screens for a structural/arrhythmic cardiac cause, which carries a worse prognosis than reflex syncope and needs active exclusion; *what:* murmurs, irregular pulse, or other signs of structural heart disease). Postural blood pressure measurement — lying-standing BP (*why:* directly tests for orthostatic hypotension as the cause, a common and easily-confirmed diagnosis; *what:* ↓SBP >20 or ↓DBP >10 is diagnostic of orthostatic hypotension). ECG for all patients (*why:* screens for arrhythmic and structural causes (long QT, pre-excitation, conduction disease, evidence of prior MI/structural disease) — a mandatory baseline test in any syncope work-up given how much a cardiac cause changes management/prognosis; *what:* may show conduction abnormalities, arrhythmia, or be normal). Other tests depend on clinical features (e.g. echo if structural heart disease suspected, tilt-table testing for suspected reflex syncope, ambulatory ECG monitoring if arrhythmia suspected but not captured on resting ECG).

### Cardiac syncope
- Arrhythmias: bradycardias (AV conduction, e.g. heart block — Stokes-Adams attack; sinus node dysfunction), tachycardias (supraventricular or ventricular)
- Structural: valvular heart disease, MI, HOCM
- Others: pulmonary embolism

> [!note] Stokes-Adams attack = transient LOC secondary to AV block causing decreased cardiac output.

### Non-cardiac syncope

**Reflex (neurally-mediated) syncope:** symptomatic hypotension resulting from neural reflex vasodilation and/or bradycardia.
- Vasovagal syncope: stimuli (emotional, pain, etc.) → vagal nerve stimulation → vasodilation & ↓BP → ↓cerebral perfusion → syncope
- Carotid sinus syndrome: pressure on carotid sinus → ↓HR, vasodilation, ↓BP
- Situational: cough, micturition, GI stimulation (e.g. swallowing, defecation)
- Reflex anoxic seizures (not a true seizure): syncope in response to pain/emotional stimuli; ?neurally-mediated transient asystole in children (6mo–3yo); child goes pale → drops to floor → rapid recovery; no long-term sequelae

**Orthostatic syncope:** dysautonomia (e.g. Parkinson's disease, diabetic neuropathy), hypovolaemia, drug-induced (e.g. β-blockers)

**Syncope secondary to anatomical problems:** subclavian steal syndrome (subclavian artery steals blood from vertebrobasilar circulation, reducing cerebral blood flow)

**Neurological causes:** SAH, traumatic brain injury, migraine (uncommon)

**Metabolic:** hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, hyperventilation + hypocapnia

**Mx of reflex syncope:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute Mx tier — episodes are self-resolving by definition):** patient education usually sufficient — prevent known triggers (e.g. good hydration), know prodromal symptoms, respond and try to abort (physical counter-pressure manoeuvres — squatting, arm tensing, leg crossing), standing training (progressively longer periods).
- **Pharmacological (for refractory/frequent episodes):** fludrocortisone (volume expansion), midodrine (α1-agonist).

> [!note] Presyncope — feeling of almost passing out without loss of consciousness; same causes as syncope. Cataplexy = sudden muscle weakness triggered by strong emotions (e.g. laughter).

## Brain Tumours

**Metastases:** most common type of brain tumour, most commonly from lung, breast, bowel, skin (melanoma), and renal cancers. Mx: radiotherapy, chemotherapy, palliation; surgery rarely an option.

**Glioblastoma multiforme:** 45% of primary brain/CNS tumours; 5-year survival only 5.5%. CT/MRI: lesions with central necrosis + vasogenic oedema. Histology: pleomorphic tumour cells around a necrotic centre. Mx: surgery + chemo ± RT (dexamethasone for swelling).

**Meningioma:** tumour originating from the meningeal layer of the brain/spinal cord. Majority benign, but can undergo malignant transformation (32–64% 5-year survival). 37% of primary brain/CNS tumours. R: neurofibromatosis type 2, radiation, hormonal therapy, family history. CT/MRI — 65% show a dural tail. Histology: spindle cells in concentric whorls ("onions") and calcified psammoma bodies. Mx: surgery + chemo ± RT; if asymptomatic, ?watch and wait.

**Vestibular schwannoma:** see entry under ENT.

> [!note] This entry covers **adult** brain tumours. The paediatric tumours are a different set — [[15_12b_Paeds_-_Brain_Tumours]] Brain tumours owns medulloblastoma, brainstem astrocytoma and the rest, organised by location. Added by the pairs audit (2026-08-29).

**Pilocytic astrocytoma:** malignancy of the astrocytes ("pilocytic" = hair-like). Most common brain cancer in children, usually slow-growing, good prognosis. Histology: Rosenthal fibres (corkscrew eosinophilic bundle). Mx: resection is usually curative.

**Medulloblastoma:** most common malignant brain tumour in children, very aggressive. Arises and spreads from the fourth ventricle; can spread to the whole CNS. Histology: small round cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratio, very invasive.

**Ependymoma:** malignancy of cells lining the ventricular system, commonly the 4th ventricle. Rare (1.7%). Histology: perivascular pseudorosettes.

**Oligodendroglioma:** malignancy of grey matter, commonly frontal lobes. Usually benign and slow-growing. Histology: "fried egg"-like microscopic calcifications.

**Haemangioblastoma:** vascular tumour occurring in brain, spinal cord, and retina. Associated with Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome (multiple tumours over lifetime). Histology: foam cells, high vascularity.

**Craniopharyngioma:** most common supratentorial brain tumour in children. Arises from remnants of Rathke's pouch (near the pituitary gland). Can cause bitemporal hemianopia due to location.

**Pituitary adenoma:** benign tumours of the pituitary gland; divided into secretory or non-secretory, and micro (<1cm) or macro (>1cm) adenomas. Secretory tumours cause symptoms of hormone excess (e.g. Cushing's from ↑ACTH, acromegaly from ↑GH). Classically causes bitemporal hemianopia via optic chiasm compression. Mx: hormonal ± surgical (transsphenoidal approach).

> [!info] Bitemporal hemianopia: lower quadrant loss = craniopharyngioma (tumour compresses from below/behind); upper quadrant loss = pituitary adenoma (tumour compresses from below).

> [!note] Unless otherwise noted, most brain tumours are treated with a combination of surgery, radiotherapy ± chemotherapy.

## Brain Lesion Localisation

**Parietal lobe lesions:** sensory inattention; apraxia (can understand but cannot perform commands); astereognosis (inability to discriminate shape/size by touch, cannot recognise objects by touch); inferior homonymous quadrantanopia; Gerstmann's syndrome.

**Occipital lobe lesions:** homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing; cortical blindness (eyes are fine but patient cannot see); visual agnosia (cannot recognise objects despite seeing them).

**Temporal lobe lesions:** Wernicke's aphasia (fluent speech but unrelated, word substitutions, neologisms); superior homonymous quadrantanopia; auditory agnosia (cannot recognise words heard); prosopagnosia (difficulty recognising faces).

**Frontal lobe lesions:** Broca's (expressive) aphasia (patient knows what to say but cannot convey it — non-fluent, laboured, slow speech); disinhibition; perseveration (insistent repetition); anosmia; inability to generate a list.

## Cranial Nerve Disorders and Vertigo

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Neurology category) — Bell's Palsy, Horner's Syndrome, and the vertigo cluster (BPPV, Vestibular Neuritis, Central vs Peripheral Vertigo) were not covered in the source notes; added here given their genuine teaching importance (Bell's palsy and Horner's syndrome are classic localising-sign topics, and vertigo is a common, high-yield presenting complaint).

### Bell's Palsy

**D:** acute, unilateral, idiopathic facial nerve (CN VII) palsy — a diagnosis of exclusion once other causes of facial weakness are ruled out.

**R:** pregnancy (especially third trimester/immediately postpartum), diabetes, upper respiratory tract infection, family history.

**A:** idiopathic, though HSV reactivation is suspected to play a role in many cases (analogous to the mechanism proposed for Ramsay Hunt syndrome, below).

**P:** inflammation and swelling of the facial nerve within the narrow bony facial canal → compression → conduction block/axonal injury.

**S/Smx:** unilateral facial weakness affecting **both the upper and lower face** (forehead involvement is the key distinguishing feature from a central/UMN cause — see below); onset over hours to 1–2 days; ± hyperacusis (stapedius muscle involvement), ± loss of taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue (chorda tympani involvement), ± reduced tear production, ± post-auricular pain preceding the weakness.

> [!info] Forehead-sparing is the key localising sign
> **UMN facial weakness** (e.g. stroke): forehead is **spared** — the patient can still wrinkle their forehead on the affected side — because the forehead has bilateral cortical representation. **LMN facial weakness** (Bell's palsy and other CN VII lesions): forehead is **also affected** — the patient cannot wrinkle their forehead on the affected side, since the entire nerve from the nucleus outward is involved. This single bedside sign is one of the highest-yield distinctions in clinical neurology.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* Bell's palsy is diagnosed on the characteristic history and exam findings, and is a diagnosis of exclusion; *what:* the forehead-involving LMN pattern above, with no other neurological signs). Exclude alternative causes if atypical features present — bilateral involvement, other cranial neuropathies, or a slowly progressive (rather than acute) course should prompt imaging/further work-up (MRI to exclude a structural cause, e.g. tumour compressing CN VII; serology if Lyme disease is a geographic/exposure consideration; vesicles in the ear canal would suggest Ramsay Hunt syndrome — VZV reactivation in the geniculate ganglion, causing a similar LMN facial palsy plus a vesicular rash and often more severe pain, requiring aciclovir + steroids rather than steroids alone).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** oral corticosteroids (e.g. prednisolone) started within 72h of onset — the single most evidence-supported intervention, improving the chance of full recovery; specific regimens used include prednisolone 25mg twice daily for 10 days, or 60mg once daily for 5 days followed by a 5-day taper (reducing by 10mg/day) — either is reasonable, given equivalence hasn't been clearly established between the two; eye care is essential given the risk of exposure keratopathy from impaired eyelid closure — lubricating eye drops/ointment, and consider taping the eye closed at night, given the cornea is now unprotected on the affected side.
- **Definitive:** most patients recover spontaneously over weeks to months; antivirals (aciclovir) added to steroids remain of uncertain additional benefit for straightforward Bell's palsy specifically, though used for suspected Ramsay Hunt syndrome given the confirmed viral aetiology there. If no improvement within 3 weeks, refer urgently to ENT (± plastics ± ophthalmology if eye problems persist) rather than continuing to watch and wait, given this timeframe suggests the diagnosis or expected course should be reviewed.
- **Chronic/long-term:** if incomplete recovery, referral to specialist facial nerve services; physiotherapy for facial rehabilitation; watch for and manage synkinesis (aberrant nerve regeneration causing unwanted associated movements) as a longer-term complication.

**P:** the majority of patients recover fully within 3–4 months (roughly 80%); a minority have residual moderate-to-severe weakness if untreated (~15%), or synkinesis. Poorer prognosis with complete (rather than partial) paralysis at onset, older age, and delayed treatment initiation. Recurrence occurs in a small minority (~7%).

> [!warning] Bell's palsy is a diagnosis of exclusion — refer to ENT or neurology (rather than continuing to treat as straightforward Bell's palsy) if: **bilateral** facial palsy (raises a broader differential — Lyme disease, Guillain-Barré syndrome, leukaemia, sarcoidosis, EBV, trauma, myasthenia gravis — none of which is "bilateral Bell's palsy," given Bell's palsy is by definition unilateral), or **recurrent** episodes (~7% of cases, itself atypical enough to warrant re-evaluation of the underlying cause).

> [!info] Recognised risk factors also include intranasal influenza vaccination and pregnancy (particularly the third trimester and immediate postpartum period, ~3× risk) and diabetes (~5× risk) — an association with COVID-19 has also been noted (see [[02_Respiratory]] Viral pneumonia/pneumonitis (including COVID-19) for the general COVID-19 entry, not repeated here), though the mechanism (as with the broader HSV-reactivation theory) remains incompletely understood.

### Horner's Syndrome

**D:** a clinical triad resulting from disruption of the sympathetic nerve supply to the eye/face, localising to one of three neuron levels along the sympathetic pathway.

**S/Smx (the classic triad, all ipsilateral to the lesion):** partial ptosis (drooping of the upper eyelid — partial, not complete, since sympathetic fibres only supply the accessory levator muscle, unlike the more complete ptosis of a CN III palsy); miosis (constricted pupil, from unopposed parasympathetic activity); anhidrosis (reduced sweating — present or absent depending on which of the three neurons is affected, since sweat fibres to the face branch off at different points along the pathway).

> [!info] Localising by associated features (three-neuron pathway)
> **1st-order neuron** (hypothalamus → brainstem → spinal cord C8–T2): associated with brainstem/spinal cord pathology (e.g. lateral medullary/Wallenberg syndrome — see the Arterial Territory Syndromes table above) — anhidrosis affects the whole ipsilateral body/face.
> **2nd-order neuron** (spinal cord → sympathetic chain → superior cervical ganglion, passing near the lung apex and subclavian vessels): associated with **Pancoast tumour** (apical lung cancer — a critical diagnosis not to miss in a smoker presenting with Horner's), brachial plexus trauma, thyroid/neck surgery, central line insertion — anhidrosis affects the face only.
> **3rd-order neuron** (superior cervical ganglion → along the internal carotid artery → eye): associated with carotid artery dissection (a critical diagnosis in a younger patient with sudden-onset Horner's ± neck pain — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] for carotid-related content), cluster headache (see above), cavernous sinus pathology — anhidrosis is typically absent, since sweat fibres to the face have already branched off before this point.

**Ix:** the priority is identifying the underlying cause given how variable (and sometimes life-threatening) it can be — CXR/CT chest (*why:* screens for Pancoast tumour, a critical and treatable-if-caught-early cause; *what:* apical lung mass). CT/MR angiogram of the neck (*why:* screens for carotid dissection, particularly urgent in a younger patient with acute-onset Horner's and neck pain, given the stroke risk from dissection; *what:* may show an intimal flap or narrowed true lumen). Apraclonidine or cocaine eye drops (*why:* a pharmacological test that can confirm the diagnosis and distinguish Horner's from other causes of anisocoria when the clinical picture is ambiguous; *what:* pupillary response pattern differs between the Horner's-affected and normal eye) — a specialist-ordered test, not routine first-line.

**Mx:** entirely dependent on the underlying cause identified above — Horner's syndrome itself is a sign, not a disease, so Mx targets the causative pathology (e.g. oncological management for Pancoast tumour, urgent anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy and vascular referral for carotid dissection).

### Vertigo (Peripheral vs Central, BPPV, Vestibular Neuritis)

**D:** an illusory sensation of movement (typically spinning) of oneself or the environment, reflecting a mismatch between vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive input.

> [!info] Peripheral vs central vertigo — the key bedside distinction
> **Peripheral** (inner ear/vestibular nerve): sudden onset, severe/intense, often with hearing loss/tinnitus (if the cochlea is involved), horizontal or horizontal-torsional nystagmus that **suppresses with visual fixation**, no other neurological signs, worsened by head movement. Much more common overall.
> **Central** (brainstem/cerebellum): can be more insidious, often less severe intensity but more constant, vertical or direction-changing nystagmus that does **not** suppress with fixation, associated neurological signs (diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, limb weakness — see the DANISH cerebellar signs above), less affected by head movement position. Red flags for a central cause include any of these associated neurological signs, and warrant urgent imaging.

**Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV):**
- **D:** the most common cause of peripheral vertigo — dislodged otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) within the semicircular canals (most often posterior canal) triggering false movement signals with head position change.
- **S/Smx:** brief (seconds, typically <1 minute) episodes of vertigo triggered specifically by head position change (rolling over in bed, looking up, bending down); no hearing loss or tinnitus.
- **Ix:** Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre (*why:* the diagnostic test — reproduces the vertigo and characteristic nystagmus by provoking the affected canal; *what:* a positive test shows torsional/upbeating nystagmus with a brief latency, fatiguing with repetition).
- **Mx:** Epley manoeuvre (*why/what:* a repositioning technique that moves the dislodged otoconia out of the semicircular canal — highly effective and typically curative in one or a few sessions, more effective than medication for this specific cause); vestibular sedatives (e.g. prochlorperazine) have a limited role, mainly for short-term symptomatic relief rather than definitive treatment, since they don't address the mechanical cause.

**Vestibular Neuritis (and Labyrinthitis):**
- **D:** presumed viral inflammation of the vestibular nerve (vestibular neuritis) or the labyrinth itself, the latter also affecting hearing (labyrinthitis = vestibular neuritis + hearing loss/tinnitus).
- **S/Smx:** acute, severe, continuous vertigo (unlike BPPV's brief positional episodes) lasting days, often with nausea/vomiting; no other neurological signs; hearing loss/tinnitus present in labyrinthitis but not vestibular neuritis specifically.
- **Ix:** clinical diagnosis; HINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) used by specialists to help distinguish from a central (posterior circulation stroke) cause in acute vestibular syndrome, given the two can otherwise look similar and the consequences of missing a stroke are severe.
- **Mx:** vestibular sedatives (e.g. prochlorperazine, betahistine) for short-term symptomatic relief in the acute phase only (prolonged use can impair central vestibular compensation and delay recovery); vestibular rehabilitation exercises to promote compensation once the acute phase settles; corticosteroids sometimes used though evidence is mixed.

#### Added from unverified layer — persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD)

**Chronic non-vertiginous dizziness and unsteadiness lasting months**, characteristically **worse when upright, worse with movement, and worse in visually complex environments** — supermarket aisles, crowds, scrolling on a phone, patterned carpets.

That is the link to the compensation point above: the same central adaptation that resolves vestibular neuritis becomes the problem when it does not switch off.

**It is a positive diagnosis, not "nothing wrong".** It responds to **vestibular rehabilitation, an SSRI or SNRI, and cognitive behavioural therapy**. Naming it is itself therapeutic — these patients have usually had extensive negative investigation and been told there is no cause.

## Strokes

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with a striking age-of-onset gap — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians experience stroke at substantially higher rates and at a much younger age than non-Indigenous Australians.** A remote-Australia healthcare network study found age-standardised stroke incidence roughly **3-fold higher** overall, with a median onset age of 54 — **17 years younger** than non-Aboriginal patients. The disparity is even starker in younger patients specifically: in Aboriginal patients aged under 55, ischaemic stroke incidence was **14-fold greater**, and intracerebral haemorrhage **19-fold greater**, than in non-Aboriginal patients of the same age — this isn't a modest shift in risk, it's a fundamentally different age-related risk profile.
> - **A genuinely actionable, current gap in screening guidance**: national guidelines currently recommend cardiovascular/stroke risk screening for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people from age 35 — but a 2018 Australian National University study found significant cardiovascular/stroke risk already present in people **under 35**, with the Stroke Foundation explicitly calling for screening to start earlier than current guidelines recommend. This mirrors the same "standard screening threshold doesn't capture true risk in this population" pattern already established for AUSDRISK in Type 2 Diabetes (see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), not repeated here) — a recurring theme where general-population screening ages/tools are demonstrably too conservative for this population's actual risk profile.
> - **This connects directly to content already established elsewhere in this project**: rheumatic heart disease (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.22a Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD), not repeated here) is itself a stroke risk factor via cardioembolic mechanism, and the broader cardiovascular risk burden described for CKD (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), not repeated here) and T2DM above compounds directly into this elevated stroke risk — these aren't separate, unconnected disparities, but overlapping and compounding ones in the same population.

### Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA)

**D:** transient episode of neurologic dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischaemia, without acute infarction ± symptoms lasting <24h.

**Mx:**
1. Aspirin 300mg STAT unless contraindicated (contraindicated in patients already on anticoagulation — CT head to exclude haemorrhage; or already on aspirin)
2. Arrange specialist review within 24h; if >1 TIA ("crescendo TIA") — admit; advise not to drive (see Austroads Driving Standards further down this file — 2 weeks minimum for a single TIA in a private driver, longer for crescendo TIA at clinician discretion)
3. At specialist review: MRI brain (more sensitive for small infarcts); urgent carotid Doppler unless not a candidate for endarterectomy

> [!info] Gap-filled — the ABCD² score is genuinely known and worth being able to name, but current Australian guidance carries a specific, important caution about relying on it in isolation. **ABCD² score:** **A**ge ≥60 (1 point), **B**lood pressure ≥140/90 at first assessment (1 point), **C**linical features (unilateral weakness = 2, speech disturbance without weakness = 1, other = 0), **D**uration (≥60min = 2, 10–59min = 1, <10min = 0), **D**iabetes (1 point) — total 0–7, historically used to stratify low (0–3), moderate (4–5), and high (6–7) risk of stroke in the days following a TIA. **The Australian Stroke Foundation's own Living Clinical Guidelines explicitly warn against using ABCD² in isolation to determine urgency of investigation** — a "low-risk" ABCD² score does not exclude high-risk features (atrial fibrillation, symptomatic carotid stenosis) and should never delay urgent assessment of a patient with a genuinely suspected TIA, given relying on the score alone has been shown to risk missing these treatable causes. **The practical, current-guideline-consistent takeaway: every suspected TIA gets urgent (within 24h) assessment regardless of ABCD² score** — the score can inform triage prioritisation in a busy service, but is not a tool for deciding who can safely wait.

**Secondary prevention:**

> [!info] Verified against the Stroke Foundation Australia and New Zealand Living Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management, Aug 2026 — Australian guidance has a materially different first step from the UK protocol below (short-term DAPT), though the remaining drug classes are broadly similar.
> **Antiplatelet:** the Australian Living Guidelines now give a **strong recommendation for dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel together) for the first 3 weeks after a minor stroke or TIA** — this is a specific, upgraded, time-limited recommendation not present in the UK-sourced clopidogrel-monotherapy-first approach below; after the 3-week DAPT period, transition to single antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel, or aspirin + dipyridamole if clopidogrel contraindicated) for long-term secondary prevention. Statin: as per the UK figure — no material AU-specific dose difference identified. BP control: antihypertensives as needed. If AF present and haemorrhage excluded: anticoagulation with warfarin or a DOAC (dabigatran, apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) — consistent with the UK approach, no material change.

### Ischaemic Stroke

**Ix:** non-contrast CT head to exclude haemorrhagic stroke.

**Acute Mx:** all patients — give 300mg aspirin if no bleed.

> [!info] Verified against the Stroke Foundation Australia and New Zealand Living Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management, Aug 2026 — Australian practice differs from the UK protocol below in drug choice (tenecteplase now prominent) and reflects a more recently updated evidence base; the time windows themselves are largely consistent.
> **IV thrombolysis:** **tenecteplase (0.25mg/kg, max 25mg) is now used as an alternative to alteplase (0.9mg/kg, max 90mg) within 4.5h of onset** — a genuinely Australian-endorsed option not mentioned in the UK-sourced note below, and often preferred for large vessel occlusion specifically. Time windows:
> - Within 4.5h of symptom onset — if haemorrhage definitively excluded + no contraindication (alteplase or tenecteplase)
> - Extended to **up to 9h** after last-known-well (or from the midpoint of sleep for wake-up strokes) if brain imaging shows the patient will benefit — consistent with the UK figure already here, no change needed to this specific window.
> - New (2024 update): **recommendation against thrombolysis in non-disabling ischaemic stroke** — not part of the UK-sourced note below.
> - Lower BP to ≤185/110 before thrombolysis — no AU-specific change to this threshold.
>
> **Thrombectomy:** for large vessel occlusion (internal carotid, proximal MCA/M1 segment, or tandem cervical+intracranial occlusion) — undertaken **between 6 and 24 hours** after last-known-well if CT perfusion or MRI shows salvageable brain tissue (this AU figure frames it as a single 6–24h window based on imaging-confirmed salvageable tissue, rather than the UK's separate "within 6h" vs "within 24h" tiering — practically similar in effect, but don't teach it as two hard-separated time-based categories in an AU context). Basilar artery occlusion — thrombectomy should be undertaken in selected patients. M2-segment MCA occlusion — may be considered based on individual patient/imaging factors, a more selective indication than proximal occlusions.

> [!info] Young <55yo: screen for thrombophilia, autoimmune disorders, or cardiac disorders (e.g. ASD).

> [!info] Rosier score for stroke
> −1 for LOC/syncope or seizure; +1 for each of: asymmetric facial weakness, asymmetric arm weakness, asymmetric leg weakness, speech disturbance, visual field defect. Stroke likely if Rosier score >0.

> [!info] Gap-filled from CSV — NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale), not covered in the source notes. A standardised 0–42 point scale quantifying stroke severity across 11 domains (level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor arm/leg, limb ataxia, sensory, language, dysarthria, extinction/inattention) — used to objectively grade severity at presentation, track change over time (e.g. improvement/deterioration after thrombolysis), and as part of eligibility criteria for thrombolysis/thrombectomy in clinical practice and trials. Higher scores indicate more severe stroke; a score is typically documented at baseline and repeated at intervals to monitor trajectory. Unlike the Rosier score above (a bedside screening tool asking "is this a stroke?"), NIHSS answers "how severe is this stroke?" once the diagnosis is already suspected/confirmed.

> [!danger] Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis
> Previous ICH, seizure at stroke onset, intracranial neoplasm, suspected SAH, stroke or TBI in preceding 3 months, LP in preceding 7 days, GI haemorrhage in preceding 3 weeks, active bleeding, pregnancy, oesophageal varices, uncontrolled HTN >200/120

> [!warning] Relative contraindications: concurrent anticoagulation (INR >1.7), haemorrhagic diathesis, active diabetic haemorrhagic retinopathy, suspected intracardiac thrombus, major surgery/trauma in preceding 2 weeks. Note also the 2024 AU guideline update's new consensus-based recommendation on dabigatran reversal prior to thrombolysis where relevant, not detailed further in this source.

**Secondary prevention:**
> [!info] Verified against the Stroke Foundation Australia and New Zealand Living Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management, Aug 2026 — as with TIA above, the short-term DAPT recommendation is the main AU-specific addition; remaining content is broadly consistent.
> **Antiplatelet:** as for TIA above — dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel) for the first 3 weeks after a minor ischaemic stroke is now a strong recommendation in current Australian guidance, then transitioning to single antiplatelet (clopidogrel first-line; aspirin + modified-release dipyridamole if clopidogrel contraindicated) for long-term prevention. Statin: as per the UK figure (started >48h after due to haemorrhagic transformation risk) — no material AU-specific change identified. Carotid artery endarterectomy: only if carotid stenosis >50–70%, performed within 7 days — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Carotid Artery Stenosis for the fuller AU-verified detail on this threshold and timing, not repeated here. If secondary to AF: start DOAC or warfarin 14 days after ischaemic stroke — no material AU-specific change identified.

### Haemorrhagic Stroke
Specifically referring to intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH).

**R:** HTN, ↑age, M>F, heavy alcohol use, illicit drug use, family history, blood/clotting disorders, sickle cell disease, anticoagulation, pregnancy

**A:** cerebral amyloid angiopathy and HTN can directly cause ICH. Secondary ICH: cerebral infarction or tumour, drug misuse (e.g. cocaine, methamphetamine), AVMs.

**P:** vascular rupture → bleeding into brain parenchyma → direct damage to brain tissue ± expanding haematoma causing mass effect.

**S/Smx:** similar to ischaemic stroke (clinically indistinguishable).

**Ix:** non-contrast CT head; once ICH confirmed, CT angiogram.

**Mx:**
- Stabilise patient (ABCDE) — consider endotracheal intubation
- Refer immediately to neuro and neurosurgery
- Rapid control of SBP if >150–220: aim for SBP ≤140, ensuring drop does not exceed 60mmHg within 1h `UNVERIFIED — **R1** all four figures here (the 150–220 trigger band, the ≤140 target, the 60mmHg maximum fall and the 1h window) against the Stroke Foundation Living Guidelines for Stroke Management, which is open and free. - Urgent reversal of anticoagulation
- Supportive care as necessary
- Definitive Mx if undertaken: coiling or craniotomy and clipping

| Category | Criteria |
|---|---|

### Added from unverified layer — stroke chameleons, and two things to examine for
`SRC:D3_Stroke_and_Focal_Neurological_Deficit §0.5` `SRC:D3 §0.6` `UNVERIFIED — model knowledge, not source-checked.`

> [!danger] Chameleons — strokes that do not look like strokes
> Stroke **mimics** are covered above and in §Weakness — Differential Diagnosis. The opposite error is less discussed and more costly, because the patient goes home.
> - **Isolated vertigo.** A posterior circulation stroke presenting as "dizziness" is the most consequential missed stroke, because these patients are discharged with a vestibular label. The **HINTS examination** (§Vertigo below, and [[Examination]]) distinguishes central from peripheral and is **more sensitive than early MRI** — early diffusion-weighted imaging misses a meaningful proportion of posterior circulation infarcts.
> - **Acute confusion or delirium**, particularly with **non-dominant hemisphere or thalamic** strokes. §Delirium above lists stroke among the precipitants; the point here is that the stroke may produce *no other sign at all*.
> - **Isolated dysarthria, or isolated monoparesis** — a single deficit is still a deficit.

> [!warning] Locked-in syndrome
> **Ventral pontine infarction.** The patient is **fully conscious and aware**, with **only vertical eye movement and blinking preserved**, and is **repeatedly assumed to be unconscious**.
> This is the reason to test **vertical gaze and blinking to command** in any apparently unresponsive patient before concluding that consciousness is absent — the GCS at §Glasgow Coma Scale scores what the patient can *demonstrate*, which in this syndrome is almost nothing.

- **Pronator drift** — with the arms outstretched, palms up, eyes closed, a subtly weak arm **pronates and drifts downward**. It detects a pyramidal weakness too mild to show on formal power testing, and costs seconds. See [[Examination]] for the neurological examination sequence it belongs to.


## Brain Bleeds

> [!info] Monro-Kellie doctrine: brain composition — parenchyma 80%, CSF 10%, blood 10%.

### Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH)

**D:** intracranial haemorrhage into the subarachnoid space ("on the surface of the brain"). Divided into traumatic and spontaneous.

**R:** HTN, smoking, family history, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease

**A:** intracranial (berry) aneurysm — 85%, associated with ADPKD, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, coarctation of the aorta; AVMs; pituitary apoplexy; infective aneurysms

**S/Smx:** headache — sudden onset "thunderclap", peak intensity within 1 min of onset, occipital location, "worst headache of my life" ± history of a less severe 'sentinel' headache in the weeks prior. Nausea and vomiting. Meningism (photophobia, neck stiffness). Seizures, coma. ECG changes if a large bleed — ST elevation.

**Ix:** non-contrast CT head — SAH shows as a bright white (blood) signal, typically in the basal cisterns and sulci; ideally done within 6h of onset.
- If CT head within 6h and normal → do not do LP
- If CT head >6h and normal → do LP (should be done ≥12h after symptom onset to allow development of xanthochromia; normal or raised opening pressure)
- If CT head confirms SAH, refer ASAP to neurosurgery → order CT angiogram to guide neurosurgical Mx, order bloods/G&S/cross-match to prepare for surgery

**Mx:**
- Stabilise patient (ABCDE)
- Nimodipine to prevent vasospasm
- Supportive: bed rest, analgesia, VTE prophylaxis
- Reversal of anticoagulation
- Definitive Mx if undertaken should be within 24h: coiling, or craniotomy and clipping by neurosurgeons

**Complications:**
- Rebleeding — in 10% of patients, most common in the first 12h of initial SAH; if suspected, repeat CT head; associated with high mortality
- Hydrocephalus — bleep neurosurgeons; temporarily treated with external ventricular drain; may need long-term ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt
- Vasospasm (delayed cerebral ischaemia) — usually 7–14 days after SAH; prevented with euvolaemia; may require vasopressor treatment
- Hyponatraemia — usually secondary to SIADH
- Seizures — due to brain parenchyma damage

**P:** poorer prognostic factors — reduced consciousness on admission, ↑age, increased amount of blood visible on CT head.

### Subdural Haematoma

**D:** collection of blood between the dura and arachnoid layer of the meninges. Divided into acute, subacute, chronic.

**R:** trauma, coagulopathy/anticoagulant use, >65yo (especially chronic), ± alcoholic

**A:** trauma most common (e.g. falls, shaken baby syndrome)

**P:** torsional forces disrupt the bridging cortical veins → bleeding into the dural venous sinuses. Elderly people and alcoholics at higher risk due to brain atrophy.

**S/Smx:** wide range depending on how the trauma was sustained. Can be asymptomatic. Confusion, decreased consciousness, neurological deficit. Acute: may present with other associated injuries or be picked up on CT scan ordered for head injury. Chronic: presents over weeks to months with slowly developing confusion, decreased consciousness, neuro deficit.

**Ix:** CT head (*why:* the diagnostic test, and the shape/density pattern both confirm the diagnosis and help estimate the age of the bleed, guiding urgency of Mx; *what:* sickle ("C")-shaped collection not limited by suture lines — since subdural blood can spread freely across the skull vault rather than being constrained by dural attachment points, unlike extradural bleeds below; acute — bright white signal (blood); chronic — hypodense (dark) compared to brain tissue ± mass effect (e.g. midline shift), as the haematoma liquefies and is progressively resorbed over time).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** ABCDE + resuscitate as for any head injury; reverse anticoagulation if applicable; ICP monitoring/management as needed for larger bleeds with mass effect.
- **Definitive:** conservative — watch and wait for small, asymptomatic collections; surgical — burr hole drainage (often sufficient for chronic SDH specifically, given its more liquid consistency) or decompressive craniectomy for larger/symptomatic acute collections.
- **Chronic/long-term:** monitor for recurrence, particularly in elderly/alcoholic patients where ongoing brain atrophy continues to predispose to further bleeds; address modifiable risk factors (anticoagulation review, falls risk assessment).

### Extradural (Epidural) Haematoma

**D:** collection of blood above the dural meninges — blood collects between the dura and skull.

**A:** trauma, typically a low-impact hit to the head or a fall. Collection tends to occur at the pterion (confluence of 4 skull bones) where the skull is thin and the middle meningeal artery sits underneath.

**S/Smx:**
> [!danger] Classically "talk and die": patient initially loses consciousness, then regains consciousness ("lucid interval") appearing to improve, then loses consciousness again due to expanding haematoma and brain herniation. If severe, can cause brain herniation → blown pupil due to compression of CN III.

**Ix:** CT head (*why:* the diagnostic test — essential and urgent given how rapidly an EDH can expand and deteriorate, particularly during the deceptive "lucid interval"; *what:* egg-shaped bright white lesion; blood movement limited by suture lines of the skull (also described as biconvex or lentiform shaped) — this shape distinction from SDH's sickle shape reflects the dura's firm attachment at suture lines, constraining extradural blood to a lens shape).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** careful monitoring is essential given patients may present during the deceptively reassuring lucid interval — a normal-appearing patient with a confirmed EDH still needs urgent definitive management, not discharge.
- **Definitive:** conservative if genuinely asymptomatic and small (with close monitoring); craniotomy and evacuation of haematoma for symptomatic/significant bleeds — this is a time-critical surgical emergency given the risk of rapid deterioration and herniation.

### Cerebral Aneurysm

**D:** focal abnormal dilation of the wall of an artery in the brain.

**R:** smoking (formation, growth, rupture), moderate-high alcohol consumption (rupture), family history (especially in siblings; ?autosomal dominant), previous SAH, heritable connective tissue disease (e.g. ADPKD, Ehlers-Danlos)

**A:** haemodynamically induced injury to the vascular wall.

**S/Smx:** can be asymptomatic, picked up incidentally on imaging. Headache (variable character). Uncommon (?secondary to mass effect of the aneurysm): seizures, nuchal rigidity, decreased consciousness, focal neuro deficit.

**Ix:** CT head (*why:* first-line screen, may show the aneurysm incidentally or identify complications if symptomatic; *what:* may show the aneurysm itself if large enough, or be normal for small unruptured aneurysms) + catheter-based angiogram or CT angiogram (*why:* the definitive test for characterising aneurysm size, morphology, and location — essential for the observation-vs-treatment decision below; *what:* defines the aneurysm precisely, feeding directly into the size/growth-based Mx decision).

**Mx:**
- Unruptured: case by case — choice of observation vs treatment undertaken by neurosurgeons and patient; if symptomatic, generally treated; if enlarging (>1mm/year), treat; small aneurysms (<7mm) can generally be observed
- Ruptured: see ICH or SAH — coiling or clipping

## Weakness — Differential Diagnosis

### No objective weakness
Most often presents as a global pattern, ?generalised fatigue. Causes: cardio-pulmonary disease, anaemia, chronic infection (e.g. TB), malignancy, depression, deconditioning, arthritis, fibromyalgia, endocrine disease.

### With objective weakness — perform neurological exam

**UMN signs (↑tone, ↑reflexes):**
- Unilateral: intracranial haemorrhage, brainstem stroke, spinal cord stroke (all acute-onset)
- Bilateral: multiple sclerosis, motor neuron disease, myelopathy (disc prolapse, transverse myelitis, space-occupying lesion), brainstem stroke

**LMN signs (↓tone, ↓reflexes):** further divided by whether there are sensory changes.

**Mixed UMN & LMN signs:** ALS, cervical myelo-radiculopathy, syringomyelia

**No sensory changes — motor neuron and motor neuropathy:**
- Lead toxicity — classically affects wrist and finger extensors first, then spreads proximally
- Progressive muscular atrophy — MND subset involving only LMN
- Hodgkin's lymphoma — secondary to neoplastic infiltration
- Polio — CNS infection destroys motor neurons, causing acute flaccid paralysis
- Multifocal motor neuropathy — acquired progressive disorder with asymmetric weakness and no sensory problems (UL>LL)
- Spinal muscular atrophy — autosomal recessive, 4 subtypes with varying severity

**Neuromuscular junction:**
- Myasthenia gravis — antibody against post-synaptic nicotinic ACh receptor; associated with thymoma; fatiguability (increasing contractions → decreasing strength)
- Lambert-Eaton syndrome — antibody against pre-synaptic voltage-gated Ca channel; associated with small cell lung cancer; increasing contractions → increasing strength (contrast with MG)
- Botulism — secondary to *Clostridium botulinum* toxin; causes flaccid paralysis
- Congenital myasthenic syndromes

**Myopathy:**
- Polymyositis and dermatomyositis — proximal muscle weakness ± tenderness; associated with malignancies
- Duchenne muscular dystrophy — X-linked recessive, dystrophin gene damage; progressive weakness from age 5 + calf pseudohypertrophy (Becker MD is a less severe variant)
- Statin toxicity — usually proximal muscles first ± myalgia ± dark urine
- Viral infection — infectious or post-infectious myositis (e.g. HIV, influenza, EBV)

**With sensory changes — does it follow a nerve root/nerve distribution?**

**Radiculopathy** (follows nerve root distribution — mechanical compression of a nerve root): disc prolapse (?acute), spondylosis, spinal cord tumour, infection (e.g. discitis, abscess)

**Mononeuropathy:** compression (carpal tunnel syndrome, cubital tunnel syndrome, radial tunnel syndrome, peroneal nerve); other (trauma, space-occupying lesion, ischaemia — all can be acute)

**Mononeuritis multiplex** (nerve lesions in ≥2 named nerves in separate body parts): vasculitis (e.g. affecting right peroneal and median nerves → right foot drop + carpal tunnel syndrome), diabetes

**Polyneuropathy:**
- Nutritional (e.g. thiamine deficiency)
- Alcohol (reduced nutrition ± inflammatory effect on neurons)
- Toxins (e.g. chemotherapy, isoniazid) — generally length-dependent
- Paraproteinaemic (e.g. secondary to haematologic malignancy)

### Diabetic Neuropathy

> [!note] Gap-filled — genuinely conspicuous by its absence given diabetic retinopathy (see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Diabetic Retinopathy) and diabetic nephropathy (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Diabetic Nephropathy) both have dedicated entries as the other two classic diabetic microvascular complications, while neuropathy — the third — was previously only a passing mention here (diabetic amyotrophy specifically) and within [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Diabetic Foot. Verified against the American Diabetes Association's position statement on diabetic neuropathy and current international expert consensus, Aug 2026.

**D:** nerve damage attributable to diabetes, encompassing several genuinely distinct clinical patterns rather than a single uniform condition — the classification below matters because each pattern has a different natural history and, in some cases, different management.

> [!info] Classification — four genuinely distinct patterns:
> - **Distal symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy (DSPN):** by far the most common pattern, affecting up to half of people with diabetes over their lifetime — the "glove and stocking" distribution already referenced in the Diabetic Foot entry, not repeated here.
> - **Autonomic neuropathy:** affects the autonomic nervous system rather than sensorimotor function — cardiovascular (resting tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension — see Orthostatic syncope above, not repeated here), gastrointestinal (gastroparesis, diabetic diarrhoea/constipation), genitourinary (erectile dysfunction, neurogenic bladder), and sudomotor (reduced sweating contributing to dry, fissure-prone skin — see Diabetic Foot in [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]], not repeated here) manifestations.
> - **Mononeuropathy:** compression neuropathies (e.g. carpal tunnel syndrome) occur with genuinely increased frequency in diabetes, alongside acute cranial mononeuropathies — classically **CN III palsy with pupillary sparing**, given diabetic microvascular disease preferentially affects the nerve's central fibres while sparing the peripherally-located pupillomotor fibres (already established in [[05_Ophthalmology]] Ocular Manifestations of Systemic Disease — Consolidated Reference, not repeated here).
> - **Proximal diabetic neuropathy (diabetic amyotrophy):** affects nerves supplying the thighs, hips, buttocks ± lower limbs → muscle wasting, weakness, and pain, secondary to lumbosacral plexus damage — genuinely different from the other patterns given its often asymmetric, subacute presentation rather than the classic symmetric distal pattern.

**S/Smx (DSPN specifically, given it's the pattern most commonly tested and encountered):** sensory symptoms predominate — burning, tingling, electric-shock-like sensations ("positive" symptoms), alongside numbness and sensory loss ("negative" symptoms) in the classic glove-and-stocking distribution, worse at night; a genuinely important safety point already established in Diabetic Foot is that sensory loss means injury can go unnoticed, directly driving the foot ulceration risk built in that entry.

**Ix:** primarily clinical — annual foot/sensory screening (already established as part of Diabetic Foot surveillance, not repeated here) using simple bedside tools (10g monofilament testing, vibration sense); nerve conduction studies reserved for atypical presentations, asymmetric findings, or where an alternative cause is genuinely suspected (given routine DSPN doesn't require electrophysiological confirmation).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** optimise glycaemic control — genuinely the only intervention with evidence for slowing progression of the underlying neuropathy itself, though this doesn't reverse established nerve damage; the remaining Mx below is symptomatic (pain control) rather than disease-modifying.
- **Definitive (pharmacological management of painful DSPN):** current international consensus (including the ADA's own position statement) recommends **pregabalin or duloxetine as first-line**, given both have the strongest evidence base and are specifically approved for this indication; gabapentin is a reasonable alternative first-line option, particularly where cost or drug interactions favour it over pregabalin. Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline) are also effective but used with more caution given a higher risk of significant side effects, particularly in older patients or those with cardiac disease. **Suboptimal pain relief on monotherapy is genuinely common** (response achieved in only around half of patients) — combination therapy (a gabapentinoid plus an antidepressant) is a reasonable next step for inadequate response to a single first-line agent, rather than simply escalating the dose of the same drug indefinitely.
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing foot care and injury-prevention education given the established sensory loss (see Diabetic Foot, not repeated here); management of the specific autonomic manifestations as they arise (e.g. prokinetics for gastroparesis, fludrocortisone/midodrine for significant orthostatic hypotension) rather than a single unified autonomic-neuropathy treatment.

**P:** established sensorimotor neuropathy is generally **not reversible** even with subsequent excellent glycaemic control — this is precisely why early detection (via the annual screening above) and prevention (glycaemic control from diagnosis) matter more than treatment once neuropathy is established, and why patient education about the irreversibility is genuinely important for motivating early, sustained glycaemic control rather than only after symptoms appear.


- Inherited (e.g. Charcot-Marie-Tooth)
- Inflammatory (e.g. Guillain-Barré syndrome)

**Plexopathy:** brachial neuritis (immune-mediated inflammatory process, 80% unilateral onset, pain + muscle atrophy), diabetes (e.g. lumbosacral plexopathy), space-occupying lesion

**Poly-radiculopathy:** spondylolysis (vertebral fracture), chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, space-occupying lesion, infection

## Neuroinflammatory Disorders

### Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

**D:** demyelinating CNS condition clinically defined by ≥2 episodes of neurological dysfunction (brain, spinal cord, or optic nerves) separated in space and time.

**R:** F>M (3:1), family history, northern latitude, autoimmune disorders. "Typical patient": 20–40yo white female.

**A:** genetics + environment

**P:** activated T cells against an unknown antigen → cascade of inflammation, demyelination, and axonal loss. Multifocal areas of demyelination, decreased oligodendrocytes, loss of axons in the white matter of the CNS.
- If episode(s) not disseminated in time/space — clinically isolated syndrome (CIS)
- Relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) — 85%; acute attacks (1–2 months) followed by remission
- Secondary progressive — 65% of RRMS progress to this, generally within 15 years of diagnosis
- Primary progressive — no RRMS period, just deterioration

**S/Smx:** significant lethargy. Visual: optic neuritis, optic atrophy, Uhthoff's phenomenon (worsening vision with rise in body temperature), internuclear ophthalmoplegia. Sensory: pins and needles, numbness, trigeminal neuralgia, Lhermitte's phenomenon (paraesthesiae in limbs on neck flexion). Motor: spastic weakness, especially in legs. Cerebellar: ataxia, tremor. Others: urinary incontinence, sexual dysfunction, intellectual deterioration.

> [!info] Gap-filled — Uhthoff's and Lhermitte's phenomena were both named without explaining the mechanism, despite both stemming directly from the same demyelination process already established above (P:), just triggered by two different physical stimuli. **The shared foundation**: demyelinated axons lose their normal myelin insulation, which both slows/impairs their electrical conduction and makes them **abnormally sensitive to physical perturbation** — a normal, myelinated axon simply doesn't respond this way to temperature change or mechanical stretch. **Uhthoff's phenomenon (thermal sensitivity)**: nerve conduction velocity is temperature-dependent, and demyelinated fibres — already operating with a reduced safety margin for successful action potential propagation — have their conduction pushed below the threshold needed for reliable transmission by even a small rise in body temperature (from exercise, a hot shower, or fever), producing transient worsening of symptoms (classically visual, given optic nerve involvement) that resolves once temperature normalises. **Lhermitte's phenomenon (mechanical sensitivity)**: demyelinated axons in the cervical dorsal columns become mechanically irritable — neck flexion stretches the spinal cord, and this mechanical deformation triggers **ectopic (abnormal, spontaneous) action potentials** in the exposed, unstable axonal membrane, perceived as an electric-shock-like sensation radiating down the spine and limbs. Both are genuinely useful clinical signs precisely because they reveal the presence of demyelinated tissue through its abnormal responsiveness to an otherwise unremarkable stimulus (a hot shower, a neck movement) — not because the stimulus itself is doing anything unusual.

**Ix:** MRI (brain and spinal cord) (*why:* the key diagnostic test, demonstrating the dissemination in space (lesions in ≥2 typical locations) and time (new/enhancing lesions on follow-up, or simultaneous enhancing and non-enhancing lesions) required for diagnosis; *what:* multifocal white matter lesions, classically periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, or spinal cord). LP (*why:* supports the diagnosis when MRI findings are equivocal, particularly by demonstrating intrathecal antibody production; *what:* oligoclonal bands present in CSF but not serum — indicating CNS-specific antibody synthesis). Visual evoked potentials (*why:* detects subclinical optic nerve demyelination even when the patient has no current visual symptoms, supporting dissemination in space; *what:* delayed conduction velocity, indicating prior/subclinical optic neuritis).

> [!info] Verified against MS Australia and Australian PBS listing data, Aug 2026 — drug classes are consistent with Australian practice; one important currency flag added below.
> **Mx of relapses:** high-dose steroids (e.g. IV methylprednisolone) for 5 days — shortens symptom duration but does not affect degree of recovery. No AU-specific change.
>
> **DMARDs:** natalizumab IV or ocrelizumab IV remain high-efficacy, PBS-listed, first-line-in-practice options for many Australian patients (consistent with the source note) — **ofatumumab is another PBS-listed high-efficacy option** not mentioned in the source note, increasingly used similarly to ocrelizumab. Others — fingolimod PO, β-interferon SC/IM, glatiramer acetate SC — all remain PBS-listed lower-efficacy/traditional first-line options.
> [!info] Resolved (update to the earlier flag here) — the PBS funding threat to ocrelizumab and ofatumumab was live from around July 2026, but Health Minister Mark Butler confirmed in late July 2026 that **both drugs will remain on the PBS**, following PBAC advice against delisting despite the underlying pricing dispute (triggered by a third anti-CD20 therapy, ublituximab, being listed at a substantially lower price and pulling the reference-pricing benchmark down). The government has separately asked PBAC to review how high-efficacy MS medicines are used on the PBS and whether current listings reflect contemporary evidence — this review is ongoing and could still affect future pricing/listing details, but the core access question (will these drugs remain PBS-subsidised at all) is settled for now. Both remain genuine PBS-listed, clinically appropriate high-efficacy options as described above.
>
> **Fatigue:** exclude other causes (e.g. anaemia); trial amantadine, CBT. No AU-specific change identified.
>
> **Spasticity:** baclofen and gabapentin first-line; others — diazepam, dantrolene. No AU-specific change identified.
>
> **Urinary incontinence:** bladder US to assess emptying; if significant residual volume, intermittent self-catheterisation; if not, anticholinergics may help. No AU-specific change identified.

**P:** variable prognosis. Negative prognostic factors: frequent relapses, motor or cerebellar onset, increased MRI lesion burden.

### Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

**D:** acute inflammatory polyneuropathy, divided into axonal and demyelinating forms.

**R:** preceding infection (viral, bacterial, mosquito-borne, hepatitis E) ~6 weeks before, especially associated with *Campylobacter jejuni* and CMV

**A/P:** immune-mediated attack on the myelin sheath or Schwann cells of sensory and motor nerves, via cellular and humoral immune mechanisms; cross-reaction of antibodies to gangliosides.

**S/Smx:** progressive symmetrical muscle weakness of all limbs — legs first then arms (ascending); flaccid paralysis; areflexia; paraesthesia in feet and hands. Acute development over 2–4 weeks, then plateau. Mild dysautonomia (2/3 of patients): urinary retention, ileus, respiratory failure (20–30% require mechanical ventilation). Cranial nerve involvement: diplopia, bilateral facial nerve palsy, oropharyngeal weakness. Rarely, papilloedema.

**Ix:** LP (*why:* supports the diagnosis via a characteristic pattern; *what:* ↑protein with normal WBC — "albuminocytological dissociation," a classic and specific finding distinguishing GBS from an infective process, which would typically show pleocytosis). Nerve conduction studies (*why:* confirms the demyelinating (or axonal) neuropathy and helps predict prognosis (electrically inexcitable nerves are a poor prognostic sign — see below); *what:* ↓motor conduction velocity, consistent with demyelination).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** IVIG or plasma exchange first-line (equally effective, choice often based on availability/patient factors — no added benefit to combining them); supportive Mx is critical given the risk of respiratory failure — regular FVC monitoring, VTE prophylaxis (given the immobility/dysautonomia risk), mechanical ventilation PRN if respiratory muscles become involved.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation once the acute phase plateaus and recovery begins — physiotherapy/occupational therapy given the potential for prolonged recovery even in patients with a good ultimate prognosis.

> [!danger] **Why the monitoring is FVC and not oximetry — the single most important thing to understand about neuromuscular weakness, and the error is being reassured.** **FVC = forced vital capacity**, the volume a patient can breathe out in one maximal effort. Here it is measured serially at the bedside, which is a different use from the FEV1/FVC *ratio* used to classify obstructive vs restrictive lung disease (see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Spirometry and Peak Flow).
>
> *The mechanism:* in GBS and myasthenia **the lungs are normal** — it is the respiratory *pump* that is failing. Gas exchange across a healthy alveolar membrane stays efficient right up until the bellows give out, so **oxygen saturation and CO₂ stay normal until immediately before the patient decompensates**, and then both change abruptly. A patient who is tiring, using accessory muscles and unable to complete a sentence, with saturations of 98%, is not reassuring — the saturation is measuring the wrong organ.
>
> **What to do instead:** serial FVC, at defined intervals, with the trend recorded. It falls progressively and gives hours of warning that the numbers everyone looks at do not. The commonly taught escalation trigger is the **"20/30/40 rule"** — VC below ~20 mL/kg, maximal inspiratory pressure below 30 cmH₂O, maximal expiratory pressure below 40 — prompting ICU review for likely intubation. Treat the figures as the trigger for a conversation with ICU, not a threshold to wait for: **a falling trend matters more than any single value**, and intubation in this setting is planned, not emergent, if the trend is being watched.
>
> Also watch for the bedside signs that precede any number: inability to count aloud to 20 in one breath, a weak cough (which predicts failure to clear secretions), and bulbar weakness — the last of which threatens the airway independently of the FVC.

> [!info] **IVIG — what it is, and why its use here is not the same as the immunoglobulin given after an exposure.** **IVIG = intravenous immunoglobulin**, pooled human antibody given by infusion. The corpus uses the abbreviation in eight files and had never expanded it.
>
> The distinction matters because the *reason* for giving it differs completely. In post-exposure use — VZIG, tetanus or rabies immunoglobulin, anti-D — the antibody is **targeted at one thing and does the work directly** (see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Passive Immunisation — Immunoglobulin After an Exposure). In GBS, CIDP, myasthenic crisis, ITP and Kawasaki disease, **high-dose IVIG is an immunomodulator**: the point is not what the antibodies bind, it is that a large load of pooled immunoglobulin dampens the patient's own autoantibody-driven attack. Hence the dose is far larger, the indication is autoimmune rather than infective, and there is no "window" to meet.
>
> *The practical consequence for an intern:* IVIG here is a **treatment**, not a prophylaxis, so it is not time-critical in the same way — but it is a blood product, so it carries the same consent, documentation and traceability requirements (see [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]]), and supply is limited enough that it is usually authorised rather than simply ordered.

**P:** 85% good functional recovery. Negative prognostic factors: severe weakness, rapid onset, ↑age, muscle wasting, electrically inexcitable nerves, preceding diarrhoeal illness.

> [!note] Miller-Fisher syndrome — subset of GBS with ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia; usually descending paralysis.

### Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating (Poly)Neuropathy (CIDP)

**D:** acquired demyelinating peripheral neuropathy of presumed autoimmune aetiology.

**R:** M>F, autoimmune conditions, DM, infection, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS)

**A:** unclear; 20–30% preceded by infection, immunisation, or surgical procedure.

**P:** autoimmune, cell-mediated disease against myelin.

**S/Smx (typical CIDP):** chronically progressive, step-wise, or recurrent condition; symmetrical proximal and distal weakness and sensory dysfunction of all extremities; develops over ≥2 months; decreased or absent tendon reflexes in all extremities. Many variants exist.

**Ix:** nerve conduction studies (*why:* the key diagnostic test, demonstrating the demyelinating pattern and distinguishing CIDP from GBS mainly by the chronic (≥2 month) time course rather than the electrophysiological pattern itself, which can look similar; *what:* slow conduction, ↑latency, conduction block). LP (*why:* supports the diagnosis via a similar albuminocytological-dissociation-type pattern to GBS, reflecting the same underlying immune-mediated mechanism; *what:* WCC <10, protein >45 mg/dL).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute/chronic split in the GBS sense — CIDP is managed as a chronic relapsing/progressive condition from diagnosis, though flares may need escalation of the same therapies):** IVIG, corticosteroids, or plasma exchange; analgesia for neuropathic pain.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation; ongoing immunotherapy often needed long-term given the chronic/relapsing nature (unlike GBS, which is typically monophasic).

**P:** 80% respond to treatment within the first few months; 50% go into remission, 10% have poor outcomes (severe disability or death). Negative prognostic factors: ↑age, progressive course, CNS involvement, axonal loss on biopsy.

> [!note] No crossover between the demyelinating syndromes due to differing underlying pathology: MS affects oligodendrocytes in the CNS; GBS and CIDP affect Schwann cells in the PNS.

### Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

**D:** chronic autoimmune disorder of the post-synaptic membrane at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) in skeletal muscle.

**A/P:** antibodies against nicotinic AChR on the post-synaptic membrane (90%) → complement-mediated destruction → decreased action potential in skeletal muscle → weakness. Also anti-muscle-specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) antibodies (10–40%).

> [!info] Classification
> Class I: any eye weakness ± ptosis | Class II: mild weakness of other muscles | Class III: moderate weakness | Class IV: severe weakness | Class V: intubation required to maintain airway. Classes II–IV split into (a) predominantly limb/axial/both and (b) oropharyngeal/respiratory/both.

**S/Smx:** muscle fatiguability (patients usually complain of increasing weakness toward the end of the day); ptosis, diplopia; dysphagia; dysarthria; facial paresis; proximal limb weakness.

> [!info] Gap-filled — "fatiguability" was stated as MG's defining feature without explaining why weakness specifically *worsens with repeated use*, unlike most other causes of muscle weakness. **Mechanism:** normal neuromuscular transmission has a substantial **safety margin** — even in healthy people, each successive nerve impulse releases somewhat less acetylcholine than the one before (normal presynaptic vesicle depletion with repeated firing), but this is inconsequential because there are far more postsynaptic AChRs available than strictly needed to trigger a muscle action potential. In MG, **antibody-mediated destruction of postsynaptic AChR (per the A/P above) erodes this safety margin** — with fewer receptors remaining, the normal, otherwise-harmless decline in ACh release during repeated stimulation becomes clinically significant, since there are no longer enough receptors in reserve to compensate. Progressively fewer muscle fibres reach the threshold needed to fire with each successive contraction, producing genuine, measurable **fatiguable weakness** — worse with sustained or repeated activity, better after rest, and classically worse by the end of the day as the safety margin is exhausted by a day's cumulative use. This same principle underlies the "jitter" seen on single-fibre EMG above, and is also the physiological basis for testing fatiguability directly at the bedside (e.g. sustained upgaze provoking ptosis).

**Epidemiology:** females <40yo; males >60yo. Link with thymoma (20–40% of thymoma patients develop MG). Associated with other autoimmune disorders.

**Ix:** single fibre electromyography (*why:* the most sensitive test for MG, detecting the characteristic abnormal neuromuscular transmission even when other tests are negative; *what:* increased "jitter" between muscle fibre action potentials, high sensitivity >92%). CT thorax (*why:* screens for thymoma, a significant and treatable association requiring its own management (thymectomy); *what:* may show a thymic mass). CK (*why:* helps exclude a primary myopathy as an alternative cause of weakness; *what:* normal in MG, distinguishing it from myopathic causes of weakness where CK is typically elevated). Autoantibodies to AChR (*why:* the key diagnostic serological test; *what:* positive in ~90% — anti-MuSK antibodies tested if AChR-negative, positive in a further 10–40% of the remainder). In crises, forced vital capacity (*why:* the key bedside test for monitoring respiratory muscle involvement and predicting impending respiratory failure — trended serially rather than relying on a single measurement; *what:* declining FVC signals worsening respiratory muscle weakness and impending need for ventilatory support).

**Mx (Class I–III):** pyridostigmine first-line; corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (e.g. azathioprine, rituximab), thymectomy second-line.

**Mx (Class IV–V):** combination of both above.

**Mx of myasthenic crisis:** (1) serial FVC ± mechanical ventilation, (2) plasmapheresis or IVIG, (3) supportive care, remove trigger. **The FVC monitoring and the IVIG here are the same as in GBS** — see the two boxes under Guillain-Barré Syndrome above for why oximetry misleads in neuromuscular respiratory failure, and for what IVIG is doing, not repeated here.

> [!note] Myasthenic crisis = acute worsening of symptoms, often triggered by stressors — can lead to respiratory failure.

**Mx of MG in pregnancy:** PO pyridostigmine (not IV), prednisolone, azathioprine, tacrolimus, ciclosporin, IVIG, plasmapheresis.

> [!warning] Drugs that worsen MG: β-blockers, lithium, antibiotics (gentamicin, macrolides, quinolones, tetracyclines), phenytoin, quinidine, procainamide, penicillamine.

### Neuromyelitis Optica (Devic Disease)

**D:** chronic disorder of the brain and spinal cord dominated by optic neuritis and myelitis.

**R:** autoimmune disorders (PMH or FHx), non-white predominance relative to MS

**A/P:** similar to MS; antibody-mediated disease.

**S/Smx:** may be preceded by viral infection. Optic neuritis. Transverse myelitis: motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction below a certain spinal level, may be unilateral.

**Ix:** as per MS + labs — test for anti-aquaporin 4 (AQP4-IgG) (*why:* the key distinguishing serological test from MS, since NMO and MS can look clinically similar but require different long-term Mx (some MS DMARDs can actually worsen NMO); *what:* highly specific, moderately sensitive — a positive result strongly supports NMO over MS) and anti-myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies (*why:* identifies a related but distinct antibody-mediated demyelinating spectrum (MOG-antibody disease) with its own prognostic and treatment implications; *what:* positive in a subset of AQP4-negative patients with a similar clinical picture).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** high-dose IV methylprednisolone; plasmapheresis for severe/steroid-refractory attacks.
- **Chronic/long-term:** long-term immunosuppression — critical to distinguish from MS DMARD choices, since using an MS-specific therapy in NMO can worsen outcomes; relapse prevention is the main long-term goal given the risk of cumulative disability with recurring episodes.

**P:** in most cases, partial recovery occurs; permanent loss of function can occur with recurring episodes.

## CT Head, Head Injury, and Intracranial Pressure

### Who gets a CT head for head injury?

> [!info] Verified — this UK NICE-derived algorithm (ABCDE/LATE mnemonics) is a reasonable reflection of the evidence base, but **at least some Australian diagnostic-imaging pathway guidance is explicitly built on the Canadian CT Head Rule (CCHR) instead of NICE**, so don't assume this exact mnemonic-based version is "the" Australian standard — practice varies by state/hospital, and the underlying high-risk-factor logic is broadly similar between the two rules but not identical in detail. The Canadian CT Head Rule's own high-risk factors (CT mandatory): GCS <15 at 2h post-injury, suspected open/depressed skull fracture, any sign of basal skull fracture, ≥2 episodes of vomiting, age ≥65 — closely overlapping with, but not worded identically to, the ABCDE list below. Its medium-risk factors (CT recommended, since a clinically important brain injury could still be missed): amnesia before impact ≥30min, dangerous mechanism (similar to the "L" in LATE below). Note the CCHR only applies to patients who had loss of consciousness or amnesia — patients without either aren't covered by it, though evidence suggests some residual risk can still exist if other risk factors are present. Given this genuine local variation, check the current protocol at the specific institution/state rather than treating either the NICE-style ABCDE/LATE mnemonic below or the CCHR as the single definitive Australian answer.

**CT within 1 hour if any of:**
> [!danger] Mnemonic "ABCDE"
> **A**fter-hit seizures, **B**one fractures (suspected open/depressed skull fracture, or signs of basal skull fracture — racoon eyes, mastoid ecchymosis, haemotympanum, CSF otorrhoea/rhinorrhoea), **C**oma (GCS <13 initially, or GCS <15 at 2h post-injury), **D**eficit (focal neurological), **E**mesis (>1 episode of vomiting since injury)

If none of the above, but loss of consciousness or amnesia after injury occurred, check for:

> [!warning] Mnemonic "LATE"
> **L**egendary tale (dangerous mechanism — pedestrian/cyclist struck by vehicle, occupant ejected from vehicle, fall from >1m or >5 stairs), **A**mnesia (>30 min retrograde amnesia of events before injury), **T**hrombin disorder (Hx of bleeding/clotting disorder), **E**lderly (≥65yo)

If any present → CT within 8h of injury (and check if the patient is on warfarin → if so, CT <8h regardless). If none present → no CT head required.

### Intracranial Pressure

Normal ICP: 7–15 mmHg (higher in adults than children). Cerebral perfusion pressure = mean arterial pressure − intracranial pressure.

### Low ICP
**Causes:** iatrogenic (e.g. lumbar puncture headache, post-epidural), spontaneous.

**Ix:** MRI may show thickened meninges. LP opening pressure <4–5 mmHg suggestive.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** bed rest, fluids, caffeine — after LP, patients should lie flat for ≥1h. Consenting patients for spinal/epidural should be warned about the risk of low-pressure headache.
- **Definitive (if conservative measures fail):** epidural blood patching — patient's own blood injected into the epidural space; blood acts as a tamponade and clots over the epidural leak; usually works very well.

### Raised ICP
**Causes:** traumatic head injuries (secondary to bleeding, haematomas), infections (e.g. meningitis, TB), tumours, hydrocephalus, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (a diagnosis of exclusion).

**S/Smx:** headache — typically worse in the morning or on lying down; vomiting; decreased consciousness; papilloedema.

> [!danger] Cushing's triad (late stage): widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular breathing. **The mechanism resolves an apparent paradox** — rising ICP reduces cerebral perfusion, triggering a **two-stage** autonomic response, not a single simultaneous reflex: (1) **sympathetic activation first** — raised ICP → cerebral ischaemia → sympathetic surge → peripheral vasoconstriction → hypertension (the widening pulse pressure, systolic rising more than diastolic), an attempt to restore cerebral perfusion pressure against the raised ICP; (2) **baroreceptor-mediated vagal bradycardia second** — the resulting hypertension is detected by carotid/aortic baroreceptors, triggering a **parasympathetic (vagal) reflex bradycardia** — genuinely the *opposite* of the reflex tachycardia hypertension would normally cause, which is exactly why the combination is a specific, recognisable sign rather than two unrelated findings. Irregular breathing reflects brainstem compression/ischaemia directly. **Genuinely a preterminal, late sign** — ED patients with 2 of 3 signs present have roughly double the mortality of those with normal vitals — so relying on the full triad to recognise raised ICP means acting too late; earlier signs (headache, vomiting, altered consciousness, per the S/Smx above) should prompt action well before this triad appears.

**Ix:** CT/MRI first-line (see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] CT Head — Systematic Approach for the general reading framework, including the cistern-effacement sign of raised ICP specifically, not repeated here). LP opening pressure >20 suggestive of raised ICP.

> [!danger] If suspecting raised ICP due to mass effect (e.g. haematoma, tumour), DO NOT perform LP — risk of herniation. The mass must be dealt with first to relieve pressure.

Invasive ICP monitoring: catheter placed into the lateral ventricles to monitor ICP ± collect samples/drain small amounts.

**Mx:**
- Treat underlying cause if possible (dexamethasone can help reduce swelling in brain tumours → decreased ICP)
- Head elevation to 30°
- IV mannitol (osmotic diuretic)
- Controlled hyperventilation — ↓pCO2 → cerebral artery vasoconstriction → ↓ICP (temporary solution)
- Removal of CSF — ventricular drain, repeated LPs (e.g. in IIH), ventriculoperitoneal shunt (for hydrocephalus)

### Hydrocephalus

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Hydrocephalus," High yield) — Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus is covered as its own subtype under Dementias above, but general hydrocephalus (classification and the acute/obstructive presentation, a genuine neurosurgical emergency) was previously only mentioned in passing as a complication of other conditions throughout this file. This entry covers the general framework; see Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus under Dementias above for that specific chronic subtype, not repeated here.

**D:** an abnormal accumulation of CSF within the ventricular system, typically causing ventricular dilatation ± raised intracranial pressure.

**Classification (by mechanism):**
- **Non-communicating (obstructive) hydrocephalus** — a physical blockage within the ventricular system itself prevents CSF flow (e.g. at the cerebral aqueduct, or the fourth ventricle outlets) — causes include tumours, congenital aqueductal stenosis, and posterior fossa masses/malformations (including Chiari malformation). This is the pattern most likely to present acutely and dangerously, since CSF continues to be produced proximal to the blockage with nowhere to go.
- **Communicating hydrocephalus** — CSF flows freely between the ventricles but is inadequately reabsorbed at the arachnoid granulations — causes include meningitis (impairing reabsorption via inflammatory exudate), subarachnoid haemorrhage (blood similarly impairing reabsorption — see Strokes/Brain Bleeds above for the disease-level detail on SAH, not repeated here), and normal pressure hydrocephalus (see Dementias above).

**A:** congenital (aqueductal stenosis, Chiari malformation, Dandy-Walker malformation, spina bifida with associated hydrocephalus), acquired (tumours, meningitis, subarachnoid or intraventricular haemorrhage, post-traumatic).

**S/Smx:** depends heavily on the rate of onset and, in children, whether the cranial sutures have fused —
- **Acute (any age):** headache (classically worse in the morning or with lying flat/Valsalva, given CSF pressure dynamics), nausea/vomiting, reduced conscious level, papilloedema (see raised ICP above for the fuller general S/Smx this overlaps with, not repeated here); untreated acute obstructive hydrocephalus can progress rapidly to brainstem compression/herniation and death, making this a genuine time-critical neurosurgical emergency.
- **Infants (before suture fusion):** the skull can expand to partially accommodate raised pressure, giving a somewhat different presentation — macrocephaly/rapidly increasing head circumference, a bulging anterior fontanelle, "sunset sign" (downward-deviated eyes from pressure on the tectal plate), poor feeding, irritability, developmental delay if untreated.
- **Chronic/gradual (older children/adults):** may present more insidiously with headache, cognitive change, or gait disturbance — overlapping with the Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus triad above in the communicating, normal-pressure subtype specifically.

**Ix:** CT or MRI head (*why:* the key diagnostic and localising test, confirming ventricular dilatation and — critically — identifying the level and likely cause of any obstruction, which directly determines whether urgent surgical decompression is needed; *what:* ventriculomegaly, with the pattern of which ventricles are enlarged helping localise an obstructive lesion — e.g. dilated lateral and third ventricles with a normal-sized fourth ventricle points to aqueductal obstruction). Further Ix to identify the underlying cause per the causes listed above (e.g. LP only once mass lesion/obstruction has been excluded on imaging, given the coning risk — see Raised ICP above for this explicit contraindication, not repeated here).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (obstructive hydrocephalus, or any acutely decompensating patient):** urgent neurosurgical referral — external ventricular drain (EVD) provides immediate CSF diversion and ICP control while the underlying cause is addressed/definitive treatment is arranged; this is genuinely time-critical given the herniation risk described above.
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause where possible (e.g. resection of an obstructing tumour); **ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt** is the mainstay definitive treatment for hydrocephalus that isn't resolved by treating the underlying cause alone — a permanent implanted device diverting CSF from the ventricles to the peritoneal cavity for reabsorption; **endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV)** is an alternative to shunting in selected obstructive cases (particularly aqueductal stenosis), creating an internal bypass route for CSF flow and avoiding a permanent implanted shunt system.
- **Chronic/long-term:** patients with a VP shunt require lifelong awareness of shunt complications — blockage (presenting with recurrence of the original hydrocephalus symptoms) and infection (shunt infection can present with fever plus the same symptoms, and is a genuine diagnostic trap if not specifically considered in a shunted patient presenting unwell) — a shunted patient presenting with headache/vomiting/reduced consciousness should always prompt consideration of shunt malfunction as a differential, given how easily this can be missed if not specifically asked about.

### Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (Pseudotumour Cerebri)

**R:** F>M (~6:1), obesity and weight gain, some drugs (COCP, steroids, lithium, tetracyclines, isotretinoin)

**S/Smx:** headache (typically worse on waking or when lying down); papilloedema; blurred vision, enlarged blind spot; ± CN VI palsy (cannot look laterally).

**Ix:** as per raised ICP; IIH is a diagnosis of exclusion (*why:* other causes of raised ICP (mass lesion, hydrocephalus, venous sinus thrombosis) must be actively excluded via imaging before accepting an IIH diagnosis, since the label itself means "no other cause found"; *what:* imaging is essentially normal aside from signs of raised pressure — no mass lesion, ventricles typically normal or small).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** repeated LP to temporarily reduce ICP — provides rapid symptomatic relief while definitive Mx takes effect.
- **Definitive/chronic:** carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (e.g. acetazolamide, topiramate — not in pregnancy, teratogenic) — MOA: decreased CSF production; long-term weight loss (a genuinely disease-modifying intervention given the strong obesity association, not just symptomatic); surgery (e.g. optic nerve sheath fenestration or CSF shunting) for vision-threatening or refractory disease.

### Head Injury

Primary injuries divided into focal or diffuse.
- **Focal:** contusion (bruising), haematoma (collection of blood — see brain bleeds section)
- **Diffuse axonal injury:** occurs from mechanical shearing after deceleration, causing disruption and tearing of axons; 90% end up in coma, very poor prognosis

**Secondary brain injuries** occur due to (1) the body's response to the primary injury, and (2) loss of autoregulatory mechanisms from the primary injury — e.g. cerebral oedema, ischaemia, infection, tonsillar or tentorial herniation, Cushing's reflex (preterminal event).

**Ix:** CT head (per algorithm above); full body survey; ICP monitoring (mandatory if GCS 3–8 + abnormal CT head; consider if GCS 3–8 + normal CT head); U&Es (may reveal hyponatraemia if SIADH develops secondary to brain injury).

**Mx:**
- ABCDE + resuscitate — C-spine collar may be needed; intubate & ventilate if GCS <8
- ICP monitoring as above
- If markedly raised ICP, decompressive craniotomy may be needed
- Depressed skull fractures: surgical reduction and debridement
- Maintain cerebral perfusion pressure ≥70 mmHg in adults, 40–70 mmHg in children

## Spinal Disorders

### Cauda Equina Syndrome

**D:** compression of the cauda equina — the bundle of lumbosacral nerve roots (L2–S5) below the level the spinal cord itself ends (~L1/L2) — causing a distinct LMN (not UMN) pattern of deficit, since these are peripheral nerve roots rather than spinal cord.

**R:** large central disc prolapse (most common cause), spinal stenosis, spinal trauma, spinal tumour (primary or metastatic), spinal epidural abscess/haematoma, ankylosing spondylitis, post-surgical/iatrogenic.

**A/P:** mechanical compression of multiple lumbosacral nerve roots simultaneously within the narrow confines of the spinal canal below the conus medullaris.

> [!note] [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Cauda equina syndrome carries a second full entry, consistent with this one. Linked by the pairs audit (2026-08-29).

**S/Smx:**
> [!danger] Red flag symptoms — ask directly, since patients may not volunteer these
> Bilateral leg pain/sciatica; saddle anaesthesia (numbness of the perineum, buttocks, inner thighs — ask specifically, e.g. "would you feel a wipe after using the toilet?"); bladder dysfunction (urinary retention with overflow incontinence is the most sensitive sign, though the patient may report incontinence rather than retention); bowel dysfunction (reduced anal tone, faecal incontinence); sexual dysfunction (new erectile dysfunction, loss of sensation); bilateral lower limb weakness and/or sensory loss; reduced/absent lower limb reflexes (LMN pattern, distinguishing this from a cord-level compressive lesion causing UMN signs).

**Ix:** urgent MRI whole spine (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test, and genuinely time-critical — this is one of the few neurological presentations where imaging delay directly translates to worse functional outcome; *what:* identifies the compressive lesion, its level, and cause). Bladder scan (*why:* quantifies post-void residual volume, supporting the diagnosis and providing an objective, trackable marker — a residual >100–200mL is concerning; *what:* elevated post-void residual supports bladder dysfunction from cauda equina compression rather than an incidental finding). PR exam (*why:* assesses anal tone and perianal sensation directly — a reduced/absent anal tone is a significant red flag; *what:* reduced tone or saddle sensory loss supports the diagnosis).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** this is a **surgical emergency** — urgent same-day neurosurgical/orthopaedic spinal referral for emergency decompression (typically discectomy), since the window for meaningful neurological recovery is time-dependent; analgesia; catheterise if in retention.
- **Definitive:** surgical decompression, ideally within 24–48h of symptom onset — earlier decompression is associated with better recovery of bladder/bowel/sexual function, though some recovery is possible even with delayed surgery.
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction rehabilitation if deficits persist post-decompression (a genuine possibility even with prompt treatment) — pelvic floor physiotherapy, urology follow-up, psychological support given the significant quality-of-life impact.

**P:** even with prompt surgery, some patients have permanent bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction — earlier decompression improves but doesn't guarantee full recovery, making this a genuine "time is function" emergency similar in spirit to "time is brain" in stroke.

### Malignant Spinal Cord Compression (MSCC)

**D:** compression of the spinal cord itself (above the level of the cauda equina, i.e. involving the cord rather than nerve roots) by a malignant process — most commonly vertebral metastases, though can also arise from direct extension or intramedullary tumour.

**R:** known malignancy (breast, prostate, lung, myeloma, and renal cell carcinoma are the most common primaries causing vertebral metastases), new back pain in a patient with a cancer history, no known cancer history but with other red flags for malignancy.

**A/P:** vertebral body metastasis → collapse or direct extension into the epidural space → mechanical compression of the spinal cord and/or its vascular supply → oedema, ischaemia, and ultimately irreversible cord damage if untreated.

**S/Smx:** progressive back pain (often the earliest symptom, may precede neurological signs by weeks — classically worse lying down/at night, and worse with coughing/straining/movement, distinguishing it from mechanical back pain); UMN signs below the level of compression (weakness, spasticity, hyperreflexia, upgoing plantars — this UMN pattern is the key distinguishing feature from cauda equina syndrome's LMN pattern, since MSCC compresses the cord itself); sensory level (a defined level below which sensation is impaired); bladder/bowel dysfunction (typically a later sign than in cauda equina syndrome, though can still occur).

> [!danger] Any patient with known malignancy presenting with new back pain should be assumed to have MSCC until proven otherwise — even in the absence of neurological signs, since pain frequently precedes deficit and early treatment before deficit develops gives the best chance of preserving function.

**Ix:** urgent whole-spine MRI (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test — whole spine imaging is needed since metastatic disease is frequently multifocal, and a lesion may exist at a different level from where symptoms localise; *what:* identifies the compressive lesion(s), extent, and any additional asymptomatic sites requiring inclusion in the treatment field). Bloods incl. calcium (*why:* screens for malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia, a relevant differential/co-existing emergency in cancer patients with neurological symptoms; *what:* may be elevated, particularly with myeloma or extensive bony metastases).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** high-dose dexamethasone started immediately on clinical suspicion, before imaging confirmation, given the drug's low harm profile and the time-critical nature of preserving neurological function — do not wait for MRI to start steroids if MSCC is clinically suspected; urgent oncology/spinal surgery/radiation oncology referral for definitive Mx planning; analgesia; VTE prophylaxis given reduced mobility.
- **Definitive:** radiotherapy (the mainstay for most patients, particularly with radiosensitive tumours and where surgery isn't indicated) ± surgical decompression/stabilisation (considered for spinal instability, single-level disease with good prognosis, or radioresistant tumours) — the specific approach depends on tumour type, extent, patient fitness, and prognosis, decided by a multidisciplinary team.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation for any residual deficit; ongoing oncological management of the underlying malignancy; palliative care involvement given this usually indicates advanced/metastatic disease.

**P:** neurological function at the time treatment starts is the strongest predictor of outcome — patients who are still ambulatory at treatment onset are far more likely to remain so than those who have already lost the ability to walk, reinforcing why pain alone (before deficit develops) should prompt urgent action rather than waiting for neurological signs to appear.

### Brown-Séquard Syndrome

**D:** hemisection of the spinal cord (incomplete transverse injury).

**Tracts affected:**
- Lateral corticospinal tract — ipsilateral weakness below the lesion
- Dorsal columns — ipsilateral loss of proprioception and vibration sensation
- Lateral spinothalamic tract — contralateral loss of pain and temperature sensation

### Subacute Combined Degeneration of the Spinal Cord

**D:** demyelination of the lateral and posterior columns of the spinal cord.

**A:** vitamin B12 deficiency, recreational nitrous oxide inhalation (deactivates B12 from its active form).

> [!info] The mechanism — genuinely worth understanding, given it explains both why B12 deficiency causes this and folate deficiency essentially never does, despite both nutrients feeding into the same methionine cycle. **B12 (cobalamin) is a cofactor for two separate enzymes**, and this dual role is the key to the distinction: (1) **methionine synthase** (cytoplasmic) converts homocysteine to methionine, using folate (as 5-methyltetrahydrofolate) as the methyl donor — methionine then becomes S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), required for methylating myelin basic protein and maintaining the myelin sheath; (2) **methylmalonyl-CoA mutase** (mitochondrial) converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, a step required for normal fatty acid metabolism and myelin lipid synthesis. **Folate is only involved in the first reaction, not the second** — so folate deficiency impairs SAM/myelin methylation similarly to B12 deficiency (which is why both cause identical megaloblastic macrocytic anaemia), but **doesn't touch the methylmalonyl-CoA mutase pathway at all**, sparing this second route of myelin damage that only B12 deficiency causes. This is also why **methylmalonic acid (MMA) is elevated specifically in B12 deficiency, not folate deficiency** — a genuinely useful distinguishing lab test between the two when the diagnosis is unclear.
> **This also explains the danger of giving folic acid to a patient with undiagnosed B12 deficiency** (already flagged in [[10_06a_Haemonc_-_Macrocytic_Anaemia]] Vitamin B12 deficiency, not repeated here): folic acid accelerates DNA synthesis and cell division, increasing demand for methionine — this further depletes an already-scarce SAM pool in a B12-deficient patient (since methionine synthase, the B12-dependent step, is already impaired), worsening myelin methylation and precipitating or accelerating SCD, even while apparently "treating" the anaemia.
> **A genuine, honest caveat**: while SCD is overwhelmingly a B12-deficiency disease, rare case reports describe SCD from folate deficiency alone — so "only B12 causes this" is a very strong rule of thumb rather than an absolute one.

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Dorsal columns — tingling, burning, or sensory loss (symmetrical, lower limb>upper limb); impaired proprioception and vibration sense
- Lateral corticospinal tracts — muscle weakness, hyperreflexia, spasticity; UMN signs lower limb first, then upper limb (brisk knee reflexes, absent ankle jerks, extensor plantars)
- Spinocerebellar tracts — sensory ataxia and gait changes, positive Romberg's sign

> [!note] Gap-filled — this entry had an unusually thorough mechanism explanation above but genuinely no Ix section, jumping straight from S/Smx to Mx.

**Ix:** serum B12 level (*why:* the first-line test given the mechanism above; *what:* low, though a genuinely important caveat is that levels can be falsely normal/borderline in early or functional deficiency). Serum methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine (*why:* directly reflects the two B12-dependent enzyme pathways established above — elevated MMA specifically indicates B12 deficiency rather than folate deficiency, per the mechanism box; *what:* both elevated in true B12 deficiency, useful when serum B12 itself is equivocal). FBC and blood film (*why:* screens for the associated megaloblastic macrocytic anaemia, though a genuinely important point is that the neurological features of SCD can occur *without* anaemia being present, so a normal FBC doesn't exclude the diagnosis; *what:* macrocytosis and hypersegmented neutrophils if the haematological manifestation is also present). MRI spine (*why:* not required for diagnosis in a straightforward case, but supports it and excludes structural mimics if the picture is atypical or diagnostic doubt remains; *what:* may show characteristic dorsal/lateral column T2 hyperintensity, an "inverted V" sign on axial imaging).

**Mx:** stop trigger, B12 replacement.

### Friedreich's Ataxia

**D:** autosomal recessive trinucleotide repeat disorder.

**S/Smx:** onset 10–15yo. Cerebellar ataxia and spinocerebellar tract degeneration — sensory ataxia, gait changes, positive Romberg's sign. Optic atrophy. Hyporeflexia — absent ankle jerks and extensor plantar reflexes. Other: cardiomyopathy (90% of patients, most common cause of death), DM, kyphoscoliosis, foot deformities, high-arched palate.

**Ix:** genetic testing (*why:* confirms the diagnosis definitively, identifying the GAA trinucleotide repeat expansion in the FXN gene; *what:* confirms the diagnosis and repeat length, which correlates loosely with severity/age of onset). Echocardiogram (*why:* screens for the cardiomyopathy that is the leading cause of death in this condition, warranting active surveillance rather than waiting for symptoms; *what:* may show hypertrophic cardiomyopathy). Glucose/HbA1c (*why:* screens for the associated diabetes; *what:* may show impaired glucose tolerance or diabetes).

**Mx:** supportive and multidisciplinary — physiotherapy for gait/mobility, cardiology surveillance and management for the cardiomyopathy (the main driver of mortality, so this is not an incidental association to overlook), diabetes management if present, orthopaedic input for scoliosis/foot deformities; genetic counselling for family members given the autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Anterior Spinal Artery Occlusion

**D:** ischaemic infarction of the anterior spinal artery (artery of Adamkiewicz) or generalised hypoperfusion. The artery of Adamkiewicz is the only major arterial supply to the anterior spinal artery along the lower two-thirds of the spine.

**A:** aortic surgery, atherosclerotic disease, emboli, vasculitis (many causes).

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Lateral corticospinal tracts — bilateral spastic paresis
- Lateral spinothalamic tracts — bilateral loss of pain and temperature sensation
- Other: acutely can cause spinal shock; later findings include neurogenic bladder/bowel, sexual dysfunction

**Ix:** MRI spine (*why:* the key diagnostic test, demonstrating the characteristic infarct pattern and excluding a compressive alternative cause; *what:* T2 hyperintensity in the anterior cord distribution, sparing the dorsal columns — consistent with the vascular territory and correlating with the dorsal-column-sparing clinical picture above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** treat the underlying cause where identifiable (e.g. manage the aortic pathology if surgery-related, address the embolic source); supportive care for spinal shock if present.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation; Mx of neurogenic bladder/bowel and other chronic spinal cord injury complications.

### Syringomyelia

**D:** collection of CSF within the spinal cord (= syrinx). Syringobulbia = fluid-filled cavity within the medulla of the brainstem.

**A:** Chiari malformation (type 1), trauma, tumours, idiopathic.

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Ventral horns — flaccid paresis (usually starting with intrinsic hand muscles)
- Lateral spinothalamic tract — loss of pain and temperature sensation, classically in a 'cape-like' pattern (neck, shoulders, arms)
- Other: spastic weakness of lower limbs, neuropathic pain, upgoing plantars, autonomic features (neurogenic bowel/bladder), scoliosis in the long term

**Ix:** full spine MRI + brain MRI (to look for Chiari malformation).

**Mx:** treat underlying cause; if persistent or symptomatic, shunt into the syrinx to drain.

### Tabes Dorsalis

**D:** syndrome secondary to CNS infection by *Treponema pallidum*.

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Dorsal columns and roots — loss of proprioception and vibratory sense; ataxia, lancinating pains, bladder dysfunction, paraesthesias
- Other neuro symptoms: pupillary abnormalities (Argyll Robertson pupils), ocular palsies, decreased reflexes, Charcot joints

> [!note] Classically known as "the great imitator" — requires a degree of clinical suspicion.

**Ix:** serology — treponemal (e.g. TPPA) and non-treponemal (e.g. RPR/VDRL) tests, LP with CSF VDRL (*why:* confirms neurosyphilis specifically, since tabes dorsalis is a late manifestation of untreated syphilis and CSF involvement must be demonstrated to guide the correct (neurosyphilis-dose) antibiotic regimen; *what:* positive serology supports syphilis exposure, CSF findings confirm CNS involvement).

**Mx:** IV benzylpenicillin (neurosyphilis-specific high-dose regimen, given standard-dose penicillin for early syphilis doesn't adequately penetrate the CNS) — supportive Mx for established neurological deficits, which may not fully reverse even with adequate treatment given the demyelination is often established by the time of diagnosis.

### Neurofibromatosis

**D:** neurocutaneous genetic disorder, autosomal dominantly inherited — NF1 (more common) and NF2.

**R:** family history

**A:** NF1 — mutation on the gene encoding neurofibromin, located on chromosome 17. NF2 — mutation on chromosome 22.

**P:** decreased neurofibromin in Schwann cells, glia, melanocytes, mast cells, vascular endothelial and smooth cells → dysplasias and neoplasias.

**S/Smx of NF1:** café-au-lait spots (≥6 spots, flat light brown birthmarks); axillary/groin freckles; peripheral neurofibromas (tumours hanging off the skin); iris Lisch nodules; scoliosis; phaeochromocytomas.

**S/Smx of NF2:** bilateral vestibular schwannomas; multiple intracranial schwannomas, meningiomas, and ependymomas.

**Ix:** MRI/CT scans, genetic testing to confirm NF1.

**Mx:** symptomatic (as/when features cause problems); neurofibromas need surveillance and removal if assessed to be malignant.

### Tuberous Sclerosis

**D:** neurocutaneous and multi-systemic genetic disorder, autosomal dominantly inherited.

**R:** family history

**A/P:** mutations of TSC1 and TSC2 genes → cellular hyperplasia, tissue dysplasia, multi-organ hamartomas.

**S/Smx:** hypopigmented "ash-leaf" spots (fluoresce under UV light); roughened patches of skin over the lumbar spine (Shagreen patches); adenoma sebaceum (angiofibromas distributed over the nose); fibromata beneath nails; café-au-lait spots.

**Neuro problems:** developmental delay, epilepsy (infantile spasms), intellectual impairment, retinal hamartomas, rhabdomyomas of the heart, gliomatous changes of the brain.

**Other:** polycystic kidneys, renal angiomyolipomata, lymphangioleiomyomatosis (multiple lung cysts).

**Ix:** as per neurofibromatosis.

**Mx:** as per neurofibromatosis; mTOR inhibitors everolimus and sirolimus approved for some indications under tuberous sclerosis.

## Other Neurology Topics

### Restless Legs Syndrome

**D:** compulsive urge to move the legs.

**R:** conditions associated with iron deficiency (e.g. ESRD), pregnancy, family history (50%), use of antidepressants/antihistamines/metoclopramide, uraemia, DM

**A/P:** not fully understood; associated with iron and dopamine dysfunction.

**S/Smx:** akathisia — uncontrollable urge to move the legs; usually occurs at night, but progresses to daytime as the condition worsens; may be associated with paraesthesias (crawling/throbbing sensations); movement during sleep may be noted by a partner (periodic limb movements of sleep).

**Ix:** FBC and iron studies (especially ferritin) to exclude iron deficiency.

**Mx:** treat any iron deficiency; remove potentially offending drugs; lifestyle modifications (walking, stretching, massaging affected limbs; stop smoking; trial cutting caffeine and alcohol).

> [!info] Verified — dopamine agonists (ropinirole or pramipexole) as first-line pharmacological Mx for restless legs syndrome, with benzodiazepines/gabapentin as options, reflects standard international practice and is consistent with Australian eTG/AMH prescribing — no change needed to the drug classes below.

### Abnormal Involuntary Movements

**Chorea:** sudden, rapid, involuntary, purposeless movements (Greek for dance/choreography). Results from basal ganglia damage.
> [!info] Important causes — Neuro: Huntington's disease, Wilson's disease, ataxic telangiectasia, stroke/TIA (basal ganglia); Rheum/autoimmune: SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, rheumatic fever (Sydenham's chorea); Drugs: COCP, L-dopa, antipsychotics; Haem: polycythaemia rubra vera, neuroacanthocytosis; Pregnancy (chorea gravidarum); thyrotoxicosis; carbon monoxide poisoning.

**Athetosis:** slower form of chorea with a writhing, snake-like quality; often accompanies chorea.

**Dystonia:** slow, prolonged contraction of trunk and limb muscles.

**Ballism & hemiballism:** severe form of chorea involving proximal muscles, characterised by violent flinging movements. Hemiballism affects only one side of the body. Results from subthalamic nucleus damage. Mx: antidopaminergics (e.g. haloperidol).

**Tics:** intermittent, stereotypical, repetitive, involuntary movements (e.g. blinking, shrugging). Tourette syndrome: multiple motor and vocal tics beginning in childhood ± comorbid neuropsychiatric disorders. Mx: clonidine, atypical antipsychotics.

**Oculogyric crisis:** subtype of dystonia characterised by restlessness, agitation, and involuntary upward eye deviation. Causes: antipsychotics (especially first-gen), metoclopramide, post-encephalitic PD. Mx: stop offending drug, IV antimuscarinics (e.g. benztropine, procyclidine).

**Essential tremor (benign essential tremor):** progressive, mainly symmetrical, rhythmic, involuntary oscillatory movement disorder of the hands and forearms. Autosomal dominant. Usually absent at rest, present during intentional movements (e.g. finger-to-nose test). Improves with alcohol and rest (opposite of Parkinson's). Associated with titubation (head tremor). Mx: propranolol first-line; ?primidone.

### Austroads Driving Standards (Neurological Conditions)

> [!info] Verified against Austroads *Assessing Fitness to Drive* 2022 edition (current at Aug 2026), private-vehicle standards, Aug 2026 — Australian standards are structured quite differently from the UK DVLA rules, generally built around a single "default standard" seizure-free/event-free period rather than the UK's variable tiered system. Commercial-vehicle standards are considerably stricter (e.g. 10-year seizure-free default for some categories) — check the source document directly if advising a commercial driver.

**Epilepsy/seizures:**
- General principle: a person with epilepsy must be seizure-free for **12 months** to hold an unconditional private licence (Austroads' "default standard") — this replaces the UK's variable 6-month/12-month split by structural cause/EEG findings with a single, simpler 12-month threshold for private drivers.
- Sleep deprivation is explicitly **not** considered an acceptable "provoking factor" exemption (since it can't be reliably avoided) — a seizure attributed to sleep deprivation is still assessed under the standard epilepsy criteria, not treated as a one-off provoked event.
- Switching from one antiepileptic to another, or planned withdrawal of medication in a driver who otherwise meets the conditional-licence standard: **3-month non-driving period** applies.
- General safety principles for all licence-holders with epilepsy: take antiseizure medication regularly as prescribed, ensure adequate sleep, avoid substances (e.g. alcohol) known to increase seizure risk; periodic review recommended (at least annually for conditional-licence holders).

**Syncope:** (see also [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.34.5 for the fuller Austroads cardiovascular-syncope driving table) — a non-driving period of at least 4 weeks applies generally for cardiovascular-cause syncope in private drivers, with the specific period depending on whether a treatable cause was identified and addressed.

**Stroke/TIA:**
- **Stroke:** minimum **4 weeks** non-driving for private drivers (this applies as a blanket minimum regardless of severity, reflecting the non-progressive nature of stroke once stabilised — a notably simpler rule than the UK's deficit-dependent approach). Private drivers discharged from specialist care within 4 weeks of a stroke, assessed as fit to drive at discharge, can continue driving on their current licence without needing formal reassessment (a 2022-edition streamlining).
- **TIA:** minimum **2 weeks** non-driving for private drivers (materially shorter than the UK's 4-week/6-month tiered approach) — reflecting the genuinely low risk of a TIA itself causing a crash (a TIA essentially never causes loss of consciousness). A conditional licence is not required, since there's no expected long-term impairment.
- **Subarachnoid haemorrhage:** minimum **3 months** non-driving for private drivers, given the higher rate of associated neurological injury and post-SAH seizures compared to other stroke types.

**Narcolepsy/cataplexy:** case-by-case, depending on symptom control — consistent with the general Austroads approach for sleep disorders.

**Chronic neurological disorders (e.g. MS, MND):** Australia doesn't use a UK-style "PK1 form" — instead, the treating doctor assesses and reports to the state/territory driver licensing authority as required, with licensing decided case-by-case based on functional impairment rather than diagnosis alone. Patients have a legal obligation to notify the licensing authority themselves if a condition is likely to affect driving safety.

**Neurosurgery:** not separately verified this session — the source's UK-specific craniotomy/meningioma/pituitary-tumour timeframes below have not been individually checked against Austroads; treat as illustrative only and confirm against the current Austroads "Other neurological and neurodevelopmental conditions" chapter before relying on a specific figure:
- Craniotomy: UK figure (unverified for AU use) — no driving for 12 months
- Benign meningioma + no seizure history: UK figure (unverified for AU use) — licence reconsidered 6 months after surgery if no seizures
- Pituitary tumour: UK figures (unverified for AU use) — craniotomy: no driving for 6 months; transsphenoidal surgery: if safe to drive, OK

### Wernicke's Encephalopathy

**D:** neuropsychiatric disorder caused by thiamine (B1) deficiency.

**R:** alcoholism, AIDS, chemotherapy treatment (due to increased vomiting), GI surgery, malnutrition

**P:** decreased activity of thiamine-dependent enzymes → neuronal death.

**S/Smx:** oculomotor dysfunction (nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia — lateral rectus palsy); gait ataxia; encephalopathy (confusion, disorientation, indifference, inattentiveness); peripheral sensory neuropathy.

**Ix:** MRI (*why:* supports the diagnosis, though Wernicke's is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis and treatment should never be delayed pending imaging; *what:* may show characteristic symmetrical signal changes around the third ventricle, periaqueductal grey, and mammillary bodies — though a normal MRI does not exclude the diagnosis).

**Mx:** urgent thiamine replacement — **give thiamine before or with any glucose administration**, since glucose given first can precipitate or worsen Wernicke's in a thiamine-deficient patient by consuming remaining thiamine stores in glucose metabolism; this is a genuine and easily-missed medical emergency where treatment should start on clinical suspicion alone, never delayed for confirmatory Ix.

**P:** if untreated, Korsakoff's syndrome can develop (antero/retrograde amnesia and confabulation — confabulation = making up memories).

### Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease

**D:** hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy.

Different inheritance patterns; can affect Schwann cells and myelin (CMT1) or axons (CMT2). Look for family history.

**S/Smx:** distal muscle weakness and atrophy; hyporeflexia; high-stepping gait/foot drop (history of frequently sprained ankles); high-arched feet (pes cavus); hammer toes; "stork leg" deformity.

**Ix:** nerve conduction studies (*why:* distinguishes the demyelinating (CMT1, slowed conduction) from axonal (CMT2, reduced amplitude with relatively preserved velocity) subtypes, relevant to genetic counselling and prognosis; *what:* pattern depends on subtype as above). Genetic testing (*why:* confirms the specific genetic subtype, informs inheritance pattern counselling for family members, and can guide prognosis; *what:* identifies the specific causative mutation among the many genes associated with CMT).

**Mx:** supportive — physical and occupational therapy (gait aids, ankle-foot orthoses for foot drop); podiatry for foot deformities; genetic counselling for at-risk family members given the hereditary nature.

> [!note] Other random notes from this source: local anaesthetic toxicity — Mx with 20% IV lipid emulsion. Brain pathology causing nausea/vomiting — Mx with cyclizine.
