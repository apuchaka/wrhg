## 0.1 Otitis externa

> [!note] Expanded and localised — verified against RACGP's "Approach to otitis externa in Australian general practice" (AJGP, Dec 2024), Aug 2026. The original entry cited "EarCalm" and "Otosporin" — **neither is the correct Australian product name**: EarCalm is a UK-market brand; the genuine Australian equivalent combination product is Kenacomb Otic/Otocomb Otic. Also expanded with the AAO-HNS-aligned quinolone/non-quinolone framework (the actual clinically important distinction driving Australian prescribing) and a genuinely important Indigenous Australian risk-equity point, both previously absent.

- **D:** diffuse inflammation of the external ear canal.
- **A/P:** most commonly *P. aeruginosa* or *S. aureus*. Fungal causes (otomycosis — *Aspergillus*, *Candida*) often present as co-pathogens, particularly in immunocompromised patients or after prolonged topical antibacterial use. Can be secondary to seborrhoeic dermatitis or contact dermatitis.
- **R:** external canal obstruction, high environmental humidity/temperature (a recognised issue in tropical Australian climates, e.g. North Queensland), swimming, local trauma (including cotton bud use), allergy, skin disease, diabetes, immunocompromise.
- **S/Smx + Ix:** ear pain, itch, discharge — **tragal tenderness is widely regarded as pathognomonic**. Otoscopy: red, swollen, oedematous, moist canal — assess TM integrity, since this directly determines which topical agent is safe to use (below). Swab if empirical treatment fails or a non-*Pseudomonas* cause is suspected; refer to ENT if refractory.

> [!danger] A genuine, important Australian health-equity finding: a Northern Territory retrospective analysis of necrotising otitis externa found the majority of cases were in Indigenous Australian patients, all diabetic, presenting on average around 16 years younger than non-Indigenous patients with the same condition — clinical vigilance for otitis externa progressing to necrotising disease should be higher, not lower, in young Indigenous Australian patients with diabetes, given this population is genuinely over-represented and presents earlier than the "typically older, diabetic patient" stereotype might suggest.

- **Mx — the quinolone vs non-quinolone distinction is the genuinely important clinical decision, driven entirely by tympanic membrane integrity:**
  - **TM intact (or a wick will be inserted):** non-quinolone-based topical agents are cost-effective first-line with comparable efficacy — e.g. **Sofradex** (framycetin + gramicidin + dexamethasone) or **Kenacomb Otic/Otocomb Otic** (neomycin + gramicidin + triamcinolone) — 3 drops 2–4×/day, not exceeding 2 weeks.
  - **TM perforated, or integrity cannot be adequately assessed (genuinely common in general practice given pain, swelling, and debris routinely limit visualisation):** use a **quinolone-based agent instead**, given non-quinolone agents carry ototoxicity risk in the middle ear — e.g. **Ciloxan** (ciprofloxacin 0.3%) or **Ciproxin HC** (ciprofloxacin + hydrocortisone), 3–5 drops twice daily, not exceeding 2 weeks. This uncertainty-driven default to the non-ototoxic option, rather than assuming the TM is intact, is the actual practical decision point in general practice.
  - **Suspected fungal (otomycosis):** clotrimazole 1% (**Canesten**) or clioquinol + flumetasone (**Locacorten-Vioform**) — neither suitable if TM perforated.
  - Clear canal debris/microsuction where available; consider an ear wick (e.g. a Pope Wick) if canal oedema precludes drop penetration — remove and reassess at 72 hours, the expected timeframe for improvement.
  - **Oral/systemic antibiotics are avoided for uncomplicated cases** — topical agents achieve concentrations 100–1000-fold higher locally than systemic dosing achieves, without added benefit — reserved for infection extending beyond the canal (e.g. periauricular cellulitis) or high-risk patients (diabetic, immunocompromised), and should involve infectious disease input given limited anti-pseudomonal options and rising resistance.
  - Regular (not "as needed") paracetamol/NSAIDs for analgesia; topical analgesics are discouraged given limited efficacy and potential interference with the topical antibiotic itself.
  - **Refractory beyond 72 hours:** reassess compliance/technique and adequate drug delivery before assuming treatment failure; **refractory beyond 2 weeks, or any focal neurological signs (e.g. facial nerve palsy) or periauricular cellulitis, warrants urgent ENT referral** for possible necrotising otitis externa — see the danger box above for the specific Indigenous Australian risk context, not repeated here.
  - **Prevention:** ear plugs during water exposure, drying the canal with a low-heat hairdryer or acetic-acid/alcohol drops (e.g. **Aqua Ear**) after swimming, avoiding cotton bud use.

> [!danger] If the tympanic membrane is perforated or its integrity is uncertain, avoid non-quinolone/aminoglycoside-containing agents (ototoxicity risk) — use a quinolone-based agent instead, per the framework above.

> [!tip] Administration of topical antibiotics: lie down with the affected ear up; put as many drops as needed to fill the ear canal; massage the ear canal and pinna to help the drops go in; remain in this position for ≥5 minutes; avoid exposing the ear to water.

- **Complications:** malignant otitis externa (see below); CN VII palsy (poor prognosis).
- **P:** 65–90% resolve within 7–10 days.

### 0.1.1 Malignant otitis externa

- **D:** osteomyelitis of the skull base.
- **R:** diabetes, immunocompromise.
- **A:** *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* most commonly.
- **P:** infection spreads from the soft tissues of the external auditory meatus → bony ear canal → temporal bone/skull base.
- **S/Smx:** severe, unrelenting, deep otalgia; temporal headache; purulent discharge; ± dysphagia, hoarseness, CN VII dysfunction.
- **Ix:** CT head.
- **Mx:** urgent referral to ENT; IV antibiotics covering *Pseudomonas*.

## 0.2 Causes of otalgia (painful ear)

> [!note] Persistent unilateral otalgia (especially with a normal ear exam) or unilateral middle ear effusion in an adult is itself a red-flag symptom for head and neck cancer, given referred pain from the pharynx/larynx via the glossopharyngeal or vagus nerve is a recognised presentation — see [[13_06a_ENT_-_Dysphonia_and_HNSCC]] Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) for the full red-flag list, not repeated here. This doesn't apply to the benign local causes below when the ear examination itself explains the pain.

- Otitis externa + malignant OE.
- Otitis media — acute or chronic.
- **Furunculosis** — a painful Staph abscess arising in a hair follicle within the canal. Lance with a sucker fine end; treat with oral antibiotics; check for diabetes.
- **Barotrauma** — severe pain ± secondary effusion as a transudate or haemotympanum. Prevention: don't fly with a URTI, use decongestants, repeatedly yawn/swallow/move jaw.
- **TMJ dysfunction** — earache, facial pain, joint clicking related to teeth-grinding + stress. Conservative treatment ± analgesia.

## 0.3 Causes of a discharging ear

- Otitis media — acute or chronic.
- **Eustachian tube dysfunction:** closed (blocked) vs constantly open (patulous — "sniffing"). Blocked management: grommet insertion, balloon dilation. Patulous management: topical intranasal irritants to cause oedema of the Eustachian tube orifice; also surgery.
- Otitis externa + malignant OE.
- **Cholesteatoma:** peak incidence age 5–15. S/Smx: discharge ± deafness, headache, pain, facial paralysis, vertigo — signs of impending CNS compromise. Mx: mastoid surgery.
- **Mastoiditis:** S/Smx — fever, tenderness, swelling, redness behind the pinna, protruding auricle. CT to diagnose. Mx: antibiotics, myringotomy ± mastoidectomy.

## 0.4 Otitis media (acute)

- **D:** inflammation/infection involving the middle ear space.
- **R:** day care attendance, older siblings, young age, family history, supine feeding, craniofacial anomaly, immunocompromise.
- **A/P:** usually preceded by a URTI. Most infections are bacterial — *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, and *M. catarrhalis*.
- **S/Smx:** otalgia due to a bulging tympanic membrane; ear discharge if the TM ruptures (with less or no otalgia); fever in 50%; hearing loss; recent URTI (usually viral).

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with a specific, critical diagnostic pitfall — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children experience some of the highest rates of otitis media in the world.** In remote Northern Territory community surveys, up to 90% of Aboriginal children had some form of otitis media; chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM, "runny ears") reaches 12–46% in some communities — the WHO defines a CSOM prevalence **above just 4%** as "a massive public health problem requiring urgent attention," meaning affected communities can see rates **up to 10 times** this crisis threshold. Onset is typically earlier, and episodes more frequent, more severe, and more persistent, than in non-Indigenous children — often beginning in the first months of life.
> - **The single most important, genuinely easy-to-miss clinical pitfall: the severe pain classically associated with a bulging TM is rarely experienced by Aboriginal infants and young children in remote areas.** This means the standard S/Smx above (relying on otalgia as a presenting complaint) systematically under-detects AOM in this population — a child can have significant, active middle ear disease with no pain, no fever, and no obvious distress, meaning **routine, proactive ear examination (not waiting for the child to present unwell) is genuinely necessary** rather than optional in high-risk communities and age groups.
> - **Consequences are genuinely lifelong, not just an ear problem**: the associated conductive hearing loss occurs during the critical window for speech and language development, and is linked to language delay, poor school attendance, and long-term educational disadvantage — the downstream impact extends well beyond otology.
> - **Current national guidelines (Centre of Research Excellence in Ear and Hearing Health of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Children, 2020) define a distinct "high risk of treatment failure" category** — living in a remote community, age <2 years, first episode before 6 months of age, family history of CSOM, current/previous TM perforation, craniofacial abnormality, or existing hearing loss — patients meeting this threshold warrant a lower threshold for treatment and closer follow-up than the general-population approach below.
- **Ix:** otoscopy — bulging TM → loss of light reflex; opacification/erythema of the TM (indicates middle ear effusion); perforation + purulent otorrhoea.
- **Mx:**
  - Analgesia alone — 60% of cases resolve in 24h without antibiotics; consider a delayed prescription.
  - Antibiotics if: symptoms ≥4 days with no improvement; systemically unwell; immunocompromised; <2 years old with bilateral OM; OM with perforation ± discharge.
  - Antibiotics: **amoxicillin 30mg/kg/dose (max 1g) 12-hourly for 5–7 days** — this is the Australian licensed/Therapeutic Guidelines dose (60mg/kg/day total), genuinely **lower** than the "high-dose" 80–90mg/kg/day convention more commonly cited in US guidelines, given Australian prescribing has not adopted the higher dose despite it being studied and licensed overseas — worth being aware of this international variation rather than defaulting to the higher figure from memory. Or erythromycin/clarithromycin if penicillin-allergic.
- **Complications:** perforation of the TM → can cause chronic otitis media (if >6 weeks); hearing loss; labyrinthitis. Can lead to cholesteatoma, which can in turn lead to mastoiditis, meningitis, brain abscess, CN VII palsy.

## 0.5 Glue ear (otitis media with effusion / serous otitis media)

> [!info] Gap-filled — hearing loss was stated as a feature without explaining the mechanism. **Why middle ear fluid causes hearing loss specifically, and why it's conductive rather than sensorineural**: normal hearing depends on the tympanic membrane and the three ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) vibrating freely to transmit sound energy from the air-filled outer/middle ear into the fluid-filled inner ear (cochlea) — see [[Examination]] Rinne and Weber Tests for the broader conductive vs sensorineural framework this fits into, not repeated here. When the middle ear space fills with effusion (fluid) instead of air, this free vibration is **mechanically dampened** — the TM and ossicular chain can't move as efficiently through fluid as through air, impairing sound transmission before it ever reaches the cochlea. This is precisely why the hearing loss is **conductive** (a problem with sound transmission through the outer/middle ear) rather than sensorineural (which would reflect cochlear or nerve dysfunction) — the cochlea itself is unaffected, hearing is impaired purely by the fluid barrier upstream of it, which is also why the hearing loss typically resolves once the effusion clears.

- **R:** M>F, siblings with glue ear, spring/winter, bottle feeding, day care attendance, parental smoking.
- **S/Smx:** peaks at age 2. Hearing loss in 80% — most often noticed by parents/teachers (poor listening, poor speech, language delay, inattention). Can be painless.
- **Otoscopy:** variable appearance of the TM — dull, grey, yellow; bubbles or a fluid level. Otoscopic diagnosis is very inaccurate.
- **Ix:** refer for formal assessment of hearing — audiograms (conductive defects), tympanometry (look for flat "Type B" trace).
- **Mx:**
  - Active observation for 3 months — 50% of children with bilateral hearing loss of 20dB are likely to resolve in 3 months; reassess hearing at 3 months.

> [!danger] If Down syndrome or cleft palate, refer to ENT urgently due to increased risk of infections.

  - Autoinflation of the Eustachian tube.
  - **Surgery** — for persistent bilateral OME + hearing level in the better ear of 25–30dBHL or worse over 3 months.
    - Grommets: allow air to pass through to the middle ear. Risks: infection, tympanosclerosis. Aural cleaning, topical antibiotics; may require removal. Swimming is OK, but no diving; use ear plugs/swim hats. Extrude after 3–12 months, recheck hearing; 25% need re-insertion.
    - Tympanostomy for adults.
  - Hearing aids for persistent bilateral OME and hearing loss if surgery is not acceptable.

> [!warning] Topical or systemic antibiotics are NOT recommended for glue ear; also not recommended: dietary changes, antihistamines, decongestants, steroids, acupuncture.

## 0.6 Chronic otitis media (COM)

- **D:** tympanic membrane perforation in the setting of recurrent/chronic infections (>6 weeks).
- **Types:** benign COM (dry), chronic serous OM, chronic suppurative OM.
- **S/Smx:** hearing loss, otorrhoea, fullness, otalgia.
- **Mx:** topical or PO antibiotics, aural cleaning, water precautions, follow-up appointments ± surgery (grommets, etc).
- **Complications:** cholesteatoma.
