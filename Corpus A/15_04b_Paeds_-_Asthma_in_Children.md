## Asthma in children

- **D:** reversible airway obstruction ± expiratory polyphonic wheeze, dyspnoea, or cough. Prevalence ~10%.
- **R:** ↓birth weight, family history, bottle fed, atopy, M>F, pollution, past lung disease.
- **Dx:** no gold standard. Spirometry (FEV1/FVC <70%); bronchodilator reversibility test (↑FEV1 ≥12%); FeNO.

> [!info] Verified — the Australian Asthma Handbook (National Asthma Council) has been independently verified for the adult/adolescent presentation in [[02_Respiratory]] Asthma, confirming Australia has moved to essentially the same AIR/MART-based stepped approach as current UK guidance (both now reflect GINA international consensus) — but Australia uses its own "Treatment Level" numbering rather than the NICE step numbers, so the specific step labels below should be translated to AU terminology, not treated as identical, before use in an Australian context. Paediatric-specific asthma management (particularly for children <5, where diagnosis and treatment approach differs meaningfully from older children/adults given the difficulty confirming the diagnosis at this age) has its own dedicated section in the Australian Asthma Handbook — check this directly for the current AU-specific paediatric step structure and drug choices given the <5-years age band especially is genuinely different in approach from adult asthma management, not just a scaled-down version of it.

### Management in children <5 years old

1. Newly diagnosed asthma: **SABA (short-acting beta-2 agonist** — salbutamol, the blue reliever puffer**)**.
2. Newly diagnosed asthma with ≥3 weeks of symptoms or night-time waking: SABA + 8-week trial of moderate dose **ICS (inhaled corticosteroid** — the preventer**)**. After 8 weeks, stop ICS and monitor response — if symptoms don't resolve, reconsider the diagnosis; if symptoms restart within 4 weeks of stopping ICS, restart at low-dose maintenance; if symptoms restart after 4 weeks, repeat the 8-week trial of moderate dose ICS.
3. SABA + low dose ICS + leukotriene receptor antagonist (LTRA).
4. Stop LTRA, refer to an asthma specialist.

### Management in children 5–16 years old (similar to adults)

1. Newly diagnosed asthma: SABA.
2. Newly diagnosed asthma with ≥3 weeks of symptoms or night-time waking: SABA + paediatric low dose ICS.
3. SABA + low dose ICS + LTRA.
4. SABA + low dose ICS + LABA.
5. SABA + switch ICS or LABA for MART (Maintenance and Reliever Therapy) that includes low dose ICS.
6. SABA + MART (moderate dose ICS).
7. SABA + one of: MART (high dose ICS); trial of an additional drug (e.g. theophylline); referral to an asthma specialist.

## Acute attacks in ages 2–5

| Severity | Features |
|---|---|
| **Moderate** | SpO2 >92%; no features of severe asthma |
| **Severe** | SpO2 <92%; too breathless to talk/feed; HR >140; RR >40; use of accessory neck muscles |
| **Life-threatening** | SpO2 <92%; silent chest; poor respiratory effort; agitation; cyanosis; altered consciousness |

## Acute attacks in ages 5–16

| Severity | Features |
|---|---|
| **Moderate** | SpO2 >92%; PEF >50% best or predicted; no features of severe asthma |
| **Severe** | SpO2 <92%; PEF 33–50% best or predicted; too breathless to talk/feed or cannot complete sentences; HR >125; RR >30; use of accessory neck muscles |
| **Life-threatening** | SpO2 <92%; PEF <33% best or predicted; silent chest; poor respiratory effort; agitation; cyanosis; altered consciousness |

- Attempt to measure PEF.

### Management of mild to moderate attacks

- Bronchodilator therapy — SABA via spacer, 1 puff q30–60s, max 10 puffs. If symptoms not controlled, go to the Emergency Department.
- Steroid therapy for 3–5 days — dose 1–2mg/kg OD.

### Management of severe to life-threatening attacks

- As above + sit up, high flow O2.
- Get senior input ASAP ± ICU.
- Consider one IV dose of magnesium sulfate 40mg/kg over 20 minutes (≤2g) if poor response to nebulisers.
- Consider IV salbutamol over 10 minutes ± infusion (1–2mcg/kg/min), or aminophylline (5mg/kg IV over 20 minutes).
- Salbutamol nebulisers continuously until improving, then increase intervals.
- Consider starting CPAP in the ED.

## The Atopic Child (Atopic March / Atopic Multimorbidity)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("The atopic child," High yield) — genuinely absent as a unifying concept despite eczema and asthma both being individually built elsewhere ([[09_04_Dermatology_-_Eczema__Psoriasis__Rosacea]] Eczema (atopic dermatitis) and this file's Asthma in Children above) — this entry ties the individual conditions together as the connected pattern this topic is actually testing, and covers allergic rhinitis and food allergy, neither of which has a standalone entry elsewhere in this project.

**The core concept — "the atopic march":** a classically-described sequential pattern in which atopic dermatitis (eczema) presents first in infancy, followed by the later development of food allergy, allergic rhinitis, and asthma as the child grows older — reflecting an underlying shared predisposition (atopy: a personal/familial tendency to produce IgE antibodies and become sensitised to environmental triggers) rather than four unrelated coincidental diagnoses in the same child.

> [!tip] Genuinely important nuance for current understanding: **the atopic march as a strict, predictable sequential progression is now understood to be the less common pattern** — one recent analysis found a "classic" full march (eczema → food allergy → rhinitis → asthma, in that order) in only ~3% of children with atopic disease. The current, more accurate framing is **"atopic multimorbidity"** — these conditions cluster together and share risk factors and pathophysiology, but the timing, sequence, and combination genuinely varies between children rather than following one fixed order. Both framings are worth knowing: "atopic march" for the classic teaching concept and underlying shared-mechanism logic, "atopic multimorbidity" for the more accurate current picture of how these conditions actually cluster in practice.

**Shared pathophysiological thread:** an epithelial/skin barrier defect (e.g. filaggrin gene mutations — see [[09_04_Dermatology_-_Eczema__Psoriasis__Rosacea]] Eczema (atopic dermatitis) for the specific detail already built there, not repeated here) is increasingly understood as a genuinely important initiating event, allowing allergen sensitisation through the disrupted skin barrier — this "outside-in" sensitisation route is a meaningful part of why treating eczema early and effectively is thought to matter beyond just the skin itself, though this remains an active area of research rather than settled fact.

**The four conditions and where each is built:**
- **Atopic dermatitis (eczema)** — typically the earliest presentation, often in infancy; see [[09_04_Dermatology_-_Eczema__Psoriasis__Rosacea]] Eczema (atopic dermatitis) for the full entry, not repeated here.
- **Food allergy** — commonly emerges after or alongside eczema in infancy; cow's milk protein intolerance specifically is built in [[15_09b_Paeds_-_Infant_Feeding_Problems]] Cow's milk protein intolerance, not repeated here — food allergy more broadly (IgE-mediated reactions to other common allergens such as egg, peanut, tree nuts) is not yet built as its own standalone entry in this project.
- **Allergic rhinitis (hay fever):** typically emerges in early-to-mid childhood — not yet built as its own standalone entry in this project; presents with sneezing, nasal congestion/discharge, itchy/watery eyes, often seasonal (specific pollens) or perennial (dust mite, pet dander) depending on trigger. **"United airway disease"** is a related concept worth knowing — asthma and allergic rhinitis are increasingly understood as manifestations of a single shared inflammatory process across a continuous airway, rather than two separate diseases that simply happen to co-occur, which is part of why treating allergic rhinitis well can improve asthma control and vice versa.
- **Asthma** — often the last condition to emerge in the classic sequence, typically in later childhood; see Asthma in Children above in this file, not repeated here.

**Clinical relevance of recognising the pattern (whether "march" or "multimorbidity"):**
- **A child presenting with early, more severe eczema is at meaningfully higher risk of later developing the other atopic conditions** — this is worth flagging to families proactively rather than only addressing each condition reactively as it separately arises, given the genuine predictive value of early eczema severity.
- **A personal or family history of one atopic condition should raise the index of suspicion for the others** — e.g. a wheezy child with a personal or family history of eczema/allergic rhinitis is more likely to have asthma specifically (rather than, say, viral-induced wheeze alone) as the explanation, and this history should actively inform the diagnostic reasoning rather than being noted only in passing; see [[15_04a_Paeds_-_URTI_and_LRTI]] Viral-induced wheeze (VIW) for the relevant differential this feeds into, not repeated here.
- **Optimising control of one atopic condition may benefit the others**, per the united airway disease concept above — this is part of the rationale for treating allergic rhinitis proactively in a child with asthma, not just treating the asthma in isolation.
