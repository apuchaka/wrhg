## Urinary tract infection (UTI)

- Bacteriuria = bacteria in urine uncontaminated by urethral flora.
- More common in boys until 3 months old; overall, 8% of girls and 2% of boys have a UTI in childhood. Up to 40% have a GU abnormality.

> [!info] Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) — urine flowing backwards from the bladder to the kidneys via the ureters.
> - Grade I: incomplete filling of the upper urinary tract without dilatation.
> - Grade II: complete filling ± slight dilation.
> - Grade III: ballooned calyces.
> - Grade IV: megaureter.
> - Grade V: + hydronephrosis.
>
> Reflux nephropathy: renal scarring secondary to reflux, which can lead to hypertension and ESRD.

### Presentation

- Infant: poor feeding, vomiting, irritability.
- Younger children: abdominal pain, fever, dysuria.
- Older children: dysuria, frequency, haematuria.
- UTI: temperature >38°C, loin pain/tenderness.

### Investigation

- If asymptomatic, DO NOT test urine.
- Test urine if: symptoms suggestive of UTI; unexplained fever ≥38°C; alternative site of infection identified but remains unwell.
- Urine sample: clean catch preferred; if not, use urine collection pads. Invasive methods (e.g. suprapubic aspiration) as a last resort.

### Management

> [!info] Verified — trimethoprim, cephalexin/other cephalosporins, and amoxicillin all remain genuine current options for paediatric UTI in Australia too (not UK-specific choices), with gentamicin commonly used for upper UTI/pyelonephritis in Australian paediatric antibiotic guidelines specifically (a detail worth adding, given it's less prominent in the original note) — trimethoprim resistance rates should be considered locally given genuine, non-trivial resistance exists in community isolates, but this is a general antimicrobial stewardship consideration rather than an AU-specific correction. Check current eTG or local paediatric antibiogram for the precise current first-line choice and duration given antibiotic guidelines are reviewed regularly and resistance patterns are genuinely local/time-variable.

- Infants <3 months old — immediate referral to paediatrician.
- Upper UTI: consider admission. PO antibiotics (cephalosporin or co-amoxiclav) for 7–10 days.
- Lower UTI: trimethoprim, nitrofurantoin, cephalosporin, or amoxicillin for 3 days — come back if still unwell in 24–48h.
- Consider prophylaxis if recurrent.

## Nephrotic syndrome

> [!info] Defined by the presence of: (1) heavy proteinuria; (2) hypoalbuminaemia (<30g/L); (3) peripheral oedema. Not a single disease, but a syndrome caused by several renal diseases.
>
> [!danger] **The proteinuria threshold here was "> 3.5g per 24h" — the adult figure, in the paediatric entry.** It is an **absolute** quantity, so it does not scale: a 15kg child in florid nephrotic syndrome will never excrete 3.5g in a day, and applying this number to a child excludes the diagnosis it is supposed to define. Paediatric nephrotic-range proteinuria is **indexed to body size** — expressed per square metre per hour, or as a urine protein:creatinine ratio on a spot sample — rather than as an absolute daily mass. **The specific Australian paediatric threshold is not stated here because it has not been verified** — see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B64**. The absolute figure has been removed rather than replaced. Found by the G26 round, 2026-08-29; the adult entry is [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome.

### Aetiology

- **Minimal change disease** — possibly idiopathic; secondary to Hodgkin's lymphoma, leukaemia, etc. Peak onset <6 years old, M:F 2:1.
- Intrinsic kidney disease: FSGS.
- Secondary to systemic illness: HSP, diabetes, infection, amyloidosis.

### Pathophysiology

- Podocytes are affected → glomerular proteinuria.
- Urinary loss of albumin secondary to glomerular damage — the liver tries to compensate for protein loss, and in the process, there is ↑cholesterol production.
- Oedema secondary to ↓oncotic pressure from hypoalbuminaemia + primary renal Na retention.
- Hypercoagulability and thrombosis secondary to loss of anticoagulant proteins.

### S/Smx

- A well child with insidious onset of pitting oedema, initially periorbital, then generalised. Often a history of recent URTI.
- May progress to anorexia, GI changes, irritability, ascites, oliguria, SOB.
- Risk of infection and thrombosis.

### Investigation

- Urine: frothy, protein 3+, PCR >200, Na <10.
- Blood: albumin <25, U&Es, lipids, C3/C4 (normal), Hb.
- MCD: urinalysis will show small molecular weight proteins and hyaline casts.

> [!note] Visible haematuria, hypertension, and markedly ↑creatinine are NOT typical features of nephrotic syndrome.

### Management

> [!info] Verified — the mg/m² body-surface-area-based steroid dosing structure for nephrotic syndrome (high-dose induction, then alternate-day tapering) is a long-established, internationally standard approach (originating from the classic ISKDC regimen and reflected in current KDIGO international guidelines) rather than a UK-specific protocol — this dosing structure is genuinely shared across jurisdictions including Australia, not something requiring wholesale replacement. Check current Australian paediatric nephrology guidance or eTG for the precise current total course duration and exact tapering schedule given steroid-course-length recommendations for nephrotic syndrome have evolved somewhat over recent years internationally (some evidence supports shorter courses than older regimens), but the core dosing structure and steroid-sensitive-vs-resistant distinction below remain clinically sound.

- Managed by an experienced paediatrician.
- High dose steroids (prednisolone): 60mg/m²/day (max 80mg) for 4 weeks, then 40mg/m² alternate days for 4 weeks, then taper. 80% are steroid sensitive; 80% will relapse at some point and require more steroids. Patients who struggle to wean are referred to as "steroid dependent". For steroid resistant disease, use ACE inhibitors and immunosuppressants (e.g. tacrolimus, ciclosporin).
- Low salt diet (high protein diet NOT recommended).
- Diuretics for oedema.
- Albumin infusion PRN.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis if severe.

> [!danger] Live vaccines are contraindicated in children receiving high dose prednisolone.

### Complications

- Hypovolaemia — fluid leaks from the intravascular space into the interstitial space → oedema and low BP.
- Thrombosis — kidneys leak clotting factors.
- Infection — kidney leaks immunoglobulins + use of steroids.
- Acute or chronic renal failure.
- Relapse: defined as ≥3+ proteinuria for 3 consecutive days.

### Minimal change disease

- **D:** the most common form of nephrotic syndrome in children, characterised by minimal histological changes in the kidney.
- **R:** age 1–8, Hodgkin's lymphoma, leukaemia, recent viral illness.
- **A:** 90% idiopathic.
- **S/Smx:** nephrotic syndrome; normotensive; highly selective proteinuria (e.g. albumin, transferrin).
- **Ix:** renal biopsy (normal glomeruli, fusion of podocytes, effacement of foot processes).
- **Mx:** as per nephrotic syndrome.
- **P:** 1/3 have frequent relapses, 1/3 have infrequent relapses.

## Acute glomerulonephritis

- **D:** glomerular injury usually involving inflammatory changes in the glomerular capillaries and the GBM.
- **A:** leukocyte infiltration, antibody deposition, complement activation. Causes: infections; systemic inflammatory conditions (e.g. SLE, RA, anti-GBM disease); drugs (e.g. lithium, PTU, NSAIDs); metabolic disorders (e.g. diabetes); malignancy; hereditary disorders (e.g. Fabry's disease, Alport syndrome); deposition diseases (e.g. amyloidosis).
- **P:** most nephritic syndromes are triggered by immune-mediated injury.

> [!info] Features: haematuria with red cell casts (macro or microscopic); proteinuria; oliguria; hypertension (~50%, more common in nephritic syndrome).

> [!note] A patient can have a "nephritic syndrome" (inflammation of the kidneys) resulting in "nephrotic syndrome" (the triad of proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia, and peripheral oedema) — especially if glomerular inflammation damages the podocytes, causing severe proteinuria.

### Complicated presentations

- Hypertensive encephalopathy: restless, drowsy, severe headache, fits, ↓vision, vomiting, coma.
- AKI: acidosis, hyperkalaemia, seizure, stupor.
- Fluid overload: pulmonary oedema, cardiac failure.
- Mixed nephritic-nephrotic syndrome.

### Investigation and management

- **Dx/Ix:** urinalysis, bloods, throat swabs, CXR (if fluid overload suspected). Renal biopsy if severe.
- **Mx:** supportive. Treat underlying infection/cause. Monitor fluid balance — fluid restriction if oliguric, diuretics if overloaded. Check BP often + manage hypertension + low salt diet. If encephalopathy — give sodium nitroprusside. Haemodialysis if severe renal failure.

### Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis

- The most common cause of acute GN in children.
- Presents 7–21 days after strep infection (pharyngitis, impetigo).
- **S/Smx:** hypertension, haematuria ± oedema, malaise, anorexia, fever, abdominal pain.
- **Dx/Ix:** clinical + confirm recent strep infection with ASO titre, anti-DNAse B, throat swab.
- **Mx:** supportive. PO penicillin for 10 days to reduce spread of nephritogenic bacterial strains. Oedema usually resolves in 5–10 days; hypertension, haematuria, proteinuria may last several weeks or months.
- **P:** very good; 95% recovery.

## Enuresis (urinary incontinence)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Enuresis and Encopresis," Medium yield) — genuinely absent despite being an extremely common paediatric presentation, placed here given the topical/anatomical relevance to the rest of this file.

- **D:** involuntary urinary incontinence in a child old enough that continence would normally be expected (conventionally age ≥5 years) — **primary enuresis** (the child has never achieved a sustained period of dryness) is distinguished from **secondary enuresis** (a return of wetting after a prior dry period of ≥6 months), given this distinction meaningfully changes the differential.
- **A/P:** primary nocturnal enuresis is usually a developmental/maturational delay in bladder control (often with a family history), rather than an underlying pathological cause — genuinely the most common scenario and important to recognise as such, given inappropriately investigating every case as if it were pathological causes unnecessary anxiety and cost. Secondary enuresis is more likely to reflect an underlying trigger — UTI (see [[15_10_Paeds_-_UTI__Nephrotic_Syndrome__Glomerulonephritis]] Urinary tract infection (UTI) above, not repeated here), constipation (a genuinely common and under-recognised contributor, given a loaded rectum can mechanically reduce bladder capacity), diabetes (new-onset polyuria — see [[15_16b_Paeds_-_Diabetes_Mellitus__MODY__DKA]] Type 1 diabetes mellitus, not repeated here), or psychological/emotional stress (a new sibling, family disruption, bullying, or other significant life change).
- **S/Smx:** nocturnal (the most common pattern, "bedwetting") vs diurnal (daytime) wetting — diurnal enuresis is more likely to reflect an underlying bladder dysfunction or anatomical/neurological cause and generally warrants a lower threshold for further assessment than isolated nocturnal enuresis. Screen for associated symptoms: dysuria/frequency (UTI), straining/hard stool/soiling (constipation, see Encopresis below), polyuria/polydipsia (diabetes), and psychosocial stressors.
- **Ix:** primarily clinical — a careful history (including a bladder diary if diagnosis is unclear) and examination (including an abdominal exam to screen for a palpable loaded colon suggesting constipation) is usually sufficient; urinalysis to screen for UTI or glucosuria; further investigation (renal tract ultrasound, referral) reserved for atypical features (diurnal enuresis, secondary enuresis without an obvious trigger, associated red flags).
- **Mx:**
  - **Immediate/acute:** address any identified underlying cause first (treat UTI or constipation, optimise diabetes control) — treating enuresis as a standalone symptom without addressing a genuine underlying driver is unlikely to succeed.
  - **Definitive:** reassurance and general advice for uncomplicated primary nocturnal enuresis in younger children (fluid management — adequate daytime fluid intake with reduced intake before bed, regular voiding, avoiding punitive responses which are genuinely counterproductive and can worsen the child's distress without improving continence); an **enuresis alarm** (a moisture-sensing device that wakes the child at the first sign of wetting, training a conditioned response over weeks) is the most effective first-line intervention for children old enough to engage with it, given it addresses the underlying maturational delay directly rather than just suppressing symptoms; desmopressin (reduces nocturnal urine production) is a reasonable option for short-term control (e.g. sleepovers, camps) or where an alarm has failed/isn't appropriate, though it doesn't address the underlying maturational delay the way an alarm does.
  - **Chronic/long-term:** most primary nocturnal enuresis resolves with time/maturation regardless of intervention, and this natural history is itself an important part of reassuring the family; persistent enuresis despite the measures above, or the presence of red flag features, warrants referral to paediatric urology/nephrology.

## Encopresis (faecal incontinence)

- **D:** involuntary or voluntary passage of stool in an inappropriate location in a child old enough that continence would be expected — most commonly **overflow incontinence secondary to chronic constipation**, which is the single most important concept to understand about this presentation, given it changes both the framing of the problem for families and the treatment approach.
- **A/P:** chronic constipation leads to a large, hard faecal mass in the rectum; the rectum becomes chronically over-distended and loses normal sensation, and liquid stool from higher in the bowel leaks around the impacted mass — this **overflow** soiling is frequently mistaken by families (and sometimes clinicians) for diarrhoea or deliberate/behavioural soiling, when the actual underlying problem is severe constipation, not loose stool or defiance; correcting this misunderstanding is often itself a meaningful part of management, given how much shame and family conflict can build up around a presentation that's actually a straightforward (if severe) constipation problem.
- **R:** functional/dietary constipation is the overwhelming majority of cases; toilet-training difficulties or a history of a painful bowel movement leading to stool-withholding behaviour (a genuinely important vicious cycle — withholding worsens the constipation, which makes the next bowel motion even more painful); psychosocial stressors; rarely, an organic cause (e.g. Hirschsprung's disease — see [[15_08_Paeds_-_Surgical_Abdomen__Appendicitis__Intussusception__Pyloric_Stenosis__Hirschsprung__Oesophageal_Atresia_]] Hirschsprung disease, not repeated here — though this is typically identified earlier in infancy given the classic delayed meconium passage, making it a less common cause of encopresis presenting later in childhood specifically).
- **S/Smx:** soiling (often small amounts, sometimes mistaken for diarrhoea by families as above), a palpable abdominal faecal mass, infrequent bowel motions, pain on defecation, stool-withholding behaviour (typically posturing — stiffening, clenching buttocks — rather than actually attempting to pass stool), abdominal pain.
- **Ix:** primarily clinical (history and abdominal ± gentle rectal examination if needed and appropriate) — imaging is not routinely required for straightforward functional constipation with overflow soiling; screen for red flags (delayed meconium passage in the neonatal history, ribbon-like stools, growth faltering) that would raise concern for an organic cause such as Hirschsprung's disease.
- **Mx:**
  - **Immediate/acute:** disimpaction of the faecal mass first, given ongoing maintenance treatment cannot succeed while a large impacted mass remains — typically with an escalating osmotic laxative regimen (e.g. macrogol-based) per current local paediatric guidance.
  - **Definitive:** ongoing maintenance laxative therapy (typically continued for months, not weeks, given the rectum needs time to recover its normal calibre and sensation after chronic over-distension) alongside behavioural measures — a regular toileting routine (sitting on the toilet at consistent times, particularly after meals to use the gastrocolic reflex), a reward-based (not punitive) approach to engagement with the toileting routine, and adequate dietary fibre and fluid intake.
  - **Chronic/long-term:** family education is genuinely central to successful treatment — explicitly reframing the presentation as an overflow-from-constipation problem (not deliberate soiling or "diarrhoea") reduces family conflict and shame, which in turn improves the child's engagement with treatment; relapse is common if maintenance laxatives are stopped too early, so gradual, guided weaning rather than abrupt cessation is the standard approach.
