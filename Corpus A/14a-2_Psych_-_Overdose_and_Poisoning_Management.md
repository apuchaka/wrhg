> [!warning] This is a quick-reference antidote table only — always confirm doses and current protocols against Australian poisons information (Poisons Information Centre 13 11 26) and eTG before treating any overdose.

## 0.1 Overdose / poisoning — management by agent

| Agent | Management |
|---|---|
| **Benzodiazepines** | Antidote: flumazenil. Otherwise, supportive management. |
| **Beta-blockers** | If HR <40 or unstable: atropine, glucagon. |
| **Carbon monoxide** | 100% oxygen; hyperbaric oxygen. |
| **Cyanide** | Hydroxocobalamin (vitamin B12); or combination of amyl nitrite, sodium nitrite, and sodium thiosulfate. |
| **Digoxin** | Digibind; correct potassium levels and manage arrhythmias. |
| **Ethylene glycol & methanol** | Fomepizole (inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase); ethanol if fomepizole unavailable; haemodialysis if refractory. |
| **Heparin** | Protamine sulfate. |
| **Iron** | Desferrioxamine (iron chelating agent). |
| **Lead poisoning** | Dimercaprol, calcium edetate. |
| **Organophosphates (insecticides)** | Atropine ± pralidoxime. |
| **Paracetamol** | See [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Paracetamol Overdose (N-acetylcysteine, activated charcoal). |
| **Salicylates (e.g. aspirin)** | IV bicarbonate (urinary alkalinisation); charcoal if presenting early; haemodialysis if severe. |
| **Tricyclic antidepressants** | IV bicarbonate — ↓risk of seizures and arrhythmias. |
| **Lithium** | Volume resuscitation (IV fluids); haemodialysis if severe; ± IV sodium bicarbonate. |
| **Warfarin** | See [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]] Warfarin — management of high INR (Vitamin K, PCC, FFP, etc). |

> [!danger] Two things the antidote table above does not cover
> **Delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae.** **Cognitive impairment, personality change, parkinsonism and memory deficits can develop days to weeks after apparent recovery**, in patients who were treated and discharged well. **Warn the patient and arrange follow-up** — otherwise the connection is never made, because by the time symptoms appear the poisoning is history.
> **Pregnancy.** **Fetal haemoglobin binds carbon monoxide with higher affinity and clears it more slowly**, so **the fetus is at greater risk than the maternal carboxyhaemoglobin level suggests.** A maternal level that would be reassuring in a non-pregnant patient is not reassuring here. `UNVERIFIED — the treatment thresholds in pregnancy, per the Poisons Information Centre.`

## Added from unverified layer — adverse drug reactions, and reporting them in Australia
`SRC:A5_Toxicology_II_-_Poisoned_Patient__ADRs_and_Immunotherapy §0.3` `UNVERIFIED — model knowledge, not source-checked.`

> [!tip] **Type A versus Type B** — the split that decides what you do next
> - **Type A ("Augmented")** — an **exaggeration of the drug's known pharmacology**. **Dose-related, predictable, common**, and usually managed by **dose reduction** rather than cessation. Bleeding on warfarin, bradycardia on a beta-blocker, hypoglycaemia on insulin.
> - **Type B ("Bizarre")** — **not predictable from the pharmacology**, not dose-related, uncommon, and usually **requires stopping the drug and avoiding it in future**. Anaphylaxis, DRESS, idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity.
> **The classification is the decision:** a Type A reaction rarely needs the drug abandoned; a Type B one usually does, and it belongs on the allergy list rather than in the notes.
> (Note: `Type A / Type B` is used in this vault for at least two other things — **WPW** pathways and **Stanford** aortic dissection at [[01_Cardiovascular]]. Unrelated.)

> [!info] Reporting — the Australian mechanism
> Adverse events are reported to the **TGA**, which maintains the **Database of Adverse Event Notifications (DAEN)**, publicly searchable. Newly registered medicines carry a **Black Triangle**, indicating they are under **additional monitoring** and that reporting is particularly encouraged. **Reporting is not restricted to confirmed reactions** — suspicion is the threshold.

## 0.2 Digoxin toxicity — detail

- **S/Smx:** generally unwell, anorexia, confusion, yellow-green vision, arrhythmias, gynaecomastia.
- Toxicity may be precipitated by hypokalaemia (and ↓Mg, ↑Ca, ↑Na, acidosis), renal failure, MI, hypoalbuminaemia, hypothyroidism.
- **Drug-drug interactions:** quinidine, verapamil, diltiazem, spironolactone, ciclosporin, thiazides, loop diuretics.

## 0.3 Salicylate toxicity — detail

- **S/Smx:** respiratory alkalosis (secondary to hyperventilation), then metabolic acidosis; tinnitus; GI upset/bleeding; seizures; coma.

## 0.4 Tricyclic antidepressant toxicity — detail

> [!danger] If arrhythmias develop, DO NOT use class 1a and 1c antiarrhythmics; avoid class 3 drugs.

> [!tip] Added from unverified layer — the ECG is the prognostic test, and **aVR** is the lead to look at
> `SRC:A5_Toxicology_II_-_Poisoned_Patient__ADRs_and_Immunotherapy §0.1` `UNVERIFIED — model knowledge, not source-checked. QRS duration thresholds omitted.`
> **A patient can look well and be arresting within the hour. The anticholinergic features are the distraction; the ECG is the prognostic test.**
> **QRS widening** is the key finding and its degree tracks the risk of seizures and ventricular arrhythmia. Alongside it, **a terminal R wave in aVR** with a rightward terminal QRS axis is the characteristic sodium-channel-blockade sign.
> Sodium bicarbonate is the treatment for QRS widening — see [[NEW_Drugs_17_Psychotropic]], which carries it.
