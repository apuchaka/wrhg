## Hearing loss — differential diagnosis

- **Ix:** audiometry, tympanogram. If unilateral, MRI to rule out vestibular schwannoma.
- **Mx:** treat the underlying cause; hearing aids, psychological support, CBT.

> [!info] Conductive hearing loss — abnormal otoscopy
> - **External ear:** earwax (cerumen); foreign body; otitis externa; congenital (atresia); benign mass (polyp, osteoma, exostosis); tumour (SCC); dermatologic (e.g. moles).
> - **Middle ear:** otitis media; tympanic membrane perforation; cholesteatoma; barotrauma; tumours (glomus, adenoma); Eustachian tube dysfunction.

> [!info] Conductive hearing loss — normal otoscopy
> Otosclerosis; congenital; Eustachian tube malfunction.

> [!info] Sensorineural hearing loss — symmetrical
> - **Cochlear:** presbycusis; loud noise; cochleitis; ototoxic drugs (e.g. aminoglycosides); Ménière's disease; autoimmune disorders.
> - **Congenital:** hereditary hearing disorders; TORCH infections (toxoplasmosis, syphilis, hepatitis B, rubella, CMV, HSV); teratogenic drugs; alcohol.
> - **Central (CNS):** infection (e.g. meningitis); stroke/TIA; multiple sclerosis.

> [!info] Sensorineural hearing loss — asymmetric
> Neoplasm (vestibular schwannoma); iatrogenic (radiation, surgery); idiopathic.

## Tinnitus — differential diagnosis

- **Mx (general approach):** identify and treat any reversible underlying cause first (e.g. remove impacted earwax, review ototoxic medications, treat otitis media, manage thyroid dysfunction) — a surprising proportion of tinnitus has an identifiable, addressable driver rather than being purely idiopathic. For persistent tinnitus without a reversible cause: **hearing aids** if there's associated hearing loss (amplifying external sound can reduce the relative prominence of tinnitus); **sound therapy/masking** (white noise or similar background sound reduces the contrast between tinnitus and silence, particularly useful at night); **cognitive behavioural therapy** — genuinely evidence-supported for reducing the distress and functional impact of tinnitus, even though it doesn't eliminate the sound itself, given much of tinnitus's burden comes from the psychological/attentional response to it rather than the sound alone; **tinnitus retraining therapy** (a structured combination of counselling and sound therapy) for persistent, distressing cases. Pharmacological options have limited evidence overall; treat any co-existing anxiety/depression/insomnia actively given these commonly amplify perceived tinnitus severity and are themselves treatable.

> [!info] Subjective tinnitus (only the patient hears it)
>
> **Unilateral:** vestibular schwannoma; CN VIII lesion; brainstem lesion; multiple sclerosis; infarction; Ménière's disease.
>
> **Bilateral, no hearing loss:** metabolic (thyroid dysfunction, vitamin A/B/zinc deficiency); psychogenic/anxiety/depression; drugs (e.g. salicylates); idiopathic.
>
> **Bilateral, with hearing loss:**
> - Conductive HL: earwax impaction, otitis media, otosclerosis, external or middle ear lesion.
> - SNHL: loud noise, ototoxic drugs (propranolol, levodopa, loop diuretics), presbycusis, congenital.

> [!info] Somatosensory tinnitus (patient can modify it by moving head/jaw/eyes, or pressure on face)
> TMJ dysfunction; bruxism; whiplash; skull fracture; closed head injury.

> [!info] Objective tinnitus (pulsatile/rhythmic)
>
> **Muscular:** stapedius, tensor tympani, or palatal muscle spasm; degenerative disease of head & neck; Eustachian tube dysfunction.
>
> **Vascular — arterial:** normal blood flow in an artery near the ear; congenital (persistent stapedial artery); atherosclerosis; idiopathic intracranial hypertension; systemic hypertension (acute exacerbation); glomus tympanicum (benign tumour).
>
> **Vascular — venous:** AV shunt; high jugular bulb; glomus jugulare (tumour); hyperthyroidism.

## Vertigo

- **D:** true vertigo is the illusion of rotary movement of either the body or the environment.

### Central vestibular dysfunction

- Vertigo secondary to CNS causes.
- **S/Smx:** imbalance, bidirectional nystagmus, ± neurological symptoms/signs.

> [!info] Causes of central vertigo
> - **Vascular:** stroke/TIA at the brainstem, vertebrobasilar insufficiency, basilar artery migraine — vertebrobasilar ischaemia is usually seen in elderly patients who become dizzy when they bend their neck backwards.
> - **Infection:** meningitis, cerebellar or brainstem abscess.
> - **Inflammation:** multiple sclerosis.
> - **Intoxication:** barbiturates, alcohol.
> - **Trauma:** cerebellar contusion.
> - **Lesions:** infratentorial tumours, cerebellopontine angle tumours, glomus tumours.

### Peripheral vestibular dysfunction

- Vertigo secondary to peripheral nervous system causes (i.e. causes that affect CN VIII alone).
- **S/Smx:** unidirectional nystagmus, imbalance, etc.

> [!info] Causes of peripheral vertigo
> BPPV; labyrinthitis (± hearing loss ± tinnitus, history of recent viral infection); vestibular neuronitis (history of recent viral infection, no hearing loss); Ménière's disease; vestibular schwannoma; ototoxicity (e.g. secondary to drugs); otitis media; temporal bone fractures.
