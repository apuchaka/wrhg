# NEW — Investigations: Gastroenterology and Hepatology

Entries are **snippet-sourced**. Numerics appear only on three-source agreement; assay- or laboratory-dependent values are **omitted with the omission stated in place**.

## 0.1 Liver Function Panel (ALT, AST, ALP, Bilirubin, GGT)

**D:** Panel of hepatic enzymes and bilirubin. **"Liver function tests" is a misnomer** — ALT, AST, ALP and GGT are markers of hepatocyte injury or cholestasis, not of function. **True synthetic function is albumin, INR/prothrombin time and bilirubin.**

**Ind:** Suspected liver disease; jaundice; abdominal pain; monitoring hepatotoxic drugs; alcohol assessment; unexplained systemic illness; pre-operative assessment.

**Role:** First-line. Its main value is **pattern recognition**, not the individual numbers.

> [!info] **The R ratio — the single most useful derived value** (ACG definition)
> **R = (ALT ÷ ALT upper limit of normal) ÷ (ALP ÷ ALP upper limit of normal)**
> - **R > 5 → hepatocellular** injury
> - **R < 2 → cholestatic**
> - **R 2–5 → mixed** (with ALT ≥3× ULN and ALP ≥2× ULN)
>
> **AST:ALT ratio > 2** is reported in around **90%** of alcohol-related liver disease, attributed to pyridoxal phosphate depletion reducing ALT synthesis plus release of mitochondrial AST. **A raised GGT alongside supports that interpretation**; GGT's other main use is to confirm that a raised ALP is hepatic rather than bony.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> **Any transaminase elevation with a raised INR or encephalopathy = acute liver failure** — this is the combination that matters, not the height of the ALT. It requires immediate senior involvement and discussion with a liver unit. **ALT in the thousands** suggests ischaemic hepatitis, viral hepatitis or **paracetamol toxicity** — take a paracetamol level and a careful ingestion history.
> **Painless obstructive jaundice with weight loss** — pancreatic head or biliary malignancy until proven otherwise.

**Normal:** **Reference intervals are laboratory-specific and are deliberately not stated.** Note that ALP is physiologically raised in **pregnancy (placental), childhood and adolescence (bone growth)**, and that a normal ALT does not exclude cirrhosis — enzymes often normalise as the liver fails.

**Abnormal:** Classify by pattern, then work the differential. **Hepatocellular:** viral hepatitis, drugs and toxins, alcohol, MASLD/NAFLD, autoimmune hepatitis, ischaemia, haemochromatosis, Wilson disease, α1-antitrypsin deficiency, coeliac disease, thyroid disease, muscle injury (check CK — **AST rises with muscle**). **Cholestatic:** gallstones, biliary or pancreatic malignancy, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, drugs, infiltrative disease. **Isolated raised bilirubin with otherwise normal LFTs and no haemolysis** — Gilbert syndrome.

**Alt:** Albumin and INR (function); ultrasound (first-line imaging); viral and autoimmune serology; iron studies, caeruloplasmin, α1-antitrypsin; FibroScan; biopsy.

## 0.2 Liver Auto-antibodies (AMA, SMA, ANA, anti-LKM)

**D:** Autoantibody panel for autoimmune liver disease.

**Ind:** Unexplained hepatitic or cholestatic LFT derangement after viral, alcohol, drug and metabolic causes are addressed; unexplained cirrhosis; cholestasis with a normal biliary tree on imaging.

**Role:** Supportive; diagnosis is a composite of serology, immunoglobulins, imaging and biopsy.

> [!info] **Antibody–disease associations** (source-supported)
> - **AMA (anti-mitochondrial)** → **primary biliary cholangitis**, present in **more than 90%** of patients; the serological hallmark.
> - **ANA + SMA (smooth muscle)** → **autoimmune hepatitis type 1** (the adult form).
> - **Anti-LKM1 (liver–kidney microsomal) ± anti-LC1** → **autoimmune hepatitis type 2**, characteristically **paediatric and more aggressive**.
> - Raised **IgG** supports autoimmune hepatitis; raised **IgM** supports PBC.

**Safety/cost:** Venepuncture. Panels are expensive; order against a question.

> [!warning] AMA is also found in other autoimmune disease **without** liver involvement — sources report it commonly in SLE even with normal liver enzymes. **A positive AMA with normal LFTs is not a diagnosis of PBC.**

**Normal:** Negative does not exclude autoimmune hepatitis — seronegative AIH exists and biopsy is then decisive.

**Abnormal:** Refer to hepatology. Note the association of PBC and AIH with **other autoimmune disease**, and screen accordingly.

**Alt:** Immunoglobulins; **liver biopsy** (see 0.4); MRCP for PSC (see 0.20); coeliac serology.

## 0.3 Anti-LKM Antibody (Anti-Liver Kidney Microsomal Antibody)

**D:** Antibody against liver–kidney microsomal antigen (cytochrome P450 2D6).

**Ind:** Suspected autoimmune hepatitis, **especially in a child or adolescent**, and where ANA/SMA are negative but autoimmune hepatitis remains likely.

**Role:** Defines **type 2 autoimmune hepatitis**. Ordering it matters because a child with seronegative type 1 markers may be anti-LKM1 positive, and missing that delays immunosuppression in the more aggressive phenotype.

**Safety/cost:** Venepuncture; usually part of a liver autoantibody panel.

**Normal:** Negative.

**Abnormal:** Positive supports AIH type 2 → hepatology referral, immunoglobulins, and biopsy. **Anti-LKM can also be seen in chronic hepatitis C**, so exclude that before attributing it to autoimmune disease.

**Alt:** As 0.2.

## 0.4 Liver Biopsy

**D:** Percutaneous (usually ultrasound-guided), transjugular, or laparoscopic sampling of liver tissue for histology.

**Ind:** Diagnostic uncertainty after non-invasive work-up; staging fibrosis where it changes management; suspected autoimmune hepatitis, infiltrative or storage disease; abnormal LFTs of unknown cause; graft assessment post-transplant; characterising a focal lesion when imaging is inconclusive.

**Role:** **Histological gold standard**, but increasingly reserved — non-invasive fibrosis assessment has displaced it for staging in common conditions.

> [!danger] **Safety and contraindications — this is the highest-risk test in this file**
> **Check platelets and coagulation before requesting.** Relative contraindications include significant coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia, **ascites** (percutaneous route), suspected **hydatid cyst** or vascular lesion, and an uncooperative patient. **Where coagulopathy or ascites is present, the transjugular route is used** — it also allows hepatic venous pressure gradient measurement.
> The important complication is **bleeding**, which can present late; pain and hypotension after biopsy are not to be attributed to the puncture site without assessment. Post-procedure observation is mandatory.

**Normal:** Normal architecture.

**Abnormal:** Reported as **grade** (necroinflammatory activity) and **stage** (fibrosis), plus specific features — interface hepatitis and plasma cells (AIH), granulomatous bile duct destruction (PBC), steatosis with ballooning (steatohepatitis), iron or copper quantification.

**Alt:** **FibroScan/transient elastography** (see 0.5); serum fibrosis panels; MR elastography; imaging.

## 0.5 FibroScan / Transient Elastography

**D:** Ultrasound-based measurement of **liver stiffness** as a surrogate for fibrosis, often with a steatosis measure (CAP) alongside.

**Ind:** Fibrosis staging and monitoring in chronic liver disease — sources list validation in **chronic hepatitis C (including post-transplant recurrence and HIV–HCV coinfection), chronic hepatitis B, chronic cholestatic disease, alcohol-related liver disease and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease**.

**Role:** The **non-invasive alternative to biopsy for staging**, and sources describe it as an excellent tool for **early detection of cirrhosis** and for evaluating portal hypertension, with prognostic value.

**Safety/cost:** Painless, no radiation, rapid, repeatable, outpatient. Requires equipment not universally available.

> [!warning] **Causes of falsely high stiffness** — these produce over-staging and are the practical pitfall: **acute hepatitis with high transaminases, cholestasis/extrahepatic obstruction, hepatic congestion from right heart failure, food intake (fast beforehand), and heavy alcohol use.** Measurement also **fails or is unreliable in obesity, narrow intercostal spaces and ascites** — an XL probe mitigates but does not eliminate this.

**Normal/abnormal:** **Numeric kPa cut-offs are deliberately omitted** — they differ by **aetiology** and by manufacturer, and no single set met the three-source bar. Interpret against the aetiology-specific threshold your service uses, and repeat when the transaminases have settled if the scan was done during a flare.

**Alt:** Liver biopsy; serum fibrosis scores; MR elastography; ultrasound for cirrhosis morphology and portal hypertension.

## 0.6 Hepatitis Panel (Anti-HAV, HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HCV, HCV RNA)

**D:** Serology and nucleic acid testing for viral hepatitis.

**Ind:** Acute or chronic hepatitis; abnormal LFTs; screening in pregnancy, before immunosuppression or chemotherapy, in people who inject drugs, in people from higher-prevalence countries of birth, and before/after vaccination.

**Role:** **Diagnostic and definitive** for viral aetiology.

> [!info] **Hepatitis B — the pattern to be able to read**
> | HBsAg | anti-HBc | anti-HBs | Interpretation |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | + | + (IgM) | − | **Acute infection** |
> | + | + (IgG) | − | **Chronic infection** |
> | − | + | + | **Past infection, immune** |
> | − | − | + | **Vaccinated** |
> | − | + | − | Isolated anti-HBc — window period, past infection with waned anti-HBs, occult infection, or false positive |
>
> **The window period** — sources agree there can be **1–6 months** between loss of HBsAg and appearance of anti-HBs, during which **IgM anti-HBc is the only detectable marker**. **HBeAg** marks viral replication and high infectivity.

> [!info] **Hepatitis C** — screen with **anti-HCV antibody, reflex to HCV RNA if reactive**. Antibody window is approximately **8–11 weeks**; **HCV RNA is detectable at about 1–2 weeks**. **Antibody positive + RNA negative = past or cleared infection** (spontaneous or treated); **antibody positive + RNA positive = current infection**.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> **Hepatitis B status before immunosuppression, chemotherapy or rituximab** — reactivation can be fulminant and is preventable with prophylaxis. Isolated anti-HBc positivity still carries reactivation risk. **Hepatitis B in pregnancy** — perinatal transmission is preventable with immunoglobulin and vaccination at birth.

**Normal:** All negative — susceptible; offer vaccination for A and B where indicated.

**Abnormal:** Stage, assess fibrosis, quantify viral load, check for coinfection (HIV, HDV in HBV), and refer. Hepatitis B and C are **notifiable**.

**Alt:** HBV DNA and HCV genotype for treatment decisions; hepatitis D and E serology in the right context; FibroScan for staging.

## 0.7 Pancreatic & Digestive Enzymes (Amylase, Lipase, Faecal Elastase)

**D:** **Lipase** and **amylase** — serum markers of pancreatic acinar injury. **Faecal elastase-1** — a stool marker of **exocrine pancreatic function**.

**Ind:** Serum enzymes: acute abdominal pain where pancreatitis is possible. Faecal elastase: suspected **exocrine pancreatic insufficiency** — steatorrhoea, weight loss, chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, after pancreatic surgery.

**Role:** **Lipase is preferred to amylase** — more sensitive and specific for pancreatic injury and remains elevated longer, so it still detects late presentations. Diagnosis of acute pancreatitis conventionally requires **two of three**: characteristic pain, enzyme elevation **≥3× the upper limit of normal**, and characteristic imaging.

> [!warning] **The height of the enzyme does not indicate severity** — this is a persistent misconception. Severity is assessed clinically and by organ dysfunction, not by the lipase. Conversely, **enzymes may be normal in chronic pancreatitis with an acute flare**, because there is little acinar tissue left to leak.
> **Raised amylase is not specific to the pancreas** — it also rises with salivary pathology (parotitis), bowel perforation or ischaemia, ectopic pregnancy, diabetic ketoacidosis, renal impairment, and macroamylasaemia.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> Pancreatitis **with hypocalcaemia, hypoxia, oliguria or rising CRP** — severe disease; escalate. **Gallstone pancreatitis with cholangitis** (fever, jaundice, raised bilirubin) needs **urgent ERCP** (see 0.19).

**Normal:** Assay-specific; **absolute reference intervals are not stated here**. A normal faecal elastase makes significant exocrine insufficiency unlikely.

**Abnormal:** Raised lipase → confirm the diagnosis and **find the cause: gallstones and alcohol account for most**; also hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercalcaemia, drugs, post-ERCP, trauma, autoimmune. **Always request an ultrasound to look for gallstones** — the aetiology determines whether cholecystectomy is needed. Low faecal elastase → pancreatic enzyme replacement and a search for the cause. **A numeric elastase cut-off is deliberately omitted** (assay-dependent).

**Alt:** CT abdomen (severity and complications, best after 48–72h); ultrasound (gallstones); MRCP/EUS; fasting glucose and HbA1c (endocrine insufficiency accompanies exocrine).

## 0.8 Gastrin Level (Serum Gastrin)

**D:** Fasting serum gastrin, sometimes with gastric pH.

**Ind:** Suspected **Zollinger–Ellison syndrome** — multiple, refractory, distal or atypically sited peptic ulcers; ulcers with diarrhoea; ulcer disease without *H. pylori* or NSAID exposure; family history of **MEN1**.

**Role:** Screening test for gastrinoma; a specialist-initiated investigation.

> [!danger] **Interpretation is invalid on a PPI, and this is the whole practical point.** PPIs raise gastrin substantially and are the **commonest cause of hypergastrinaemia**. The test requires PPI cessation beforehand — **a decision to be made with the specialist**, because stopping acid suppression in someone with active ulcer disease carries its own risk. **The washout duration is not stated here**; sources differ and it is a specialist decision.
> Gastrin is also raised in **atrophic gastritis/pernicious anaemia** (appropriately, from achlorhydria) and in renal impairment. **A raised gastrin with a high gastric pH is physiological; with a low pH it is pathological.**

**Normal/abnormal:** **No numeric threshold is stated** — did not meet the standard, and interpretation depends on concurrent gastric pH.

**Alt:** Gastric pH; secretin stimulation test (`[CUT]` on the build list); imaging for localisation (CT, MRI, EUS, somatostatin receptor imaging); calcium and PTH to screen for MEN1.

## 0.9 H. Pylori Testing (Urea Breath Test, Stool Antigen, Rapid Urease Test)

## 0.10 H. pylori Urea Breath Test

**D (0.9 and 0.10 together):** Tests for active *H. pylori* infection — **¹³C urea breath test**, **stool antigen**, **rapid urease (CLO) test** on endoscopic biopsy, **histology**, and **serology**.

**Ind:** Dyspepsia in the absence of alarm features (test-and-treat); confirmed peptic ulcer; before long-term NSAID use in some settings; gastric MALT lymphoma; family history of gastric cancer; **confirmation of eradication after treatment**.

**Role:** **Urea breath test and stool antigen are the non-invasive tests of choice for both diagnosis and eradication confirmation.** Rapid urease test and histology are used when the patient is having endoscopy anyway. **Serology is the outlier — it detects exposure, not active infection, cannot confirm eradication, and should only be used where locally validated.**

> [!danger] **The washout rule — get this wrong and the test is worthless**
> Sources agree: **stop PPIs for at least 2 weeks** before testing, and **stop antibiotics and bismuth for at least 2 weeks, preferably 4 weeks** (some guidance specifies no antibiotics for any indication in the preceding 4 weeks). Anything that reduces bacterial density produces a **false negative**, and the consequence is an untreated ulcer or an unnecessary second course of antibiotics.
> **The same rule applies to the post-treatment eradication check**, which must also be **delayed after finishing therapy** — the interval is not stated here as sources vary.

> [!warning] **Alarm features mean endoscopy first, not test-and-treat** — dysphagia, odynophagia, unintentional weight loss, GI bleeding or anaemia, persistent vomiting, a mass, or new dyspepsia over the locally defined age threshold. See [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Peptic Ulcer Disease.

**Normal:** Negative — reliable only if the washout was observed.

**Abnormal:** Positive → eradication therapy, then **confirm eradication**. **Regimens are not stated here** — Australian guidance is egress-blocked and regimens are changing with clarithromycin resistance.

**Alt:** Endoscopy with biopsy (also excludes malignancy); culture and susceptibility testing where eradication has failed twice.

## 0.11 Panendoscopy (Oesophagogastroduodenoscopy — OGD)

**D:** Endoscopic examination of oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, with biopsy and therapeutic capability.

**Ind:** Dyspepsia **with alarm features**; upper GI bleeding (**diagnostic and therapeutic**); dysphagia; suspected coeliac disease (duodenal biopsy); iron deficiency anaemia; surveillance of Barrett's oesophagus and varices; foreign body removal; PEG placement.

**Role:** **Gold standard for upper GI mucosal disease** and simultaneously the **therapeutic** modality — banding, clipping, adrenaline injection, thermal coagulation, dilatation.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> **Haematemesis with haemodynamic compromise** — resuscitate first; endoscopy after stabilisation, generally **within 24 hours**, earlier if unstable or variceal bleeding is suspected. **A patient with reduced consciousness and active haematemesis may need intubation to protect the airway before endoscopy.**

**Safety/cost:** Sedation risks (respiratory depression, aspiration, hypotension) plus perforation and bleeding. **Fasting is required.** Anticoagulation and antiplatelet management is planned in advance. Higher risk in the frail, in significant cardiorespiratory disease and in the unstable patient.

**Normal:** Normal mucosa. **A normal OGD does not exclude coeliac disease unless duodenal biopsies were taken** — the mucosa often looks normal.

**Abnormal:** Describe and biopsy. Ulcers are graded for rebleeding risk; varices are graded and treated. **Gastric ulcers require repeat endoscopy and biopsy to exclude malignancy**; duodenal ulcers generally do not.

**Alt:** Barium swallow/meal where endoscopy is contraindicated or unavailable (see 0.13); CT for staging and for perforation; capsule endoscopy for the small bowel (see 0.17).

## 0.12 Colonoscopy · 0.13 Flexible Sigmoidoscopy · 0.14 Sigmoidoscopy · 0.15 Anoscopy

**D:** Endoscopic examination of the large bowel to differing extents — **anoscopy** (anal canal and distal rectum), **rigid/flexible sigmoidoscopy** (rectum and sigmoid), **colonoscopy** (to the caecum, usually with terminal ileal intubation).

**Ind:** **Colonoscopy** — positive faecal occult blood/FIT screening test, iron deficiency anaemia, rectal bleeding, change in bowel habit, suspected or established IBD, polyp and cancer surveillance, family history-based screening. **Flexible sigmoidoscopy** — distal symptoms, suspected distal colitis, an unprepared or frail patient, follow-up of known distal disease. **Anoscopy** — haemorrhoids, anal fissure, anal canal lesions.

**Role:** **Colonoscopy is the gold standard for mucosal assessment of the colon and is simultaneously therapeutic** (polypectomy). Sigmoidoscopy and anoscopy are limited-extent alternatives — **a normal sigmoidoscopy does not exclude proximal pathology**, which is the error that delays right-sided cancers.

> [!info] **Australian National Bowel Cancer Screening Program.** Screening is by **immunochemical faecal occult blood test (FIT/iFOBT) at home, repeated every 2 years**, currently offered to **eligible Australians aged 45–74** — *the eligible age range has changed over time (earlier iterations invited at 50, 55 and 65), so confirm current eligibility rather than relying on a remembered range.* **A positive FIT requires colonoscopy**; sources note a large minority of positive results historically did not receive follow-up colonoscopy, which is where the programme loses its benefit. See [[19_General_Practice_and_Preventive_Medicine]].

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> A **positive FIT** — it is a colonoscopy indication, not a repeat-test indication. **Iron deficiency anaemia in a man, or in a postmenopausal woman**, requires investigation of both upper and lower GI tracts.

**Safety/cost:** Bowel preparation is the main patient burden and a source of **dehydration and electrolyte disturbance, particularly in older people and in renal impairment**. Procedural risks are **perforation and bleeding** (higher with polypectomy) plus sedation risk. **Relative contraindications: suspected perforation, toxic megacolon, and acute severe colitis** — where the risk of perforation is greatest and limited unprepared sigmoidoscopy is preferred if any endoscopy is needed at all.

**Normal:** Normal mucosa to the documented extent — **read the report for how far the scope reached and whether the preparation was adequate**; a poor prep means missed lesions and an earlier repeat.

**Abnormal:** Polyps (removed and sent for histology, determining the surveillance interval), cancers, inflammation with biopsies for pattern, angiodysplasia, diverticulosis.

**Alt:** **CT colonography** (see 0.16) where colonoscopy is incomplete, contraindicated or declined; capsule colonoscopy; barium enema (largely obsolete — see 0.13 below).

## 0.16 CT Colonography (Virtual Colonoscopy)

**D:** CT of the insufflated, prepared colon with 2D/3D reconstruction.

**Ind:** Incomplete colonoscopy; patient unfit for or declining colonoscopy; frail or high-sedation-risk patients; assessing the colon proximal to an obstructing lesion.

**Role:** Good for **structural lesions**, but **purely diagnostic — no biopsy, no polypectomy**, so a positive result generates a second procedure.

**Safety/cost:** Radiation exposure; still requires bowel preparation and insufflation (discomfort); small perforation risk. Cheaper than colonoscopy but **not cheaper overall if it leads to colonoscopy anyway**.

**Normal/abnormal:** Sensitivity falls for **small polyps and flat lesions**; **extracolonic incidental findings are common** and generate further investigation — a genuine downside to counsel about.

**Alt:** Colonoscopy (diagnostic and therapeutic); barium enema.

## 0.17 Barium Swallow (and Barium Meal) · 0.18 Barium Enema

**D:** Fluoroscopic contrast studies of the upper GI tract and colon respectively.

**Ind:** **Barium swallow** — dysphagia where endoscopy is contraindicated or has been non-diagnostic, suspected motility disorder or pharyngeal pouch, assessment of strictures and post-surgical anatomy; **video-fluoroscopic swallow study** for aspiration assessment in stroke and neurological disease. **Barium enema** — now largely **obsolete**, replaced by colonoscopy and CT colonography.

**Role:** **Complementary, not first-line.** Barium swallow is better than endoscopy at showing **dynamic function and pharyngeal anatomy**; endoscopy is better at mucosa and allows biopsy.

> [!danger] **Barium is contraindicated where perforation or leak is suspected, and where there is a risk of aspiration.** Barium in the mediastinum or peritoneum causes severe inflammation. **Use water-soluble contrast (Gastrografin) instead** — and note that Gastrografin is itself hazardous if aspirated into the lung. If both perforation and aspiration are possible, discuss with radiology.

**Safety/cost:** Radiation; constipation and impaction after barium; the contraindications above.

**Normal/abnormal:** Classical appearances — **"bird's beak"** in achalasia, corkscrew oesophagus in spasm, apple-core lesion in malignancy, pharyngeal pouch.

**Alt:** Endoscopy; **high-resolution manometry** for motility (see 0.21); CT; MRI.

## 0.19 CT Enterography · 0.20 MRI Small Bowel (MR Enterography)

**D:** Cross-sectional imaging of the small bowel after distending oral contrast.

**Ind:** **Crohn's disease** — diagnosis, disease extent, and complications (stricture, fistula, abscess); obscure GI bleeding; small bowel tumours; assessment before capsule endoscopy where stricture is suspected.

**Role:** Sensitive for **transmural and extramural disease** that endoscopy cannot see.

> [!warning] **MR enterography is preferred for repeated assessment in Crohn's disease, and the reason is radiation.** These patients are typically young and need imaging repeatedly over decades. **CT enterography is faster and more available and is appropriate acutely** (suspected abscess or obstruction), but MR should be the default for surveillance.

**Safety/cost:** CT — ionising radiation and iodinated contrast (renal function, allergy). MR — no radiation but slower, claustrophobia, and contraindicated with some implants; both need a large oral contrast volume that patients find difficult.

**Normal/abnormal:** Mural thickening, mucosal hyperenhancement, comb sign, fat stranding, strictures with proximal dilatation, fistulae, abscess.

**Alt:** Capsule endoscopy (mucosal detail, no biopsy — and **contraindicated with stricture**); balloon enteroscopy; ultrasound of the small bowel in experienced hands.

## 0.21 MRCP (Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography)

**D:** Heavily T2-weighted MRI generating a non-invasive map of the biliary and pancreatic ducts.

**Ind:** Suspected **choledocholithiasis** with intermediate probability; unexplained biliary obstruction; suspected **primary sclerosing cholangitis**; pancreatic duct anatomy; assessment before ERCP.

**Role:** **The non-invasive diagnostic equivalent of ERCP**, and it has taken over the diagnostic role — **ERCP is now largely reserved for therapy**, which is the key structural point.

**Safety/cost:** No radiation, no sedation, no pancreatitis risk. Limited by claustrophobia, implants, and the patient's ability to lie still and breath-hold.

**Normal/abnormal:** Filling defects (stones), strictures, ductal dilatation, the **beaded appearance of PSC**.

**Alt:** **EUS** (better for small distal stones and for tissue sampling); **ERCP** where intervention is needed; ultrasound first-line.

> [!note] **`ERCP` is not built here** — the header check found an existing corpus header `## ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography)` in [[03_Gastrointestinal]], so ERCP was **not** on the no-header list. It is referenced from 0.20 and 0.7 rather than duplicated.

## 0.22 EUS (Endoscopic Ultrasound) · 0.23 Endorectal Ultrasound (ERUS)

**D:** Ultrasound transducer on an endoscope (**EUS**) or a rectal probe (**ERUS**), giving high-resolution imaging of the gut wall layers and adjacent structures, with fine-needle aspiration/biopsy capability at EUS.

**Ind:** **EUS** — staging oesophageal, gastric, pancreatic and rectal tumours; characterising pancreatic cystic lesions; sampling pancreatic masses and mediastinal nodes; detecting small **common bile duct stones** and microlithiasis; drainage of pancreatic collections. **ERUS** — local staging (T and N) of rectal cancer and assessment of anal sphincter integrity in faecal incontinence and obstetric injury.

**Role:** **The most accurate modality for local T staging** of luminal tumours, because it resolves individual wall layers; and the route of choice for tissue from the pancreas.

**Safety/cost:** Sedation and endoscopy risks; FNA adds bleeding, infection and (for pancreatic lesions) pancreatitis risk. Operator-dependent and not universally available.

**Normal/abnormal:** Wall-layer penetration determines T stage; adjacent nodes are assessed and sampled.

**Alt:** CT and MRI (better for distant staging — **EUS is local, CT is systemic**, and both are usually needed); PET-CT; MRI rectum for rectal cancer staging.

## 0.24 Double Balloon Enteroscopy (DBE) · 0.25 Wireless Capsule Endoscopy (WCE)

**D:** **DBE** — deep small bowel endoscopy using overtube balloons, antegrade or retrograde. **WCE** — a swallowed camera capsule imaging the small bowel.

**Ind:** **Obscure/occult GI bleeding** after negative upper and lower endoscopy; suspected small bowel Crohn's, tumours, or polyposis syndromes; iron deficiency anaemia with normal OGD and colonoscopy.

**Role:** **Capsule endoscopy is usually the first-line small bowel investigation** — non-invasive with good mucosal views; **DBE is the therapeutic and biopsy-capable follow-up** when the capsule finds a lesion, and can also be used to retrieve a retained capsule.

> [!danger] **Capsule retention is the complication that matters.** **Known or suspected small bowel stricture, significant Crohn's stricturing disease, or prior extensive abdominal surgery** raise the risk, and retention can require surgical or endoscopic retrieval. Where stricture is suspected, cross-sectional enterography (0.18/0.19) or a dissolvable patency capsule is used first. **Capsule endoscopy is also contraindicated in swallowing disorders and in suspected obstruction.**

**Safety/cost:** Capsule — non-invasive but expensive, long read time, no biopsy or therapy. DBE — prolonged procedure under sedation/anaesthesia with perforation and pancreatitis risk.

**Normal/abnormal:** Angiodysplasia, ulceration, tumours, Crohn's lesions. **A negative capsule during a non-bleeding interval does not exclude an intermittent lesion.**

**Alt:** CT/MR enterography; CT angiography or red cell scan during active bleeding; push enteroscopy.

## 0.26 Small Bowel Biopsy

**D:** Duodenal (or jejunal) mucosal biopsy, usually taken at OGD.

**Ind:** **Suspected coeliac disease** (the principal indication); unexplained malabsorption, chronic diarrhoea or weight loss; suspected Whipple disease, giardiasis, lymphoma, amyloid, or tropical sprue.

**Role:** **Histological gold standard for coeliac disease in adults.**

> [!danger] **Two technical requirements that determine whether the test is valid**
> **(1) The patient must still be eating gluten** — histology normalises on a gluten-free diet, and a biopsy taken after dietary exclusion cannot make or exclude the diagnosis.
> **(2) Multiple biopsies including the duodenal bulb** are needed, because coeliac disease is **patchy**; too few samples produce false negatives.

**Safety/cost:** Endoscopy and sedation risks; bleeding.

**Normal:** Normal villous architecture. **Normal histology in a patient eating gluten with positive serology** should prompt review of sampling adequacy before the diagnosis is dismissed.

**Abnormal:** Graded (Marsh) — intraepithelial lymphocytosis, crypt hyperplasia, villous atrophy. **Villous atrophy is not specific to coeliac disease** — also giardiasis, small bowel bacterial overgrowth, common variable immunodeficiency, drugs (olmesartan), tropical sprue and lymphoma.

**Alt:** Coeliac serology (see `NEW_Investigations_Infectious_Diseases.md` 0.18); HLA-DQ2/DQ8 for exclusion; capsule endoscopy.

## 0.27 24-hour pH Monitoring (with or without Impedance) · 0.28 Oesophageal Manometry (High-Resolution Manometry)

**D:** **Ambulatory pH ± impedance** measures acid and non-acid reflux episodes and oesophageal acid exposure over 24 hours. **High-resolution manometry** measures pressure along the oesophagus and at both sphincters.

**Ind:** **pH/impedance** — reflux symptoms refractory to PPI; before anti-reflux surgery to confirm pathological reflux; atypical symptoms (cough, laryngitis, chest pain) attributed to reflux. **Manometry** — dysphagia with a structurally normal oesophagus (**achalasia** and other motility disorders); before anti-reflux surgery to exclude a motility disorder; **and to site the pH probe correctly**, which is why the two are usually done together.

**Role:** Sources describe **ambulatory 24h pH-impedance as the consensus standard for diagnosing GORD**, providing quantitative acid exposure plus detection of non-acid and gas reflux via impedance. Manometry is the **gold standard for oesophageal motility**.

**Safety/cost:** Uncomfortable transnasal catheter for 24 hours; expensive; specialist centres only. **Medication handling matters** — whether PPIs are stopped or continued depends on the question being asked, and the study should be booked with that specified.

**Normal/abnormal:** Reported as acid exposure time and symptom association for pH studies; manometry classifies motility patterns (achalasia subtypes, spasm, ineffective motility). **Numeric normal values are deliberately omitted** — they are equipment- and protocol-specific, and one source explicitly reports differing normative values in specific populations.

**Alt:** Empirical PPI trial; endoscopy (excludes structural disease and oesophagitis but does not measure reflux); barium swallow.

## 0.29 Anorectal Manometry

**D:** Catheter-based measurement of anal sphincter pressures, rectal sensation, and the **rectoanal inhibitory reflex (RAIR)**.

**Ind:** **Faecal incontinence**; chronic constipation with suspected defecatory dysfunction; **suspected Hirschsprung disease**; pre- and post-operative assessment of the sphincter complex.

**Role:** For Hirschsprung disease it is a **screening test** — sources describe **absence of the RAIR** as indicative, reflecting aganglionosis. **Rectal suction biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard**, and manometry does not replace it.

**Safety/cost:** Uncomfortable but low-risk; specialist centres; requires patient cooperation, which limits it in small children.

**Normal:** RAIR present; normal resting (internal sphincter) and squeeze (external sphincter) pressures.

**Abnormal:** **Absent RAIR** → Hirschsprung disease suspected → **rectal biopsy**. Low resting pressure → internal sphincter dysfunction; low squeeze → external sphincter/pudendal problem; paradoxical contraction on straining → dyssynergic defecation, which is treated with biofeedback rather than laxatives.

**Alt:** **Rectal suction biopsy** (definitive for Hirschsprung); endoanal ultrasound/ERUS for structural sphincter defects; defecating proctography.

## 0.30 Diagnostic Paracentesis

**D:** Needle sampling of ascitic fluid for cell count and differential, albumin, protein, culture (**inoculated into blood culture bottles at the bedside**), and cytology where indicated.

**Ind:** **New ascites** — to establish the cause; **any patient with cirrhosis and ascites admitted to hospital, or who deteriorates** — to exclude spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, which is frequently asymptomatic.

**Role:** **First-line and definitive** for characterising ascites and for diagnosing SBP.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore — and do not delay**
> **Ascitic fluid polymorphonuclear count ≥250 cells/mm³ diagnoses spontaneous bacterial peritonitis** (in the absence of a surgically treatable intra-abdominal source) and mandates **immediate empirical antibiotics** — do not wait for culture, which is often negative. Sources agree on this threshold.
> **Any cirrhotic patient admitted with ascites should have a diagnostic tap**, because SBP presents without classical peritonism and delay increases mortality. **Encephalopathy, renal impairment or clinical deterioration in a cirrhotic patient is an indication to tap.**

> [!info] **Serum–ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) = serum albumin − ascitic albumin**
> - **SAAG ≥ 11 g/L (1.1 g/dL) → portal hypertension** — cirrhosis, heart failure, Budd–Chiari, massive liver metastases. Sources report accuracy around **97%**.
> - **SAAG < 11 g/L → non-portal causes** — peritoneal malignancy, tuberculous peritonitis, pancreatic ascites, nephrotic syndrome, serositis.
>
> Ascitic **total protein** adds to this: high protein with a high SAAG points to cardiac ascites rather than cirrhosis.

**Safety/cost:** Bedside, cheap. Complications: bleeding, bowel perforation, infection, persistent leak. **Ultrasound guidance reduces risk and is standard.** Coagulopathy in cirrhosis is **not** an absolute contraindication — routine correction before a diagnostic tap is not required, and delaying the tap is the greater risk.

**Normal/abnormal:** As above. **Cytology has limited sensitivity for malignant ascites on a single sample** — send a large volume and repeat if suspicion is high.

**Alt:** Ultrasound (confirms ascites and guides); CT; laparoscopy with peritoneal biopsy for suspected tuberculous or malignant peritonitis.

## 0.31 Pale Stools

**D:** Not a laboratory test — a **clinical sign** on the build list: acholic (pale, putty-coloured) stools, usually with dark urine, indicating **absent bile pigment reaching the gut**.

**Ind/Role:** Its value is as a **pointer to cholestasis** and, in an infant, as a red flag.

> [!danger] **In a jaundiced infant, pale stools and dark urine mean conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia until proven otherwise, and the diagnosis to exclude is biliary atresia — a time-critical surgical condition where outcome depends on operating early.** Prolonged neonatal jaundice beyond about two weeks requires a **split (conjugated/unconjugated) bilirubin**, not reassurance. See [[15_22b_Paeds_-_Neonatal_Respiratory_Distress_and_Jaundice]].

**Abnormal → next steps:** **Split bilirubin, LFTs, coagulation profile**, and **ultrasound of the biliary tree**; then MRCP/EUS as indicated. In adults, painless obstructive jaundice with pale stools and weight loss suggests pancreatic head or biliary malignancy.

**Alt:** As above; the sign itself is confirmed by inspection, not by a test.

## 0.32 CSF Studies (CSF Analysis, β-transferrin)

> [!warning] **Mis-filed in the source list** — this is a neurology/ENT investigation, listed under Gastroenterology. Built here as listed.

**D:** Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid — opening pressure, cell count and differential, protein, glucose (**with a paired serum glucose**), Gram stain, culture, PCR, cytology, oligoclonal bands, xanthochromia. **β-transferrin (β2-transferrin)** is a protein essentially unique to CSF (and perilymph/vitreous), used to prove that a clear nasal or ear discharge is CSF.

**Ind:** Suspected meningitis or encephalitis; subarachnoid haemorrhage with a non-diagnostic CT; suspected multiple sclerosis or Guillain–Barré syndrome; suspected malignant meningitis; measurement and therapeutic drainage in idiopathic intracranial hypertension. **β-transferrin** — clear rhinorrhoea or otorrhoea after head trauma or skull base surgery.

**Role:** Definitive for CNS infection and for CSF leak.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> **Do not delay antibiotics for a lumbar puncture in suspected bacterial meningitis.** **Consider CT before LP** where there are focal signs, reduced consciousness, seizures, papilloedema or immunosuppression, because of the risk of herniation; and check coagulation and platelets. **Xanthochromia** requires the sample to be taken at an appropriate interval after headache onset and protected from light — see the thunderclap headache entry in `NEW_Neurology.md`.

**Normal/abnormal:** Bacterial meningitis classically shows **high neutrophils, high protein, low CSF:serum glucose ratio**; viral shows lymphocytes with normal glucose; TB and fungal show lymphocytes with very high protein and low glucose. **Specific numeric cell-count and glucose-ratio thresholds are deliberately omitted here** — they did not meet the three-source bar in this build and are age-dependent (neonatal values differ substantially).

**Alt:** Blood cultures and meningococcal PCR; CT/MRI; high-resolution CT of the skull base for a leak.

## 0.33 Coombs / Direct Testing (Direct and Indirect Antiglobulin Test)

> [!warning] **Mis-filed in the source list** — a haematology/transfusion test, listed under Gastroenterology. Built briefly here; the fuller treatment is in `NEW_Investigations_Haematology.md` § *Immunohematology (Blood Group & Rh, Type & Screen, Direct Antiglobulin Test)* — `CORRECTED 2026-08-30: this reference read "0.7 (Immunohaematology)" since the original upload. That file has no section 0.7; the section is unnumbered, and is spelled Immunohematology.`

**D:** **Direct antiglobulin test (DAT)** detects antibody or complement **bound to the patient's red cells**. **Indirect antiglobulin test (IAT)** detects antibody **free in the plasma** — the basis of the antibody screen and crossmatch.

**Ind:** Suspected immune haemolysis; haemolytic transfusion reaction; haemolytic disease of the newborn; drug-induced haemolysis; and — relevantly to this file — **haemolysis complicating liver disease or autoimmune hepatitis**.

**Role:** The test that separates **immune** from **non-immune** haemolysis, which is the branch point in the haemolysis work-up.

**Normal/abnormal:** **Positive DAT** → autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (warm or cold), transfusion reaction, HDN, drug-induced (**including methyldopa**, which produces a positive DAT commonly and overt haemolysis rarely). **Negative DAT does not exclude haemolysis** — it points to non-immune causes: microangiopathy, mechanical, membrane and enzyme defects, infection.

**Alt:** Full haemolysis screen — reticulocytes, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, blood film.

## 0.35 Rubella / Varicella Serology (Rubella IgG, Varicella IgG)

> [!warning] **Mis-filed in the source list** — an infectious diseases/antenatal screening test, listed under Gastroenterology.

**D:** IgG serology establishing **immunity** (not acute infection) to rubella and varicella-zoster virus.

**Ind:** **Antenatal booking screening**; pre-conception counselling; healthcare worker immunity screening; before immunosuppression or transplant; after significant exposure in a pregnant or immunocompromised person.

**Role:** These are **immunity checks**. Acute infection is diagnosed by **IgM, PCR or clinical picture**, not by IgG.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> **A non-immune pregnant woman exposed to varicella** — VZV immunoglobulin is time-critical and maternal varicella pneumonitis is dangerous; **the exposure-to-prophylaxis window is not stated here** (Australian guidance egress-blocked). **Rubella infection in the first trimester** causes congenital rubella syndrome. **Rubella and varicella vaccines are live and are contraindicated in pregnancy** — a non-immune woman is vaccinated **postpartum**, and this is the routine action that gets forgotten.

**Normal/abnormal:** IgG positive → immune. IgG negative → susceptible; vaccinate at the appropriate time and counsel about exposure.

**Alt:** PCR and IgM for acute infection; documented vaccination history (though serology is used where records are unavailable).

## 0.36 Gastrografin (Water-Soluble Contrast Study) — **OUT OF SCOPE, built in error**

> [!warning] **Scope error, flagged rather than deleted.** `Gastrografin` sits on the **Conditions & Diagnoses** build list, not the investigations list, and conditions are explicitly out of scope for this bulk build. It was built before I caught the mis-attribution. **Kept because it is correct and useful, but the chat session handling conditions should treat this entry as already done and not duplicate it.**

**D:** Water-soluble iodinated contrast used for swallow, meal or enema studies, and therapeutically in adhesive small bowel obstruction.

**Ind:** **Suspected perforation or anastomotic leak** — the setting where barium is contraindicated; post-operative assessment of an anastomosis; **adhesive small bowel obstruction**, where it is both diagnostic (contrast reaching the colon predicts resolution without surgery) and therapeutic.

**Role:** The **safe alternative to barium** where a leak is possible.

> [!danger] **Gastrografin is hazardous if aspirated — it is hyperosmolar and causes a severe chemical pneumonitis.** In a patient with an unsafe swallow or reduced consciousness, it is more dangerous than barium in the lung. Where both aspiration and perforation are concerns, discuss with radiology; a low-osmolar non-ionic water-soluble agent is used instead.
> Being hyperosmolar it also draws fluid into the bowel lumen — **caution in dehydration, in neonates and in the frail**, where fluid shifts matter.

**Normal/abnormal:** Leak of contrast outside the lumen confirms perforation or anastomotic leak. In obstruction, contrast reaching the colon within the protocol's time window predicts non-operative resolution.

**Alt:** CT with oral and IV contrast (**more sensitive for leak and shows the wider abdomen**); barium where perforation is genuinely excluded.
