# NEW — Investigations: Endocrine and Metabolic

> [!danger] **Sourcing limitation applying to this whole file.** Australian primary guideline domains are **egress-blocked** (verified 2026-08-30); AMH and Therapeutic Guidelines are subscription-gated. Entries are **snippet-sourced**. Numerics appear only on three-source agreement; assay-dependent values are **omitted with the omission stated in place**.

> [!warning] **Units are the recurring hazard in this category.** Several endocrine thresholds are published in **US mass units** (ng/dL, ng/mL) while Australian laboratories report in **SI units** (pmol/L, mIU/L, mmol/L). **Where a threshold could only be found in non-Australian units, it is stated as such and not converted** — converting it here would present my own arithmetic as a sourced figure.

## 0.1 Thyroid Panel (TSH, Free T4, Free T3, Thyroid Antibodies)

**D:** **TSH** (pituitary response to thyroid hormone — the most sensitive single index of thyroid status), **free T4** and **free T3** (circulating hormone), and antibodies — **TPO**, thyroglobulin, and **TSH receptor antibody (TRAb)**.

**Ind:** Suspected hypo- or hyperthyroidism; goitre or thyroid nodule; atrial fibrillation; unexplained weight change, fatigue, or menstrual disturbance; osteoporosis; infertility and pregnancy; monitoring of thyroid replacement or antithyroid therapy; amiodarone and lithium monitoring.

**Role:** **TSH first, and in most outpatient settings TSH alone is sufficient** — sources describe TSH as outperforming T4 for detecting subtle changes in thyroid function, because the pituitary responds logarithmically to small shifts in free hormone. Free T4 is added when TSH is abnormal, when pituitary disease is suspected, or in pregnancy.

> [!info] **Reading the pattern**
> | TSH | Free T4 | Interpretation |
> |---|---|---|
> | High | Low | **Primary hypothyroidism** |
> | High | Normal | **Subclinical hypothyroidism** |
> | Low | High | **Primary hyperthyroidism** |
> | Low | Normal | **Subclinical hyperthyroidism** (check free T3 — T3 toxicosis) |
> | Low | Low | **Secondary (pituitary/hypothalamic) hypothyroidism**, or non-thyroidal illness |
> | High | High | Rare — TSH-secreting adenoma, thyroid hormone resistance, or assay interference |
>
> **Antibodies:** **TPO antibodies** → autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis, and predict progression from subclinical to overt hypothyroidism. **TRAb** → Graves disease, and is the antibody that matters **in pregnancy**, because it crosses the placenta and can cause fetal or neonatal thyrotoxicosis.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> **Do not order thyroid function tests routinely in acutely unwell inpatients.** **Non-thyroidal illness ("sick euthyroid") syndrome** produces a low T3, sometimes a low T4 and a variably low or normal TSH, which mimics secondary hypothyroidism and leads to inappropriate treatment. **Repeat after recovery** unless thyroid disease is genuinely suspected as the cause of the illness.
> **Thyroid storm and myxoedema coma are clinical diagnoses treated before the results return.**
> **In pregnancy, trimester-specific reference ranges apply** and the usual non-pregnant TSH range does not — hCG has intrinsic TSH-like activity and lowers TSH in the first trimester.

**Normal/abnormal:** **Reference intervals are assay- and laboratory-specific and are not stated here.** Sources note commonly quoted adult TSH ranges of roughly 0.4–4.0–4.5 mIU/L outside pregnancy, and define subclinical hypothyroidism as a mildly raised TSH with a normal free T4 — but **the boundary between "mildly raised" and "treat" is contested and age-dependent**, and no treatment threshold met the three-source bar here.

**Alt:** Thyroid ultrasound (0.2); radioactive iodine uptake scan (0.3); TRAb; thyroglobulin for thyroid cancer follow-up.

## 0.2 Thyroid Ultrasound

**D:** High-resolution ultrasound of the thyroid and cervical nodes, with risk stratification of nodules by a **TIRADS**-type reporting system.

**Ind:** Palpable thyroid nodule or goitre; incidentally detected nodule; cervical lymphadenopathy; assessment before or after thyroid surgery; guidance for fine-needle aspiration.

**Role:** **The imaging test of choice for thyroid structure**, and the basis for deciding which nodules need cytology.

> [!warning] **Ultrasound assesses structure; it says nothing about function.** A nodule found on ultrasound still needs a **TSH** — and this ordering matters, because **a hyperfunctioning ("hot") nodule in a patient with a suppressed TSH does not require FNA**, since it is almost never malignant and the next step is a nuclear medicine scan instead. Sending a hot nodule for cytology is a common and avoidable error.

> [!info] **TIRADS in one sentence:** sonographic features (composition, echogenicity, shape, margin, echogenic foci) generate a risk category, and the **category combined with nodule size** determines whether FNA is indicated — sources note the score particularly influences the decision in the **10–20 mm** range. **The specific size thresholds per category are deliberately not stated** — they differ between the ACR, EU and other TIRADS systems, and quoting one system's numbers as universal would be wrong.
> Sources also record a limitation worth knowing: **TIRADS-based omission of FNA overlooks a proportion of follicular thyroid cancers**, which lack the classic suspicious sonographic features.

**Safety/cost:** No radiation, cheap, repeatable. **Its main harm is overdiagnosis** — incidental thyroid nodules are extremely common and mostly benign, and detecting them can generate a cascade of cytology and surgery for disease that would never have caused harm.

**Normal/abnormal:** Report gives nodule size, features, risk category, and node status. **Rapidly enlarging nodule, hoarseness, fixation, or abnormal nodes** are concerning regardless of the score.

**Alt:** **Fine-needle aspiration cytology** (the diagnostic test, reported on the Bethesda system); nuclear medicine uptake scan for a suppressed TSH; CT/MRI for retrosternal extension and airway compression.

## 0.3 Radioactive Iodine Uptake (RAIU) and Thyroid Scintigraphy

**D:** Administration of radioiodine or technetium with measurement of thyroid uptake and imaging of its distribution.

**Ind:** **Determining the cause of thyrotoxicosis** when it is not clinically obvious — the principal indication; assessing a **"hot" versus "cold" nodule** in a patient with a suppressed TSH; assessing retrosternal or ectopic thyroid tissue.

**Role:** The test that separates the thyrotoxicoses by mechanism, which changes treatment entirely.

> [!info] **The discrimination that makes this test worth doing**
> - **High, diffuse uptake** → **Graves disease**.
> - **Focal uptake with suppression of the rest of the gland** → **toxic adenoma**; **patchy uptake** → **toxic multinodular goitre**.
> - **Low or absent uptake with thyrotoxicosis** → the thyroid is **leaking stored hormone, not making it** — **thyroiditis** (subacute/de Quervain, postpartum, amiodarone-induced type 2), **exogenous thyroid hormone** (including factitious), or **recent iodine load**. These are **not treated with antithyroid drugs or radioiodine**, which is precisely why the distinction matters.

> [!danger] **Contraindications and precautions**
> **Pregnancy and breastfeeding are absolute contraindications** to radioiodine — pregnancy must be excluded before administration. **Recent iodinated contrast** (CT scan) or amiodarone saturates the gland and suppresses uptake for weeks to months, making the scan uninterpretable — **ask about recent contrast before booking**. Breastfeeding must be stopped, and local radiation-safety advice about contact with children applies afterwards.

**Normal/abnormal:** As above. **Note the naming trap: a "cold" nodule is the one that warrants concern** about malignancy, not the hot one.

**Alt:** **TRAb** — often makes the scan unnecessary in clinically obvious Graves disease and is safe in pregnancy; thyroid ultrasound with Doppler (increased vascularity in Graves versus reduced in thyroiditis); ESR/CRP and clinical course in subacute thyroiditis.

## 0.4 Glucose / Diabetes Testing (Random and Fasting Glucose, HbA1c, Oral Glucose Tolerance Test)

**D:** **Plasma glucose** (fasting or random), **HbA1c** (glycated haemoglobin, reflecting average glycaemia over the preceding red cell lifespan), and the **75 g oral glucose tolerance test**.

> [!warning] **The 75 g load is the ADULT and pregnancy load. It is not the paediatric load.** In children the glucose load is calculated **by body weight, to a maximum of 75 g** — per-kg with an absolute cap, which is the correct paediatric form. **75 g given to a small child is a substantial overload.** Confirm the per-kg figure against the local paediatric protocol; it is not stated here because the Australian paediatric sources are egress-blocked in this environment.

**Ind:** Diagnosis and monitoring of diabetes; screening in at-risk groups; gestational diabetes screening; investigation of hypoglycaemia; assessment of any acutely unwell patient (**a capillary glucose is part of the primary survey**).

**Role:** All three are diagnostic tests; **HbA1c has the practical advantage of not requiring fasting**, and Australian guidance accepts it for diagnosis.

> [!info] **The diagnostic threshold that met the bar:** sources including the **Australian Diabetes Society**, the **MJA** and **NDSS** agree on **HbA1c ≥6.5% (≥48 mmol/mol)** as diagnostic of diabetes, with the **existing glucose criteria remaining valid in parallel**. **The fasting and OGTT glucose thresholds are deliberately not stated here** — they did not appear consistently enough across the retrieved sources to meet the three-source rule, and a wrong glucose cut-off is a consequential error. Use the current ADS/RACGP criteria.
> **A diagnosis in an asymptomatic person requires confirmation on a second test** — a single abnormal result is not a diagnosis.

> [!danger] **HbA1c is unreliable — and must not be used for diagnosis — in a defined list of situations, and this is the highest-yield content in the entry.** Sources agree it should not be used to diagnose diabetes in: **childhood, pregnancy, renal failure, haemoglobinopathy trait, anaemia, HIV, any condition with abnormal red cell turnover, or after recent drug treatment likely to affect glycaemia or red cell turnover.**
> **Suspect an unreliable result when:** the HbA1c is discordant with the patient's own glucose readings, the HbA1c is **very high (>15%)**, there is a large unexplained change from previous values, or there is **anaemia with abnormal red cell indices**.
> **Direction of error is predictable:** conditions that **shorten red cell survival** — haemolysis, blood loss, splenomegaly, pregnancy — give a **falsely low** HbA1c and can mask diabetes; conditions that lengthen it, such as iron deficiency and splenectomy, raise it.
> **Where HbA1c is unreliable, use glucose-based criteria** (fasting glucose or OGTT); sources also mention fructosamine or glycated albumin as alternatives.

**Normal/abnormal:** Confirm, then assess complications and cardiovascular risk. **Ketones** should be checked in any newly hyperglycaemic unwell patient — and note **euglycaemic DKA on SGLT2 inhibitors**, where the glucose can be near-normal.

**Alt:** Continuous glucose monitoring for management (not diagnosis); C-peptide and autoantibodies (GAD, IA-2, ZnT8) to distinguish type 1 from type 2 where the phenotype is unclear; fructosamine.

## 0.5 Prolactin

**D:** Serum prolactin, ideally with **macroprolactin assessment** where elevated.

**Ind:** Galactorrhoea; amenorrhoea, oligomenorrhoea or infertility; erectile dysfunction and reduced libido in men; suspected pituitary lesion; visual field defect; before and during antipsychotic therapy where clinically indicated.

**Role:** Screening and monitoring test for hyperprolactinaemia. **See also the Galactorrhoea entry in `NEW_Breast.md`, which covers the clinical differential and is not repeated here.**

> [!danger] **Macroprolactin is the interference that changes management, and it is common.** Sources are consistent: **macroprolactin — a biologically inactive prolactin–IgG complex — reacts in all prolactin immunoassays**, and **5–25% of results reported as hyperprolactinaemia are falsely elevated by it**. It has little or no biological activity in vivo, so it is **clinically irrelevant** — but if unrecognised it leads to unnecessary pituitary MRI, dopamine agonist treatment and misdiagnosis.
> **Best practice per sources: all sera with a raised total prolactin should be sub-fractionated (polyethylene glycol precipitation) to report the bioactive monomeric prolactin.** If your laboratory does not do this reflexively, **ask for it explicitly before acting on a raised result.**

> [!warning] **Other causes of a spuriously or transiently raised prolactin** — venepuncture stress, **breast examination or nipple stimulation before the sample**, exercise, sleep, seizure, pregnancy and lactation. **Repeat a mildly raised result under resting conditions before investigating.** And always consider the **hook effect**: a very large prolactinoma can saturate the assay and produce a **falsely low or normal** result, so if imaging shows a macroadenoma and the prolactin looks unimpressive, ask the laboratory to dilute the sample.

**Normal/abnormal:** **No numeric threshold is stated** — assay-dependent, and Australian laboratories report in **mIU/L** while much of the literature quotes **ng/mL**. Interpret against the reporting laboratory's range and units.

**Alt:** TSH (hypothyroidism raises prolactin); β-hCG; UEC and LFTs; full drug history (**antipsychotics, metoclopramide, domperidone, SSRIs, tricyclics, cimetidine, opioids, verapamil, oestrogens**); pituitary MRI; formal visual fields.

## 0.6 Renin–Aldosterone (Aldosterone-to-Renin Ratio, Plasma Renin Activity)

**D:** Paired plasma **aldosterone** and **renin** (activity or direct concentration), reported as the **aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR)**.

**Ind:** Screening for **primary aldosteronism** — hypertension with **hypokalaemia** (spontaneous or diuretic-induced), **resistant hypertension**, hypertension with an adrenal incidentaloma, hypertension of young onset, hypertension with a family history of early stroke or primary aldosteronism, and obstructive sleep apnoea with hypertension.

**Role:** **The screening test**; a positive ARR requires **confirmatory testing** demonstrating renin-independent aldosterone secretion, then **subtype differentiation** (usually adrenal vein sampling) before surgery is considered.

> [!danger] **Interfering medications are the dominant practical problem, and beta-blockers are the trap.** Sources state that evaluation should ideally be performed off interfering drugs, while acknowledging this is often unsafe. The workable position they describe:
> - **The least interfering agents are calcium channel blockers, alpha-blockers, hydralazine and possibly moxonidine** — these are what patients are switched to for testing.
> - **Beta-blockers should be discontinued wherever possible: they commonly cause false-positive results** (by suppressing renin more than aldosterone), and stopping them usually does not critically compromise blood pressure control.
> - **Spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride and high-dose diuretics** cause false negatives and generally must be stopped well in advance.
> - **ACE inhibitors and ARBs raise renin** and so can cause **false negatives** — a positive ARR on an ACE inhibitor or ARB is therefore more, not less, convincing.
>
> **Correct hypokalaemia before sampling** — low potassium suppresses aldosterone and blunts the result.

> [!warning] **A units trap, refused rather than converted.** Sources quote an abnormal ARR as **above 20 when aldosterone is expressed in ng/dL and plasma renin activity in ng/mL/h** — **US units**. Australian laboratories report aldosterone in **pmol/L** and renin as a direct concentration in **mIU/L**, giving entirely different numeric cut-offs. **The Australian threshold is deliberately not stated here**; use the reporting laboratory's own cut-off, which is method-specific.

**Normal/abnormal:** A raised ARR **with an elevated aldosterone** suggests renin-independent aldosterone excess. A raised ratio driven only by a very low renin is less convincing. Refer for confirmatory testing rather than treating on the screen.

**Alt:** Potassium and UEC; confirmatory suppression testing (saline, fludrocortisone or captopril); CT adrenals; **adrenal vein sampling** — the test that determines whether disease is unilateral and therefore surgically curable.

## 0.7 G6PD Assay (Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase Screen)

**D:** Quantitative enzyme activity assay (or flow-cytometric assay) on red cells.

**Ind:** Unexplained **haemolytic anaemia**, especially episodic and triggered; **neonatal jaundice**, particularly prolonged, severe, or in an at-risk ethnic background; **before prescribing an oxidant drug** — most importantly **primaquine, tafenoquine, dapsone, rasburicase, nitrofurantoin, sulfonamides, methylene blue**; family history.

**Role:** Definitive for the enzyme deficiency, but **the timing of the sample determines whether the answer is true**.

> [!danger] **The false-negative trap, and it is the whole entry.** Sources agree that **the assay can be falsely normal during or immediately after acute haemolysis**, because the G6PD-deficient older red cells have already been destroyed and the surviving population is enriched with **reticulocytes, which have several-fold higher G6PD activity**. **Testing at the time of the haemolytic crisis is therefore the worst time to test.**
> **The rule: if the assay is normal but the clinical picture fits, repeat it 2–3 months after the acute event**, once the red cell mass has been repopulated with cells of all ages. Sources are consistent on this interval.
> The same reasoning means **a normal result after recent transfusion is uninterpretable** — you are measuring the donor's cells.

> [!warning] **X-linked inheritance matters for interpretation.** Males are hemizygous and results are usually clear-cut. **Heterozygous females have two red cell populations and can have intermediate or normal-looking enzyme activity while still being clinically susceptible** — sources note the flow-cytometric assay is more reliable than the quantitative assay in women and in anaemic patients for exactly this reason.

**Normal/abnormal:** **Numeric activity thresholds are not stated** — assay-dependent. Interpret with the reticulocyte count and the timing of the episode. A deficient result means **lifelong avoidance advice** — oxidant drugs, **fava beans**, naphthalene (mothballs) — and family testing.

**Alt:** Blood film (**bite cells, blister cells, Heinz bodies** on supravital stain); haemolysis screen (see `NEW_Investigations_Haematology.md`); genetic testing.

## 0.8 Carnitine Levels (Free and Total Carnitine)

**D:** Plasma free and total carnitine, usually interpreted with an **acylcarnitine profile** by tandem mass spectrometry.

**Ind:** Suspected **fatty acid oxidation disorder** or organic acidaemia — hypoketotic hypoglycaemia, unexplained encephalopathy, cardiomyopathy, rhabdomyolysis, sudden unexplained infant death or a sibling with one; abnormal **newborn screening**; long-term valproate or certain other therapy; and secondary carnitine deficiency in renal replacement therapy or severe malnutrition.

**Role:** Part of a **metabolic panel**, not a standalone test. **Interpretation is a metabolic-specialist task** and results are read alongside urine organic acids, plasma amino acids, glucose, ketones, ammonia, lactate and blood gas.

> [!danger] **The critical point is sample timing, not the assay.** In suspected metabolic decompensation, **the diagnostic samples must be taken during the acute illness — before glucose or other treatment is given** — because metabolites normalise rapidly once the patient is treated. **Treatment is never delayed for the samples**; they are taken alongside resuscitation. A "critical sample" protocol exists in most Australian paediatric units — **know where yours is.**
> **Hypoketotic hypoglycaemia** — hypoglycaemia with inappropriately low ketones — is the pattern that specifically suggests a **fatty acid oxidation disorder** and should trigger the metabolic screen.

> [!warning] **Per CLAUDE.md rule 5, no absolute quantities are stated in this entry.** Reference intervals for carnitine and acylcarnitines are **age-dependent** and differ substantially between neonates, infants and adults; sources note that acylcarnitine profiles differ between preterm and term newborns and that samples are conventionally collected on around the third day of life for newborn screening. **Interpret only against age-appropriate, laboratory-specific ranges, with metabolic specialist input.**

**Normal/abnormal:** Low free carnitine with a raised acyl:free ratio suggests secondary deficiency from accumulating acyl groups. Specific acylcarnitine species point to specific disorders. **A normal profile between episodes does not exclude a disorder.**

**Alt:** Urine organic acids; plasma amino acids (0.9); ammonia, lactate, glucose, ketones and blood gas; genetic testing, which increasingly gives the definitive answer.

## 0.9 Plasma Amino Acid Screen

**D:** Quantitative plasma amino acid profile, usually with urine organic acids and an acylcarnitine profile.

**Ind:** Suspected **inherited metabolic disease** — unexplained neonatal or infant encephalopathy, vomiting, poor feeding, seizures, developmental regression, unexplained metabolic acidosis or hyperammonaemia; abnormal newborn screening; a previously affected sibling; unexplained intellectual disability.

**Role:** Part of the same metabolic panel as 0.8. Sources note that **for an at-risk sibling, urine organic acids, plasma amino acids and acylcarnitine profile are measured immediately in the newborn period in parallel with newborn screening** — rather than waiting for the screening result.

> [!danger] **Hyperammonaemia in a neonate or infant is a metabolic emergency** — urea cycle disorders present this way, and delay causes irreversible neurological injury. **Send ammonia on a free-flowing sample transported immediately on ice**, because a delayed or squeezed sample gives a spuriously high result and a real result gets dismissed as artefact. Stop protein intake, give glucose, and involve the metabolic service immediately.

> [!warning] **Sample conditions change the answer.** Amino acids are ideally taken **fasting or before a feed**, and the **clinical state at the time of sampling must be recorded** — a profile taken when the child is well may be normal in a disorder that only decompensates under catabolic stress.
> **Per CLAUDE.md rule 5, no absolute quantities are given.** Amino acid reference intervals are strongly **age-dependent** and must be read against neonatal, infant or adult ranges as appropriate.

**Normal/abnormal:** Specific patterns point to specific disorders — raised phenylalanine (PKU), raised branched-chain amino acids (maple syrup urine disease), raised glutamine with low citrulline patterns in urea cycle defects. **Interpretation is specialist.**

**Alt:** Newborn screening card (the Australian programme covers a defined panel); urine organic acids; ammonia, lactate, glucose, ketones; genetic and genomic testing.
