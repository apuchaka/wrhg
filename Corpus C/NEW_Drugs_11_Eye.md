# NEW — Drug Classes: Eye (AMH section 11)

> [!warning] **Standalone build, not yet integrated.** No cross-references written into existing corpus files.

> [!danger] **Sourcing limitation applying to this whole file.** The **Australian Medicines Handbook and Therapeutic Guidelines are subscription-gated and egress-blocked** in this environment. Entries are **snippet-sourced**, and **no doses are stated anywhere in this file.**

> [!danger] **The rule that governs the whole section: eye drops are absorbed systemically, and topical does not mean local.**
> Drops drain through the **nasolacrimal duct** to the nasal mucosa and are absorbed **directly into the systemic circulation, bypassing first-pass hepatic metabolism**. A beta-blocker drop can cause bronchospasm and bradycardia; an anticholinergic drop can cause delirium in an older patient. **Teach PUNCTAL OCCLUSION** — sources describe closing the eyes gently for one to two minutes after instillation, or applying light pressure at the inner canthus — which markedly reduces systemic absorption and, incidentally, improves ocular efficacy. **This is a 20-second instruction that prevents real harm and is almost never given.**

> [!info] **Three practical rules for any eye drop.** **One drop is enough** — the conjunctival sac cannot hold more, and the rest runs down the cheek or into the nose. **Separate different drops by at least 5 minutes**, or the second washes out the first. **Apply ointments last**, since they impede the absorption of anything following.

## 0.1 Drugs for Dry Eyes

- **Key agents:** **ocular lubricants (artificial tears)** — carmellose, hypromellose, polyvinyl alcohol, sodium hyaluronate, carbomer gels and paraffin-based ointments; **topical ciclosporin** and **lifitegrast** for inflammatory dry eye; **autologous serum drops** in severe disease; and **punctal plugs** as a non-drug measure.
- **Indications:** **dry eye disease / keratoconjunctivitis sicca** — age-related, evaporative (meibomian gland dysfunction), **Sjögren syndrome**, post-refractive surgery, and **drug-induced**.
- **Practical points that determine success:**
  - **Preservative-free preparations are preferred where drops are used more than about four times daily, or where the ocular surface is already damaged** — **benzalkonium chloride is itself toxic to the corneal epithelium** with chronic use, and the patient's "worsening dry eye" is sometimes the preservative.
  - **Gels and ointments last longer but blur vision** — use ointment at night, drops by day.
  - **Treat the eyelids too:** warm compresses and lid hygiene for **blepharitis and meibomian gland dysfunction**, which cause most evaporative dry eye and are more effectively treated than the tear film itself. **Oral doxycycline** at anti-inflammatory dose helps in meibomian gland disease and ocular rosacea.
- **Review the medication list** — **anticholinergics, antihistamines, tricyclics, antipsychotics, diuretics, beta-blockers, isotretinoin and hormone therapy** all cause or worsen dry eye, and stopping the culprit beats adding a drop.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Severe dry eye is not trivial** — it causes corneal ulceration, scarring and visual loss, and is a common cause of chronic ocular pain and reduced quality of life.
> - **Dry eyes plus a dry mouth is Sjögren syndrome until proven otherwise** — check autoantibodies and consider the systemic associations, including lymphoma risk (see `NEW_Investigations_Rheumatology.md`).
> - **A red, painful eye with reduced vision is not dry eye** — that is keratitis, uveitis or worse.
> - **Exposure keratopathy in the unconscious, sedated or facial-palsy patient** is a preventable cause of corneal damage: **eye care with lubricants and lid taping is a nursing and medical responsibility in ICU and in any patient who cannot close their eyes.**

## 0.2 Drugs for Eye Examinations and Procedures

- **Mydriatics and cycloplegics:** **tropicamide** (short-acting mydriatic, the standard for fundoscopy), **cyclopentolate** (longer, cycloplegic — used for refraction in children and in uveitis to relieve ciliary spasm), **atropine** (very long-acting — used therapeutically in uveitis and amblyopia, not for examination), **phenylephrine** (a sympathomimetic mydriatic without cycloplegia, often combined with tropicamide).
- **Topical anaesthetics:** **oxybuprocaine, proxymetacaine, tetracaine** — for tonometry, foreign body removal, and to allow examination of a photophobic eye (see `NEW_Drugs_02_Anaesthetics.md` 0.1.4).
- **Diagnostic dyes:** **fluorescein** — the essential bedside dye, revealing epithelial defects, abrasions, ulcers and the **dendritic ulcer of herpes simplex keratitis** under blue light, and used in **Seidel's test** for aqueous leak after penetrating injury; **lissamine green** and **rose bengal** for ocular surface staining. **Intravenous fluorescein angiography** is a separate retinal investigation, and **anaphylaxis is a recognised risk** requiring resuscitation facilities.
- **Miotics:** **pilocarpine** — used to reverse mydriasis and in acute angle-closure glaucoma.
- **Intraoperative and periprocedural agents:** viscoelastics, intracameral antibiotics and mydriatics, and **anti-VEGF agents** (see 0.5).

> [!danger] **NEVER give a topical anaesthetic to a patient to take home** — repeated use causes corneal epithelial toxicity, delayed healing, ulceration and permanent visual loss, and it removes the pain that would bring them back. Single clinical use only.

> [!danger] **Dilating an eye: the real risks and the false one.**
> - **Precipitating acute angle-closure glaucoma is uncommon but real** in a predisposed (shallow anterior chamber, hypermetropic, older, Asian) eye. **Warn the patient to return immediately with a painful red eye, haloes, headache or vomiting** — and know that this presentation is a **sight-threatening emergency** needing same-day ophthalmology.
> - **Do not dilate when the pupil is being used for neurological monitoring** — in head injury or reduced conscious state, a pharmacologically fixed dilated pupil destroys the most important clinical sign. **Document clearly on the chart and at the bedside if you dilate such a patient.**
> - **The patient cannot drive** until the mydriasis wears off (hours with tropicamide, up to a day or more with cyclopentolate and much longer with atropine), and should be warned about glare and blurred near vision.
> - **Systemic anticholinergic effects** — flushing, tachycardia, dry mouth and **delirium**, particularly with cyclopentolate and atropine in **children and the elderly**; and **phenylephrine can raise blood pressure**. Per **CLAUDE.md rule 5**, note that **paediatric ophthalmic drop concentrations differ from adult ones** — atropine and cyclopentolate in infants have caused systemic toxicity, and the strength must come from a paediatric source.

## 0.3 Drugs for Eye Infections

- **Bacterial conjunctivitis:** **chloramphenicol** drops and ointment (the Australian first-line and available over the counter), and topical **ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, tobramycin, framycetin**. **Most bacterial conjunctivitis is self-limiting**, and antibiotics shorten it modestly.
- **Bacterial keratitis (corneal ulcer):** **intensive fortified topical antibiotics**, ophthalmologist-directed, after corneal scrapes for microscopy and culture. **This is a sight-threatening emergency, not a conjunctivitis.**
- **Viral:** **aciclovir eye ointment** and **ganciclovir gel** for **herpes simplex keratitis**; systemic antivirals for **herpes zoster ophthalmicus**; adenoviral conjunctivitis is treated supportively and is **highly contagious** (hygiene and work/school exclusion advice matter more than any drug).
- **Chlamydial and gonococcal conjunctivitis:** require **systemic** treatment and STI management, not just drops. **Neonatal gonococcal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum) is an emergency** — it perforates corneas within days.
- **Fungal and acanthamoeba keratitis:** specialist; **acanthamoeba is strongly associated with contact lens use and water exposure**, and is characteristically very painful and slow to diagnose.

> [!danger] **THE CONTACT LENS WEARER WITH A RED PAINFUL EYE IS A MICROBIAL KERATITIS UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE — refer the same day, do not treat as conjunctivitis, and tell them to stop wearing lenses and bring them in.** *Pseudomonas* keratitis in a lens wearer can perforate a cornea in 24–48 hours.

> [!danger] **NEVER prescribe a topical corticosteroid — or a steroid-antibiotic combination — for a red eye without slit-lamp examination.** A steroid applied to a **dendritic herpetic ulcer** converts it into a **geographic ulcer** with risk of perforation and permanent visual loss, and it worsens bacterial, fungal and amoebic keratitis. **Steroid eye drops are an ophthalmologist's prescription.** See `NEW_Drugs_01_Allergy_and_Anaphylaxis.md` 0.2.2 and [[05_Ophthalmology]].

> [!warning] **Distinguish the red eye that needs referral.** **Pain, photophobia, reduced visual acuity, a fixed or irregular pupil, ciliary flush, corneal opacity, or a unilateral red eye** point away from conjunctivitis and towards **keratitis, uveitis, scleritis, endophthalmitis or acute angle-closure glaucoma.** **Itch with bilateral watery discharge and normal vision** is allergic. **Always check and document visual acuity** — it is the single most useful triage measure and is routinely omitted.

## 0.4 Drugs for Glaucoma

> [!info] **All current medical treatment works by lowering intraocular pressure** — either by **reducing aqueous production** (beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, alpha-2 agonists) or by **increasing outflow** (prostaglandin analogues, miotics, rho kinase inhibitors). **Adherence is the central clinical problem:** glaucoma is asymptomatic until vision is lost, drops are inconvenient and irritating, and patients stop. **Ask about adherence and technique at every review — most "treatment failure" is not the drug.**

### 0.4.1 Prostaglandin Analogues (Eye)
- **Key agents:** **latanoprost, travoprost, bimatoprost, tafluprost**.
- **Mechanism:** increase **uveoscleral outflow** of aqueous.
- **Role:** sources describe latanoprost as **often the most effective topical agent at reducing intraocular pressure and now frequently first-line**. **Once daily at night**, which helps adherence, and no systemic cardiorespiratory effects.
- **Adverse effects:** **conjunctival hyperaemia**, burning; **increased IRIS PIGMENTATION — sources note this is likely PERMANENT**, and is cosmetically significant in patients with hazel or mixed-colour irides (and causes visible asymmetry if only one eye is treated); **eyelash lengthening, thickening and darkening — usually reversible** (and the basis of cosmetic lash products); **periorbital fat atrophy and deepening of the upper lid sulcus**; and reactivation of herpes keratitis and macular oedema in predisposed eyes.
- **The safety advantage that matters:** sources note **asthmatic patients are unaffected by latanoprost, with no major pulmonary or cardiac toxicity in long-term studies** — which is precisely why it displaced timolol as first choice.

### 0.4.2 Beta-Blockers (Eye)
- **Key agents:** **timolol** (non-selective), betaxolol (relatively β₁-selective), levobunolol.
- **Mechanism:** sources describe timolol as **reducing intraocular pressure by reducing aqueous humour production** at the ciliary epithelium.

> [!danger] **TOPICAL TIMOLOL IS A SYSTEMIC BETA-BLOCKER, AND PATIENTS AND CLINICIANS BOTH FORGET IT IS A DRUG.**
> Sources state directly that because they are **absorbed systemically**, ocular beta-blockers can **slow the heart rate, lower blood pressure, and trigger bronchospasm in patients with asthma or COPD.** Deaths from bronchospasm have been reported.
> **Practical consequences:** **ask about eye drops when taking a drug history** — patients do not list them; **avoid in asthma, significant COPD, bradycardia, heart block and decompensated heart failure**; be alert to **additive bradycardia with systemic beta-blockers, verapamil and diltiazem**; consider them in unexplained **fatigue, exercise intolerance, depression or bradycardia** in an older patient; and remember they **mask hypoglycaemia awareness** in diabetes. **Teach punctal occlusion.** Betaxolol is relatively safer in airways disease but is not risk-free.

### 0.4.3 Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors
- **Key agents:** **topical dorzolamide and brinzolamide**; **oral acetazolamide** (and methazolamide).
- **Mechanism:** inhibit carbonic anhydrase in the ciliary body → **reduced aqueous production**.
- **Topical adverse effects:** stinging and a **bitter taste** (drainage to the pharynx), transient blurring, and superficial punctate keratitis. **They are sulfonamides** — caution with true sulfonamide hypersensitivity.
- **Oral acetazolamide** is used for **acute intraocular pressure lowering** (acute angle-closure glaucoma) and in **idiopathic intracranial hypertension**, and its systemic effects are substantial: **paraesthesia (nearly universal), metabolic acidosis, hypokalaemia, renal stones, taste disturbance (carbonated drinks taste odd), fatigue, and rarely marrow suppression and Stevens-Johnson syndrome.** **Avoid in significant renal or hepatic impairment, and in sulfonamide allergy.**

### 0.4.4 Other Drugs for Glaucoma
- **Alpha-2 agonists — brimonidine, apraclonidine.** Reduce aqueous production and increase uveoscleral outflow; sources note brimonidine is used in combination drops with timolol. **Adverse effects: ocular allergy and follicular conjunctivitis (common, and often develops after months), dry mouth, fatigue and drowsiness** — and, importantly, **CNS depression and apnoea in INFANTS AND YOUNG CHILDREN, in whom brimonidine is contraindicated.**
- **Miotics — pilocarpine.** A muscarinic agonist increasing trabecular outflow; now largely reserved for **acute angle-closure glaucoma** and after laser iridotomy. Causes **brow ache, a small pupil with dim vision (particularly troublesome with cataract), accommodative spasm and myopic shift**, and a small risk of retinal detachment.
- **Rho kinase inhibitors — netarsudil**; and **fixed-combination drops** (widely used to reduce drop burden and improve adherence).
- **Non-drug treatment:** **selective laser trabeculoplasty** is increasingly used as first-line and avoids the adherence problem entirely; **laser peripheral iridotomy** for angle closure; and **surgery (trabeculectomy, tube shunts, minimally invasive glaucoma surgery)**.

> [!danger] **ACUTE ANGLE-CLOSURE GLAUCOMA IS A SIGHT-THREATENING EMERGENCY.** Severe eye pain, headache, nausea and vomiting (frequently misdiagnosed as a gastrointestinal or neurological problem), blurred vision with **haloes around lights**, a **red eye with a hazy cornea and a fixed mid-dilated pupil**, and a stony-hard globe. **Treat immediately — acetazolamide, topical agents, and urgent ophthalmology for laser iridotomy** — and **review the patient's medications**, because **anticholinergics, tricyclics, antihistamines, some antipsychotics, topiramate, and sympathomimetics can precipitate it.** See [[05_Ophthalmology]].

## 0.5 Drugs for Retinal Disease

### 0.5.1 VEGF Inhibitors
- **Key agents:** **ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab** (used off-label and much more cheaply), **faricimab** (dual VEGF/Ang-2); given by **intravitreal injection**, initially monthly and then on a treat-and-extend basis.
- **Mechanism:** bind **vascular endothelial growth factor**, reducing pathological neovascularisation and vascular permeability.
- **Indications:** **neovascular ("wet") age-related macular degeneration**, **diabetic macular oedema and proliferative diabetic retinopathy**, **retinal vein occlusion with macular oedema**, and myopic choroidal neovascularisation.
- **Impact:** these agents transformed wet AMD from a condition causing rapid irreversible central visual loss into one where **vision is usually stabilised and often improved** — one of the most significant therapeutic advances in ophthalmology. **The corollary is that early referral matters enormously.**

> [!danger] **SUDDEN DISTORTION OR CENTRAL VISUAL LOSS NEEDS URGENT OPHTHALMOLOGY REFERRAL, NOT AN OPTOMETRY APPOINTMENT IN A MONTH.** Wet AMD treated early preserves vision; treated late it does not. **Ask about metamorphopsia (straight lines appearing wavy)** and give at-risk patients an **Amsler grid** for home monitoring.

> [!danger] **The complication of intravitreal injection that an intern must recognise: ENDOPHTHALMITIS.**
> **Increasing pain, redness, reduced vision and photophobia in the days after an intravitreal injection is endophthalmitis until proven otherwise** — it is rare but blinding, and requires **immediate ophthalmology assessment for vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotics**. It must not be dismissed as expected post-injection irritation. Other complications: raised intraocular pressure, subconjunctival haemorrhage (common and harmless), retinal tear or detachment, cataract, and a small systemic arterial thromboembolic signal.
- **Other retinal drugs:** **intravitreal corticosteroid implants** (dexamethasone, fluocinolone) for macular oedema — causing **cataract and raised intraocular pressure**; **verteporfin** photodynamic therapy; **complement inhibitors** for geographic atrophy in dry AMD; **anti-VEGF in retinopathy of prematurity** (an alternative to laser, with specialist neonatal use); and **AREDS-type supplements**, which slow progression in defined intermediate dry AMD — **but the original formulation contained beta-carotene, which increases lung cancer risk in smokers, so the smoker-safe reformulation must be used.**
- **The most effective retinal "drugs" are systemic:** **glycaemic, blood pressure and lipid control, and smoking cessation** — and **annual diabetic retinopathy screening**, which detects treatable disease before symptoms. See [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]].
