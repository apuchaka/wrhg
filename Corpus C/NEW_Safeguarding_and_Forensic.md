> [!danger] **The legal framework in this file is jurisdiction-dependent, and that is itself the most important teaching point.** Mandatory reporting of child abuse and neglect is governed by **each Australian state and territory's own child protection legislation, and those laws are not the same**. They differ in **who must report, which types of abuse and neglect must be reported, the threshold for reporting, and to whom the report is made** — for example, in the **Northern Territory every person** must report suspected abuse or neglect, whereas other jurisdictions mandate specified professional groups including doctors; **NSW, the NT and Tasmania** mandate reporting of all five recognised types of abuse and neglect, while the **ACT** mandates a narrower set. **Doctors are mandated reporters in every Australian jurisdiction.**
> **This file therefore does not state a single reporting rule, threshold or age cut-off**, because there is no national one and stating one would be wrong somewhere. **Find your state or territory's requirements and your hospital's child protection pathway now, and know who your local child protection team is before you need them.**

## Neglect Concern

- **D:** persistent failure to meet a child's basic physical, emotional, medical, educational or supervisory needs. **Neglect is the commonest form of substantiated child maltreatment and the most likely to be missed in a hospital, because there is rarely a single event to point at** — it presents as a pattern accumulated across contacts rather than as an injury.
- **What raises the concern:**
  - **Physical:** faltering growth without organic cause; poor hygiene, untreated nappy rash, dental decay; inadequate or inappropriate clothing; recurrent presentations with preventable conditions; untreated injuries or infestations.
  - **Medical:** repeatedly missed appointments; **non-adherence with treatment for a serious chronic condition** (asthma, diabetes, epilepsy) with resulting admissions; missed immunisations; delay in seeking care for a significant illness or injury.
  - **Supervisory:** injuries or ingestions inconsistent with the child's developmental stage or the level of supervision described; a young child repeatedly presenting after unsupervised access to medications or hazards.
  - **Developmental and emotional:** developmental delay without medical explanation; withdrawn, watchful or indiscriminately affectionate behaviour; poor school attendance.
- **Focused Hx:** **a careful, non-accusatory account of the child's daily life** — who cares for them, feeding, sleeping, schooling; **the family's circumstances, asked with the understanding that poverty is not neglect** but that unmet need requires support either way; **parental factors that increase risk and are themselves treatable — mental illness, substance use, intellectual disability, family violence, social isolation**; the child's medical history including **the pattern of attendances and non-attendances across services, which requires actively looking at the record rather than relying on the history given**; developmental milestones; and **the child's own account, obtained in a developmentally appropriate way, and — where they are old enough — with an opportunity to speak without the carer present.**
- **Examination:** **full examination with the child undressed, in a manner appropriate to their age and with consent** — growth parameters **plotted on a growth chart, with previous measurements retrieved and compared** (the trajectory carries the information, not a single point); hygiene and dentition; skin, including **bruising and its distribution**; signs of untreated conditions; development; and observation of the **interaction between child and carer**, which is a documented finding in its own right.
- **First-line Ix:** driven by the differential rather than by the concern itself — **the medical task is to exclude organic causes of the presenting problem while the safeguarding process runs in parallel, not sequentially.** Typically **FBC, UEC, LFTs, calcium, phosphate, ALP, iron studies, TFTs, coeliac serology, vitamin D**, urinalysis, and growth-related investigations where faltering growth is present. **Document findings factually and contemporaneously, describing what you observed rather than concluding what caused it.**

## Emotional Abuse Concern

- **D:** a persistent pattern of parental or caregiver behaviour that damages a child's emotional development — including rejection, denigration, humiliation, scapegoating, threats, exposure to family violence, and the failure to provide age-appropriate emotional responsiveness. **It is the hardest form to evidence and the most under-reported**, and it very often coexists with the other forms.
- **What raises the concern:** a child who is **fearful, withdrawn, watchful, or excessively compliant**; **age-inappropriate behaviour** — either regression or precocious caretaking of adults or siblings; self-harm; eating or sleeping disturbance; **school refusal or deterioration in performance**; developmental or speech delay without organic cause; and, observed directly, **a carer who consistently belittles, blames or is emotionally unavailable to the child**. **Exposure to family violence is emotional abuse of the child**, whether or not the child is physically harmed — this is the point most often missed, and it makes a disclosure of intimate partner violence by a parent a child safeguarding matter as well.
- **Focused Hx:** the child's functioning across settings — home, school, peers; the **carer's description of the child**, which is itself informative when it is consistently hostile or dismissive; **direct, private enquiry about family violence, using a routine, non-judgemental framing**; parental mental health, substance use and their own experience of childhood adversity; supports available to the family; and **the child's own account, sought separately where developmentally appropriate**.
- **Examination:** **general physical examination to identify coexisting physical abuse or neglect**, growth and development, and **structured observation of interactions**. Record verbatim what a child says rather than paraphrasing it.
- **First-line Ix:** there is **no test for emotional abuse.** Investigations are directed at excluding organic causes of the presenting features — **growth, developmental, hearing and vision assessment**, and mental health assessment — and at documenting the child's developmental status. **The action is referral and multidisciplinary assessment, not investigation.**

## Fabricated Illness Concern

- **D:** a caregiver fabricating, exaggerating or inducing illness in a child — variously described as **factitious disorder imposed on another** or **fabricated or induced illness**. It is uncommon, **potentially lethal**, and the diagnosis is one of the most serious allegations in medicine, so it is made by a multidisciplinary team and never by an individual clinician acting alone.
- **What raises the concern:**
  - **Symptoms and signs that only ever occur in the caregiver's presence** and resolve when the child is separated — the single most discriminating feature.
  - **A history that is inconsistent, biologically implausible, or does not match objective findings**; findings that cannot be reproduced by any clinician.
  - **Repeated presentations across multiple hospitals and specialists**, with escalating investigation and intervention; a caregiver who **seeks or resists investigations in a way that is out of keeping** with the child's apparent state.
  - Unexplained treatment failure; **recurrent apnoea, seizures or bleeding witnessed only by the carer**; unexplained biochemical results (**exogenous insulin, salt, or drug administration**).
  - **The child's own presentation** — accepting of invasive procedures, or presenting differently when the carer is absent.
- **Focused Hx:** **obtain the full record from every service involved** — the pattern across records is the evidence, and it is invisible from a single consultation; establish **who witnessed each reported event**; take **an independent account from the child where age permits, and from other family members and the school**; and record the reported history verbatim, distinguishing clearly between **what was reported and what was directly observed**.
- **Examination:** thorough general examination and documentation; **directly observed and documented episodes** where feasible.
- **First-line Ix:** investigations are directed both at genuinely excluding organic disease — **which must be done properly, because a real disease being missed is the other failure mode here** — and, where indicated and with the child protection team's involvement, at **specific tests such as toxicology, drug levels, insulin and C-peptide, or electrolytes in unexplained metabolic derangement**.
- **The process, which is the intern-level content:** **do not confront the caregiver, and do not act alone.** Discuss with your consultant immediately, involve the hospital child protection team and social work, document meticulously and contemporaneously with the source of every piece of information identified, and **make the mandatory report your jurisdiction requires**. **Covert video surveillance is a legally and ethically governed intervention undertaken only by specialist teams under formal authorisation — never arranged locally.**

## Safe Sleep Concern

- **D:** a concern that an infant's sleeping arrangements carry avoidable risk of **sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI)**, which includes SIDS and fatal sleep accidents.
- **The Australian standard: Red Nose Australia's safe sleeping recommendations.** The elements consistently stated across Red Nose material are:
  - **Sleep the baby on their back** — for every sleep, from birth, with feet at the bottom of the cot.
  - **Keep the head and face uncovered** — no quilts, pillows, cot bumpers, sheepskins, soft toys or other loose items in the sleep space, all of which increase SUDI risk.
  - **Keep the baby smoke-free before and after birth** — smoking in pregnancy and second-hand smoke both raise the risk; never smoke where the baby sleeps.
  - **A safe sleeping environment night and day** — a safe cot, a safe mattress, and safe bedding.
  - **Sleep the baby in their own safe sleep space in the same room as an adult caregiver for at least the first six months** — room sharing, not bed sharing. **Red Nose does not recommend co-sleeping**, on the basis that it can lead to unsafe sleeping practices.
  - **Breastfeeding** is associated with reduced SUDI risk.
- **Higher-risk situations to identify specifically:** sleeping on a sofa or armchair with an adult; bed sharing where an adult has consumed alcohol or sedating drugs, is a smoker, or is extremely fatigued; prematurity and low birth weight; the infant being placed prone or side-lying; overheating and head covering; and **an unplanned sleep surface** — the arrangement that occurs at 3am rather than the one described in clinic.
- **Focused Hx:** **ask concretely and without judgement — "where did the baby sleep last night, and where do they usually sleep?"** rather than asking whether the parents follow safe sleeping; who else shares the sleep surface; what is in the cot; sleep position; **smoking in the household including during pregnancy**; alcohol and drug use by anyone sharing a sleep surface; feeding method; **and the practical constraints the family faces** — because **inability to afford a safe cot is a solvable social problem, not a parenting failure**, and identifying it is what makes the conversation useful.
- **Examination:** growth and general examination of the infant; developmental assessment; and assessment for any other safeguarding concern, since unsafe sleep frequently coexists with other unmet needs.
- **First-line Ix:** **none — this is a preventive and educational intervention, not a diagnostic one.** The clinical actions are to **discuss the recommendations without blame, provide the Red Nose safe sleeping information, address the practical barriers (referral for equipment or social work support), and document the discussion.** Where the concern is that advice has been repeatedly given and the risk persists alongside other concerns, it becomes part of a wider safeguarding assessment.
