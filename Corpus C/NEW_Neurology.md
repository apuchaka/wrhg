## Acute Confusion

- **D:** an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness developing over hours to days — **delirium**. The two features that separate it from dementia are **acute onset** and **impaired attention**, and the fluctuation means a single lucid assessment does not exclude it. **Hypoactive delirium — the quiet, withdrawn, drowsy patient — is commoner than hyperactive delirium and is missed far more often**, which is the single most useful thing an intern can know here.
- **DDx — the causes are systemic far more often than neurological.** A structured mnemonic beats free recall at 3am:
  - **Infection:** urinary tract infection, pneumonia, cellulitis, intra-abdominal sepsis, **meningitis and encephalitis**, COVID and influenza.
  - **Drugs — the commonest single reversible group:** **opioids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics** (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, promethazine), antipsychotics, corticosteroids, digoxin, lithium, levodopa, antiepileptics; **and withdrawal — alcohol, benzodiazepines, nicotine**. Review the chart for what was started, what was stopped, and what was missed.
  - **Metabolic:** **hypoglycaemia** (checked immediately, in everyone), hyponatraemia and hypernatraemia, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, thyroid disease, **hypoxia and hypercapnia**, thiamine deficiency (**Wernicke's encephalopathy**).
  - **Structural/neurological:** stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, **subdural haematoma — particularly in an older person on anticoagulation after a fall that may have seemed trivial**, tumour, **non-convulsive status epilepticus** (consider in unexplained persistent confusion), post-ictal state.
  - **Cardiorespiratory:** myocardial infarction (may present as confusion alone in older people), heart failure, pulmonary embolism, arrhythmia, shock of any cause.
  - **Environmental and iatrogenic:** **urinary retention and constipation** (genuinely common and genuinely reversible), pain, **sleep deprivation and ward environment**, catheters and lines, recent surgery and anaesthesia, dehydration, sensory impairment with missing glasses or hearing aids.
- **Focused Hx:** **a collateral history is the investigation, not an optional extra** — from family, carer, GP or residential care staff. Establish **the patient's baseline cognition and function**, the **time course** of the change, and **fluctuation**. Then: complete medication reconciliation including recent changes, ceased drugs and over-the-counter agents; alcohol and other substance use with the last drink; falls and head injury; fever and localising infective symptoms; bowel and bladder function; pain; sleep; previous delirium episodes; sensory aids.
- **Examination:** **vital signs including temperature, oxygen saturation and capillary glucose** — the last is not optional. Then: conscious level and **a formal attention test** rather than a general impression; full neurological examination looking for focal signs, neck stiffness, papilloedema; **hydration status**; **palpate for a distended bladder and examine for faecal loading — both are frequently the whole answer**; full infective screen including skin, chest, abdomen and lines; signs of chronic liver disease; **asterixis**; signs of alcohol withdrawal; evidence of head injury; medication patches (fentanyl patches are missed on examination routinely).
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose immediately**, then **FBC, CRP, UEC, calcium and magnesium, LFTs, TFTs, glucose**; **urinalysis and urine culture** — with the important caveat that **asymptomatic bacteriuria is very common in older people and a positive urine dipstick alone does not explain delirium**; treating it and stopping the search is a recognised error; **blood cultures if febrile**; **chest X-ray**; **ECG**; **arterial or venous blood gas** where hypoxia or hypercapnia is plausible; **B12, folate and thiamine** where nutrition is poor; **CT brain** where there are focal signs, head injury, anticoagulation, or no cause is found after a systematic search; **lumbar puncture** where meningitis or encephalitis is suspected; **EEG** where non-convulsive status is a genuine consideration.

> [!info] **Use a validated screening tool rather than an impression.** Australian hospitals commonly use a short delirium screen at admission and with any change in behaviour. **The specific tool and its cut-off score are deliberately not stated here** — Australian tools and thresholds could not be confirmed against a primary source under the egress limitation, and quoting a cut-off from memory is precisely the failure mode this project documents. Use whichever screen your hospital mandates, and score it properly rather than eyeballing it.

## Reduced Consciousness

- **D:** impaired arousal, ranging from drowsiness to unresponsiveness. **This is an airway problem until proven otherwise** — the first action is not diagnosis but assessment of whether the patient can protect their airway.
- **DDx:**
  - **Immediately reversible — check these first, in this order:** **hypoglycaemia**; **hypoxia**; **hypercapnia**; **opioid toxicity** (pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression); **benzodiazepine toxicity**; **shock of any cause**.
  - **Structural intracranial:** stroke (particularly brainstem and large hemispheric with mass effect), intracerebral haemorrhage, **subarachnoid haemorrhage**, subdural or extradural haematoma, tumour with oedema, hydrocephalus, abscess.
  - **Metabolic and systemic:** hyponatraemia, hypernatraemia, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, **hepatic encephalopathy**, DKA and HHS, hypothyroidism/myxoedema coma, adrenal crisis, hypothermia and hyperthermia, **Wernicke's encephalopathy**.
  - **Infective:** meningitis, encephalitis, cerebral malaria in a returned traveller, sepsis with encephalopathy.
  - **Toxicological:** alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, tricyclics, antipsychotics, gamma-hydroxybutyrate, carbon monoxide, deliberate self-poisoning with any agent.
  - **Seizure-related:** post-ictal state, **non-convulsive status epilepticus**.
- **Focused Hx:** almost always collateral. **What was the patient doing and how were they found**; time last seen well; **access to medications and to alcohol or other drugs**, including what is in the house; suicide note or empty packets; head injury; recent illness, fever or headache; known epilepsy, diabetes, liver, kidney or cardiac disease; anticoagulation; recent travel; and **the ambulance record and observations en route**, which frequently contain the diagnosis.
- **Examination:**
  - **A–E first.** Airway patency and protection, breathing pattern and rate, oxygenation, circulation, **capillary glucose**, temperature.
  - **Quantify the conscious level with a validated scale and document the components**, not just the total — the breakdown is what shows deterioration.
  - **Pupils: size, symmetry and reaction** — **pinpoint suggests opioids or a pontine lesion; a unilateral fixed dilated pupil suggests uncal herniation and is an emergency**; both are actionable at the bedside.
  - **Focal neurology**, tone, reflexes, plantar responses, and **posturing**.
  - **Neck stiffness** (with cervical spine precautions if trauma is possible), **fundoscopy for papilloedema**, signs of head injury including **scalp haematoma, CSF otorrhoea or rhinorrhoea, Battle's sign and periorbital bruising**.
  - **Full skin survey** — needle marks, **medication patches**, rash, purpura.
  - Signs of chronic liver disease, asterixis, ketones on the breath.
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose and an arterial or venous blood gas immediately** (the gas gives pH, CO₂, lactate, sodium, potassium and haemoglobin within minutes); **FBC, UEC, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, LFTs, coagulation profile, CRP, TFTs, cortisol** where adrenal crisis is possible; **paracetamol and salicylate levels, ethanol, and a targeted drug screen** in any suspected overdose; **blood cultures**; **ECG**; **chest X-ray**; **CT brain — early and with a low threshold**, particularly with focal signs, head injury, anticoagulation or an unexplained picture; **lumbar puncture after CT** where CNS infection is suspected, **without delaying antibiotics**; **carboxyhaemoglobin** where carbon monoxide is possible; **β-hCG**.

> [!danger] **Give thiamine before glucose in any patient at risk of thiamine deficiency** — alcohol dependence, malnutrition, hyperemesis, bariatric surgery. A glucose load in a thiamine-deplete patient can precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy. **The dose and route are deliberately not stated here**, being egress-blocked Australian guideline content; use the local protocol, but know that the *sequence* matters.

## Acute Focal Neurological Deficit

- **D:** sudden loss of a discrete neurological function — weakness, sensory loss, speech disturbance, visual loss, ataxia. **The working diagnosis is stroke until proven otherwise, and the entire assessment is built around time**, because the treatments are time-limited.
- **DDx:**
  - **Vascular:** **ischaemic stroke**, **intracerebral haemorrhage** (clinically indistinguishable from ischaemic stroke at the bedside — **only imaging separates them**, which is why no antithrombotic is given before the scan), **transient ischaemic attack**, subarachnoid haemorrhage, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, arterial dissection (consider in a younger patient, especially with neck pain or recent trauma).
  - **Stroke mimics — around one in five suspected strokes, and worth knowing because they change management:** **hypoglycaemia** (the most important, and excluded in seconds), **seizure with Todd's paresis**, **migraine with aura**, **functional neurological disorder**, sepsis or metabolic derangement unmasking a previous deficit (**recrudescence of an old stroke**), space-occupying lesion, subdural haematoma, Bell's palsy, peripheral nerve lesion, multiple sclerosis relapse, Wernicke's encephalopathy.
- **Focused Hx:** **the time the patient was last known well — established precisely, from a named person, and documented.** This single datum determines eligibility for reperfusion therapy, and "found down on waking" is not the same as onset time. Then: exact deficit and whether it is progressing, improving or fluctuating; **seizure at onset**; headache and its character; neck pain; trauma; **anticoagulants and antiplatelets with the time of the last dose**; recent surgery or bleeding; previous stroke or TIA; atrial fibrillation; hypertension, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidaemia; pregnancy; **baseline function and independence**, which informs the treatment discussion; and **advance care directives** where relevant.
- **Examination:** **capillary glucose first** — it is a stroke mimic that is fully reversible and takes seconds to exclude. Then vital signs including **blood pressure in both arms** and temperature; **a structured stroke severity assessment** covering conscious level, gaze, visual fields, facial and limb power, sensation, language, dysarthria, neglect and ataxia; **swallow screen before anything is given orally, including medications** — a genuinely intern-level responsibility with direct aspiration consequences; cardiovascular examination for **atrial fibrillation, murmurs and carotid bruits**; fundoscopy; and signs of trauma.
- **First-line Ix:** **immediate non-contrast CT brain** — the single most urgent investigation, to distinguish haemorrhage from ischaemia; **CT angiography** (and perfusion imaging where the local pathway uses it) to identify large vessel occlusion; **capillary glucose**; **FBC, UEC, coagulation profile including INR, LFTs, glucose, lipids, HbA1c**; **ECG** for atrial fibrillation, with prolonged monitoring if the initial trace is sinus; **troponin**; **β-hCG**; **echocardiography** and **carotid imaging** as part of the aetiological work-up.

> [!danger] **The time windows, stated because they drive the whole assessment.** **Intravenous thrombolysis is recommended within 4.5 hours of symptom onset** (Australian Stroke Foundation guidelines and international guidance agree on this figure). **Endovascular thrombectomy** is available for selected patients with large vessel occlusion, and **advanced imaging has extended selection out to 24 hours in appropriately selected cases** — selection is by imaging criteria and specialist assessment, not by the clock alone.
> **Specific thrombolytic agents, doses, and blood pressure thresholds for treatment are deliberately omitted** — they are protocol-specific, they differ between Australian services, and the Stroke Foundation guideline was egress-blocked. The intern-level actions are: **recognise, protect the airway and swallow, check the glucose, establish the time last known well, escalate immediately, and do not give aspirin or any antithrombotic before the CT.**

## Thunderclap Headache

- **D:** a headache of **explosive onset reaching maximum intensity within seconds to about a minute**. The defining feature is **the speed of onset, not the severity** — a patient describing "the worst headache of my life" that built over an hour is not a thunderclap headache, and one that peaked instantly but is now moderate still is. This distinction is the entry's main teaching point, because it is what determines whether the SAH pathway is triggered.
- **DDx:**
  - **Subarachnoid haemorrhage — the diagnosis the presentation exists to exclude**, and around one in ten to one in eight thunderclap headaches. Aneurysmal in most cases.
  - **Other vascular:** **cervical or intracranial arterial dissection**, **cerebral venous sinus thrombosis** (consider particularly in pregnancy, postpartum, on the oral contraceptive pill, or with thrombophilia), **reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS)** — recurrent thunderclap headaches, often triggered by exertion, sexual activity or vasoactive drugs, **posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES)**, pituitary apoplexy, hypertensive emergency, intracerebral haemorrhage.
  - **Non-vascular:** **bacterial meningitis** (can present abruptly), **spontaneous intracranial hypotension** (a headache that is much worse upright and relieved lying flat — a positional headache with a thunderclap onset), third ventricle colloid cyst, acute obstructive hydrocephalus, **acute angle-closure glaucoma** (headache with a red painful eye, blurred vision and haloes — an ophthalmic emergency that presents to medical teams), sinusitis with acute obstruction, cough or exertional headache (a diagnosis of exclusion), **primary thunderclap headache** — also strictly a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Focused Hx:** **time to peak intensity, asked explicitly and in seconds** ("how long from the first twinge to the worst it got?"); what the patient was doing at onset (**exertion, straining, sexual activity, coughing**); **previous identical episodes** (RCVS is recurrent; a first SAH is not); associated **neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, seizure, transient or persistent loss of consciousness, focal deficit, visual change or diplopia**; **positional variation — worse upright suggests intracranial hypotension**; fever; pregnancy and postpartum status; hormonal contraception; **vasoactive drugs — cocaine, amphetamines, cannabis, decongestants, triptans, SSRIs** (RCVS); anticoagulation; **family history of subarachnoid haemorrhage or polycystic kidney disease**; smoking, hypertension and alcohol; recent head or neck trauma or manipulation; previous headache pattern and how this one differs.
- **Examination:** **vital signs including blood pressure and temperature**; **conscious level, documented formally**; **neck stiffness and Kernig's/Brudzinski's signs**; **full cranial nerve examination — a third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil suggests a posterior communicating artery aneurysm and is a critical finding**; **fundoscopy for papilloedema and subhyaloid/retinal haemorrhage**; **pupils, visual acuity and assessment for a red, hard, painful eye** (angle-closure glaucoma); focal neurological signs including limb power, sensation, coordination and plantar responses; signs of trauma; **assessment of whether the headache changes with posture**.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **Urgent non-contrast CT brain** — first-line, and **its sensitivity for subarachnoid blood is highest immediately after onset and falls progressively with time from onset**, which is why the delay from headache onset to scan must be recorded and communicated. *(A specific sensitivity percentage for the early-scan window is deliberately omitted: the figure was not confirmable to three independent sources under the egress limitation, and it is a number that drives a decision to withhold a lumbar puncture — precisely the kind of figure that must not be reproduced from memory.)*
  - **Lumbar puncture if the CT is non-diagnostic and suspicion persists** — for **red cell count in sequential tubes and, critically, xanthochromia**, which remains the reference standard for SAH with a negative CT. **Xanthochromia requires time to develop, so the LP is conventionally delayed to at least around 12 hours from headache onset**; check opening pressure at the same time, since a low pressure supports intracranial hypotension and a high one supports venous sinus thrombosis or hydrocephalus.
  - **CT or MR angiography** where dissection, RCVS or venous sinus thrombosis is suspected — **CT venography specifically for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis**, which a plain CT will not exclude.
  - **FBC, UEC, LFTs, CRP, coagulation profile, glucose, β-hCG**; **blood cultures and empirical antibiotics without delay** if meningitis is suspected.
  - **Intraocular pressure measurement** if angle-closure glaucoma is possible.

> [!warning] **A normal CT does not exclude subarachnoid haemorrhage**, and this is the error that causes preventable death in this presentation. If the history is genuinely thunderclap, the pathway continues — lumbar puncture or angiography per local protocol — regardless of a reassuring scan, and discharging on the strength of a normal CT alone is not safe practice.

## Acute Vertigo

- **D:** an acute, persistent sensation of spinning or self-motion. **The clinical question is not "is this vertigo?" but "is this a stroke?"** — around one in five to one in four cases of acute vestibular syndrome presenting to hospital have a central cause, and posterior circulation stroke is the one that is missed.
- **DDx:**
  - **Peripheral:** **vestibular neuritis** (acute, sustained, no hearing loss), **labyrinthitis** (as above **with** hearing loss), **BPPV** (brief, positional, seconds — **not** a sustained acute vestibular syndrome), **Ménière's disease** (episodic vertigo with fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness), vestibular migraine, herpes zoster oticus (**Ramsay Hunt** — vertigo, facial palsy, vesicles in the ear canal), perilymph fistula, acoustic neuroma (usually gradual).
  - **Central — the group to exclude:** **posterior circulation ischaemic stroke or TIA** (cerebellar or brainstem), **cerebellar haemorrhage** (which can compress the brainstem and needs urgent decompression), **vertebral artery dissection** (younger patient, neck pain, recent trauma or neck manipulation), multiple sclerosis, posterior fossa tumour, **Wernicke's encephalopathy** (vertigo, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, confusion).
  - **Systemic/other:** medication (aminoglycosides, anticonvulsants, alcohol), orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmia, anaemia, hypoglycaemia, anxiety and hyperventilation.
- **Focused Hx:** **timing and triggers are more discriminating than the description of the sensation** — ask **how long each episode lasts** (seconds → BPPV; minutes to hours → Ménière's, TIA, migraine; days and continuous → vestibular neuritis or stroke) and **what brings it on** (rolling over in bed → BPPV; spontaneous and continuous → acute vestibular syndrome). Then: **hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness or discharge**; **headache and neck pain** (dissection); **any other neurological symptom — diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, facial or limb weakness or numbness, ataxia** — asked individually, because patients volunteer "dizziness" and not the brainstem signs sitting alongside it; **inability to walk or stand unaided**; vascular risk factors and AF; recent viral illness; recent head or neck trauma or chiropractic manipulation; medications; migraine history.
- **Examination:**
  - **Vital signs including postural blood pressure and a capillary glucose.**
  - **Full neurological examination**, with particular attention to **cranial nerves, cerebellar signs (dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesis, intention tremor), and gait**.
  - **The single most useful bedside test is whether the patient can stand and walk unaided.** **Severe truncal ataxia — being unable to sit or stand without support — points strongly to a central cause**, because a peripheral vestibular lesion usually leaves the patient able to walk, however unsteadily.
  - **Nystagmus: characterise it.** **Unidirectional, horizontal, suppressed by visual fixation → peripheral. Direction-changing on eccentric gaze, purely vertical, or torsional → central.**
  - **The HINTS examination** (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) in a patient with **acute, persistent vertigo, nystagmus and an otherwise normal neurological examination** — its published performance is high, and sources agree that in expert hands it is **more sensitive for stroke than early MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging in the first 24–48 hours**. Findings suggesting a **central** cause are: a **normal** head impulse test, **direction-changing** nystagmus, or **skew deviation** on cross-cover testing.
  - **Otoscopy** — for vesicles, cholesteatoma, effusion; **Dix–Hallpike** where the history is positional and brief; **hearing assessment** including Rinne and Weber.
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose**, **ECG**, **FBC, UEC, glucose**; **CT brain** where haemorrhage is suspected — noting that **plain CT is poor at excluding posterior fossa infarction and a normal scan is not reassuring**; **MRI with DWI** as the imaging of choice for suspected posterior circulation stroke, with the caveat above that it can be falsely negative early; **CT or MR angiography** for suspected dissection or vertebrobasilar disease; **audiometry** where hearing loss is present; and referral along the stroke pathway when central features are found.

> [!warning] **HINTS is a rule-in/rule-out tool with strict conditions of use, and using it outside them is unsafe.** It applies **only** to a patient with **continuous, ongoing vertigo with nystagmus** — not to BPPV, not to an episodic history, and **not to a patient whose symptoms have resolved**. Applied to the wrong patient it gives false reassurance. Its reported accuracy also depends heavily on examiner training; if you are not confident performing it, the correct action is to image and escalate rather than to record a reassuring HINTS.

## Acute Facial Weakness

- **D:** new weakness of the facial muscles. **The whole assessment turns on one distinction: upper motor neurone or lower motor neurone** — because one is a stroke and the other is not.
- **The distinction, and its limits:**
  - **Lower motor neurone (facial nerve) lesion** — **the whole side of the face is affected, including the forehead**; the patient cannot raise the eyebrow or close the eye on that side.
  - **Upper motor neurone lesion** — **forehead sparing**, because the frontalis receives bilateral cortical innervation; the eyebrow still raises and the eye still closes.
  - **The important caveats, both documented in the sources:** **brainstem (pontine) stroke can produce a full LMN-pattern palsy** and so mimic Bell's palsy — but it will almost always come with other findings (limb weakness, gaze palsy, ataxia, crossed sensory signs); and **some cortical strokes do involve the upper face**, so forehead involvement does not entirely exclude a central lesion. **The rule is a strong guide, not an absolute.**
- **DDx:**
  - **LMN:** **Bell's palsy** (idiopathic — a diagnosis of exclusion), **Ramsay Hunt syndrome** (herpes zoster oticus — vertigo, severe ear pain, vesicles in the canal or on the pinna, often more severe and with worse recovery, and it changes treatment), otitis media and cholesteatoma, parotid tumour or surgery, trauma and temporal bone fracture, Lyme disease, **bilateral facial palsy** — which is *not* Bell's palsy and demands a search for **Guillain–Barré syndrome, sarcoidosis, Lyme disease or HIV**.
  - **UMN:** **ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke**, tumour, demyelination, abscess.
  - **Neuromuscular:** myasthenia gravis (fatigable, often bilateral, with ptosis and diplopia).
- **Focused Hx:** speed of onset (**Bell's palsy typically evolves over hours to about three days; instantaneous onset with other deficits suggests stroke**); **other neurological symptoms — limb weakness, speech, swallow, diplopia, vertigo**; **ear pain, rash, hearing change** (Ramsay Hunt); **taste disturbance, hyperacusis and reduced tearing** (localising the lesion along the facial nerve); recent viral illness; trauma; tick exposure and travel (Lyme); **inability to close the eye and any eye pain or redness** — the complication that causes permanent harm; previous episodes; diabetes, pregnancy and immunosuppression; vascular risk factors.
- **Examination:** **test the forehead first and explicitly** — ask the patient to raise their eyebrows and to screw the eyes shut against resistance, then to smile, puff out the cheeks and show the teeth; **grade the severity and document it**; check for **Bell's phenomenon** (the eye rolling up on attempted closure). Then: **full cranial nerve and limb neurological examination** to find the accompanying deficits that make this a stroke; **otoscopy and inspection of the pinna, canal and palate for vesicles**; **parotid palpation**; **hearing assessment**; **corneal assessment — is the eye red, is closure incomplete, is there a corneal abrasion**; blood pressure; and, in a child, examination for hypertension and for tick attachment.
- **First-line Ix:** **Bell's palsy is a clinical diagnosis and needs no imaging in a typical presentation.** Investigate when the picture is atypical, bilateral, progressive beyond a few days, recurrent, or accompanied by other neurological signs: **glucose/HbA1c**, **FBC, UEC, CRP**, **serology for Lyme disease** where exposure is plausible, **HIV testing**, **ACE and chest imaging** where sarcoidosis is considered, **MRI brain and internal auditory meatus** for suspected central or compressive lesions, and **CT brain** urgently on the stroke pathway if the pattern is UMN or other deficits are present.

> [!danger] **The complication that causes lasting damage is the eye, not the face.** An incompletely closing eye loses corneal protection, and **exposure keratopathy can cause permanent visual loss**. Eye care — lubricants, and taping or padding the eye closed at night — must be arranged at the first consultation and explained to the patient, and is an intern-level responsibility that is regularly forgotten. **Specific corticosteroid and antiviral regimens are deliberately omitted**: timing and agent differ between guidelines, evidence differs between Bell's palsy and Ramsay Hunt, and the Australian sources are egress-blocked. What matters at intern level is that **treatment is time-sensitive and started early**, so escalate rather than defer.

## Acute Neuromuscular Weakness

- **D:** rapidly developing weakness arising from nerve, neuromuscular junction or muscle rather than from brain or cord. **The reason this is a Tier 2 emergency is respiratory failure: these patients die of ventilatory failure while their oxygen saturation is still normal.**
- **DDx:**
  - **Peripheral nerve:** **Guillain–Barré syndrome** — typically **ascending, symmetrical weakness with areflexia**, often with distal paraesthesiae, commonly about **1–2 weeks after a gastrointestinal or respiratory infection**, progressing to nadir within about two weeks; variants include Miller Fisher (ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia) and forms with prominent bulbar or facial involvement; **acute intermittent porphyria**; vasculitic neuropathy; diphtheria; toxic neuropathies.
  - **Neuromuscular junction:** **myasthenic crisis** (fatigable weakness, ptosis, diplopia, bulbar symptoms, worsening through the day), **botulism** (**descending** paralysis with pupillary involvement, dry mouth — the pattern is the discriminator), organophosphate poisoning, tick paralysis (relevant in Australia — **search the scalp and skin for a tick, because removal is the treatment**), drug-induced (aminoglycosides, magnesium).
  - **Muscle:** inflammatory myopathies, rhabdomyolysis, **hypokalaemic or hyperkalaemic periodic paralysis**, critical illness myopathy, thyroid disease.
  - **Metabolic and other mimics:** **hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia, hypermagnesaemia**, hypoglycaemia, **spinal cord compression or transverse myelitis** (look for a **sensory level and sphincter involvement** — the finding that moves the lesion to the cord and changes the imaging entirely), functional neurological disorder.
- **Focused Hx:** **onset, speed of progression and direction — ascending versus descending**; symmetry; **preceding diarrhoeal or respiratory illness, and how long before**; sensory symptoms; **bulbar symptoms: swallow, voice change, nasal regurgitation, choking on food** — the questions that predict aspiration; **breathlessness, orthopnoea and disturbed sleep**, which are early markers of diaphragmatic weakness; back pain and bladder or bowel dysfunction; fatigability and diurnal variation; known myasthenia and recent medication changes; toxin, tick and food exposures (**home-preserved food for botulism**); drugs; family history of periodic paralysis; recent vaccination or surgery.
- **Examination:**
  - **Respiratory function first.** Count how long the patient can count aloud on one breath, look for **paradoxical abdominal movement, accessory muscle use, orthopnoea and inability to lie flat**, and check the **cough and swallow**.
  - **Full neurological examination**: pattern and distribution of weakness (proximal, distal, ascending, descending), **tone, reflexes — areflexia supports GBS — plantar responses, sensation with a careful search for a sensory level**, coordination and gait.
  - **Cranial nerves**, specifically **ptosis, extraocular movements, facial power, palatal elevation and tongue** — and test for **fatigability** by sustained upgaze.
  - **Autonomic assessment** — GBS causes labile blood pressure and arrhythmia, and **autonomic instability is a major cause of death in GBS**, so cardiac monitoring is not optional.
  - **Skin and scalp search for a tick**; look for rash and signs of vasculitis.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **Serial forced vital capacity — the key monitoring investigation, and it must be serial, not once.** **Oxygen saturation and blood gases are late and falsely reassuring**; a patient can be approaching ventilatory failure with normal saturations. *(Commonly cited numeric thresholds for intubation exist — the so-called "20/30/40" rule using FVC and inspiratory and expiratory pressures — but **the figures are deliberately not stated here**: they did not meet the three-source standard, and the sources that discuss them note the measurements correlate with but do not specifically predict the need for ventilation. Measure serially, trend it, and escalate on the trend.)*
  - **UEC including potassium, magnesium, phosphate, calcium, glucose, CK, TFTs, LFTs, FBC, CRP**.
  - **Lumbar puncture** for suspected GBS — the classic finding is **albuminocytological dissociation: raised CSF protein with a normal cell count**, present in the large majority within the first week, though **it may be normal in the first few days, so a normal early LP does not exclude GBS**. A raised cell count should prompt a search for infection or malignancy instead.
  - **Nerve conduction studies and EMG** — confirmatory and subtype-defining, though **normal early studies do not exclude the diagnosis**.
  - **MRI spine** where a sensory level, sphincter involvement or back pain raises cord pathology — **do this early rather than late**, because cord compression is time-critical and is the mimic that must not be missed.
  - **ECG and continuous cardiac monitoring**; anti-ganglioside antibodies, acetylcholine receptor and MuSK antibodies, stool culture and *Campylobacter* serology as directed.

> [!danger] **The two intern-level decisions that matter here:** **(1) monitor the vital capacity serially and escalate on a falling trend, not on oxygen saturation**; **(2) keep the patient nil by mouth until swallow is formally assessed** if there is any bulbar symptom. **Treatment (intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange — equally effective in GBS) is a specialist decision**; specific regimens are not stated here.

## Acute Sensory Disturbance

- **D:** new numbness, altered sensation or paraesthesiae. **The organising question is anatomical: where in the nervous system does this pattern place the lesion?** — because the answer determines the urgency.
- **DDx by pattern — this is the entry's main content, since the pattern *is* the diagnosis:**
  - **A sensory level on the trunk** → **spinal cord lesion** — compression by tumour, abscess or disc, transverse myelitis, cord infarction, trauma. **This is the emergency in the list**, and it demands urgent MRI of the whole spine and immediate escalation.
  - **Cauda equina pattern — saddle anaesthesia with bladder or bowel involvement** → cauda equina syndrome; a surgical emergency.
  - **Dermatomal, one nerve root** → radiculopathy from disc herniation, foraminal stenosis, or **shingles before the rash appears**.
  - **A single peripheral nerve territory** → mononeuropathy — carpal tunnel (median), ulnar at the elbow, common peroneal at the fibular head, meralgia paraesthetica.
  - **Glove-and-stocking, symmetrical, distal** → **peripheral polyneuropathy** — diabetes (much the commonest), alcohol, **B12 deficiency**, chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, drugs (chemotherapy, isoniazid, metronidazole, amiodarone), paraproteinaemia, vasculitis, HIV.
  - **Hemisensory, one side of the body including the face** → **cortical or thalamic stroke or lesion** — assess and treat on the stroke pathway.
  - **Crossed pattern — face on one side, body on the other** → **brainstem lesion**, which is a highly localising and serious finding.
  - **Perioral and peripheral tingling with carpopedal spasm** → **hypocalcaemia**, or **hyperventilation**; a genuinely common and rapidly reversible cause.
  - **Patchy, non-anatomical, or inconsistent on repeat testing** → consider **functional neurological disorder** — a positive diagnosis made on positive signs, not a diagnosis of exclusion, and one that still requires the organic causes to have been considered.
- **Focused Hx:** **exact distribution, drawn or pointed out by the patient**; onset speed and progression; **associated weakness**; **bladder, bowel and sexual function** — asked explicitly whenever a cord or cauda equina cause is possible; back or neck pain; trauma; **preceding infection**; diabetes and its duration and control; alcohol; nutrition and bariatric surgery (**B12**); medications including chemotherapy; known malignancy (**cord compression from metastases**); HIV risk; family history; occupational and repetitive strain factors; and vascular risk factors where a stroke pattern is suggested.
- **Examination:** map the deficit against **dermatomes and peripheral nerve territories, and determine whether there is a sensory level** — testing from an area of reduced sensation upward toward normal, which is more reliable than the reverse. Test **light touch, pinprick, temperature, vibration and proprioception**, since **dissociated loss localises within the cord** (posterior columns versus spinothalamic). Then **power, tone, reflexes and plantar responses**; **gait and Romberg's test**; **perianal sensation and anal tone where indicated**; **palpation of the spine for tenderness**; **Tinel's and Phalen's tests** for entrapment; **inspection for a vesicular rash**; and a **cardiovascular and diabetic foot assessment** where polyneuropathy is likely.
- **First-line Ix:** **glucose and HbA1c, FBC, UEC, LFTs, calcium and magnesium, TFTs, B12 and folate, CRP and ESR**; **HIV and syphilis serology** where relevant; **serum protein electrophoresis** in an unexplained neuropathy; **MRI of the relevant spinal region urgently** where a sensory level, sphincter disturbance or progressive deficit is present; **CT or MRI brain** for a hemisensory or crossed pattern, on the stroke pathway; **nerve conduction studies** for entrapment or polyneuropathy, arranged non-urgently; **lumbar puncture** where inflammatory or infective cord disease is suspected.

> [!danger] **The pattern that must trigger immediate action is a sensory level with or without weakness, or saddle anaesthesia with sphincter disturbance.** Both indicate cord or cauda equina pathology, both are time-critical, and both are missed when "numbness" is triaged as a minor complaint. **Ask about bladder and bowel function in every patient presenting with sensory disturbance of the legs or trunk, and record the answer.**
