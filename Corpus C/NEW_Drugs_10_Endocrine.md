## 0.1 Drugs Affecting Bone

### 0.1.1 Bisphosphonates
- **Mechanism:** bind avidly to **hydroxyapatite** at sites of bone turnover and are taken up by **osteoclasts**, inhibiting the mevalonate pathway (nitrogen-containing agents) → osteoclast apoptosis and **reduced bone resorption**.
- **Key agents:** oral **alendronate, risedronate**; intravenous **zoledronic acid** (yearly) and pamidronate.
- **Indications:** **osteoporosis** (postmenopausal, male, and glucocorticoid-induced); **Paget disease**; **hypercalcaemia of malignancy**; **skeletal-related events in bone metastases and myeloma**.
- **Adverse effects:**
  - **Oral: oesophagitis and oesophageal ulceration** — the reason for the strict administration rules below; dyspepsia; and a small increase in atypical fractures with long use.
  - **Intravenous: an acute-phase reaction** after the first infusion — fever, myalgia and flu-like symptoms lasting a day or two, common, benign, and much less likely on subsequent doses. **Warn the patient or they will refuse the second dose.**
  - **Hypocalcaemia** — check and correct **calcium and vitamin D before giving**, especially intravenously.
  - **Renal impairment** — zoledronic acid is contraindicated below a threshold creatinine clearance and requires hydration.
  - **MEDICATION-RELATED OSTEONECROSIS OF THE JAW (MRONJ)** — sources note **higher doses and longer duration increase the risk**. It is **rare at osteoporosis doses and much commoner at oncology doses.** **Arrange a dental review and complete invasive dental work BEFORE starting** where possible, and maintain oral hygiene.
  - **ATYPICAL FEMORAL FRACTURE** — associated with **long-term** use. **New thigh or groin pain in a patient on long-term bisphosphonate or denosumab is an atypical femoral fracture until imaged**, and it is frequently bilateral, so image both femurs.

> [!danger] **ORAL BISPHOSPHONATE ADMINISTRATION RULES — get these wrong and the drug either does not work or ulcerates the oesophagus.**
> **On waking, on an EMPTY stomach, with a FULL GLASS OF PLAIN WATER, and the patient must REMAIN UPRIGHT (sitting or standing, not lying) and take nothing else by mouth for at least 30 minutes.** Absorption is very poor and is **abolished by food, calcium, iron, antacids, coffee and juice.** **A patient who cannot sit upright or follow this should have intravenous therapy instead.**

> [!info] **Drug holidays.** Sources note that although dosing stops, **bisphosphonates have a long half-life in bone and their effect continues for years**, and that **the optimal drug holiday duration is unclear, with expert opinion suggesting two to four years** after an initial treatment period. This is a specialist-informed decision balancing atypical fracture risk against fracture risk, and it applies to bisphosphonates specifically — **not to denosumab** (see 0.1.2).

### 0.1.2 RANK Ligand Inhibitors
- **Key agent:** **denosumab** — a monoclonal antibody against **RANKL**, preventing osteoclast formation, function and survival. Subcutaneous, **6-monthly** for osteoporosis (more frequent at oncology doses).
- **Indications:** osteoporosis at high fracture risk, including where bisphosphonates are contraindicated; **it is not renally cleared, which makes it valuable in chronic kidney disease**; bone metastases and giant cell tumour of bone.
- **Adverse effects:** **hypocalcaemia** — potentially severe, and **markedly more likely in renal impairment and vitamin D deficiency; correct calcium and vitamin D before every dose**; MRONJ and atypical femoral fracture (as for bisphosphonates); cellulitis; eczema.

> [!danger] **DENOSUMAB MUST NOT SIMPLY BE STOPPED — the rebound effect causes MULTIPLE VERTEBRAL FRACTURES.**
> Sources are unambiguous: **fracture risk rises rapidly after discontinuation and multiple vertebral fractures occur with greater frequency**, because **bone density is lost within months** unless another agent is started. **A bisphosphonate must be given when denosumab is stopped.** Sources explicitly distinguish denosumab (and teriparatide, romosozumab, oestrogens and raloxifene) from bisphosphonates in **not having prolonged effects after discontinuation** — so the "drug holiday" concept **does not apply**, and a missed or delayed dose matters.
> **Practical consequence for an intern: a patient admitted who is overdue their 6-monthly denosumab needs it arranged, not deferred**, and a patient stopping it needs a documented handover plan.

### 0.1.3 Parathyroid Hormone Analogues
- **Key agents:** **teriparatide** (PTH 1-34); abaloparatide.
- **Mechanism:** the counter-intuitive one — **continuous** PTH exposure causes bone resorption, but **INTERMITTENT daily injection is ANABOLIC**, stimulating osteoblasts and building new bone.
- **Role:** **severe osteoporosis with fractures**, or failure of / intolerance to antiresorptives. **Restricted in Australia by PBS criteria with a lifetime duration limit — check the current criteria.**
- **Adverse effects:** nausea, headache, dizziness, **postural hypotension after the first doses (give the first dose where the patient can sit or lie down)**, hypercalcaemia, leg cramps. **Contraindicated** in Paget disease, prior skeletal irradiation, unexplained raised ALP, bone malignancy or metastases, and hypercalcaemia (the historical osteosarcoma signal in rats).
- **Sequencing matters:** it is **followed by an antiresorptive** to retain the gains — stopping without one loses them.

### 0.1.4 Sclerostin Inhibitors
- **Key agent:** **romosozumab** — a monoclonal antibody against **sclerostin**, uniquely **both anabolic and antiresorptive**, given monthly for a limited course (conventionally 12 months).
- **Role:** severe osteoporosis at very high fracture risk; **followed by an antiresorptive**, as with teriparatide.

> [!danger] **Romosozumab carries a boxed warning for MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION, STROKE AND CARDIOVASCULAR DEATH.** It is **contraindicated within the previous 12 months of a myocardial infarction or stroke**, and cardiovascular risk must be weighed before starting. Sources also note **MRONJ and atypical femoral fracture have been reported rarely** with it.

### 0.1.5 Calcimimetics
- **Key agents:** **cinacalcet** (oral); etelcalcetide (intravenous, for haemodialysis patients).
- **Mechanism:** allosterically **increase the sensitivity of the parathyroid calcium-sensing receptor** to calcium, so the gland "reads" the calcium as higher and **PTH secretion falls**.
- **Indications:** **secondary hyperparathyroidism in dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease**; **parathyroid carcinoma**; and **primary hyperparathyroidism where parathyroidectomy is not possible** — note it lowers calcium and PTH but **does not protect bone or prevent stones the way surgery does**, so it is not equivalent to an operation.
- **Adverse effects:** **nausea and vomiting** (dose-limiting), **hypocalcaemia** (monitor calcium closely, and watch the QT), adynamic bone disease with over-suppression.

### 0.1.6 Vitamin D
- **Key agents:** **cholecalciferol (D₃)** and ergocalciferol (D₂) — need hepatic and renal hydroxylation; **calcitriol** and **alfacalcidol** — the activated forms, which bypass the failing kidney.
- **Indications:** deficiency; with calcium in **osteoporosis** prevention and treatment; rickets and osteomalacia; malabsorption; **chronic kidney disease and hypoparathyroidism (activated forms)**.
- **Cautions:** **the activated forms cause hypercalcaemia far more readily** than cholecalciferol and need closer monitoring; **check calcium in anyone on high-dose vitamin D**. See `NEW_Drugs_07_Blood_and_Electrolytes.md` 0.4.1 for the toxicity picture.
- **Practical:** **correct vitamin D and calcium BEFORE starting any antiresorptive** — this is the commonest cause of post-treatment hypocalcaemia and is preventable.

### 0.1.7 Other Drugs Affecting Bone
- **Calcium supplements** — with vitamin D where dietary intake is inadequate (see `NEW_Drugs_07_Blood_and_Electrolytes.md` 0.3.2).
- **Menopausal hormone therapy and raloxifene** (a SERM) — reduce fracture risk; raloxifene reduces vertebral fracture and breast cancer risk but **increases VTE and hot flushes**, and does not protect the hip.
- **Strontium ranelate** — largely withdrawn from use over cardiovascular risk.
- **Calcitonin** — now little used (malignancy signal with long-term use); occasional short-term role in acute vertebral fracture pain and hypercalcaemia.
- **Non-drug management is not optional:** **falls prevention, resistance and weight-bearing exercise, adequate protein and calcium intake, smoking and alcohol reduction**, and **a review of drugs that harm bone** — glucocorticoids, aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation, some antiepileptics, long-term heparin and PPIs. **Anyone starting long-term glucocorticoids needs bone protection considered from the outset.** See [[11_08b_Ortho_-_Paget_s_Disease_and_Osteoporosis]] and `NEW_Investigations_Orthopaedics_Neurology_and_Other.md` 0.4.

## 0.2 Drugs for Diabetes

> [!info] **The modern framing for type 2 diabetes: choose the agent for the COMORBIDITY, not just the HbA1c.** Metformin remains the usual first agent, but **an SGLT2 inhibitor is added for heart failure or chronic kidney disease, and an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist for established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease** — for their outcome benefits, largely independent of glucose lowering. **Weight, hypoglycaemia risk, renal function, cost and PBS criteria** then decide between the rest.

### 0.2.1 Biguanides (Metformin)
- **Mechanism:** reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis, increases peripheral insulin sensitivity, and acts on the gut incretin axis. **It does not stimulate insulin secretion, so it does not cause hypoglycaemia alone.**
- **Role:** **first-line oral agent** in type 2 diabetes; also used in **PCOS** and in prediabetes. Weight-neutral to weight-reducing, cheap, long safety record.
- **Adverse effects:** **gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhoea, abdominal discomfort, metallic taste** (dose-related; mitigate by starting low, titrating slowly, taking with food, and using the **extended-release** formulation); **vitamin B₁₂ deficiency with long-term use — check B₁₂ periodically**, particularly in anyone with neuropathy, which is otherwise attributed to the diabetes.
- **Lactic acidosis:** sources note metformin is **contraindicated below an eGFR of about 30 mL/min/1.73 m²** because of lactic acidosis risk, though **it is rare in contemporary practice**. Risk rises with **acute kidney injury, hypoxia, sepsis, liver failure, alcohol excess and contrast**.

> [!danger] **METFORMIN "SICK DAY RULES": withhold during acute dehydrating illness** — vomiting, diarrhoea, sepsis, or anything causing volume depletion — **and around iodinated contrast and surgery per local protocol**, restarting when the patient is eating, drinking and renal function is confirmed stable. Sources note candidly that **trial evidence for this practice is lacking** while the rationale is sound and it is standard care. **Sick day advice must be given at the time of prescribing, not discovered during an admission with acute kidney injury.**

### 0.2.2 Sulfonylureas
- **Mechanism:** close the **ATP-sensitive potassium channel** on the beta cell → depolarisation → insulin release. **Insulin secretion is stimulated regardless of the glucose level — which is why they cause hypoglycaemia.**
- **Key agents:** **gliclazide** (generally preferred — shorter-acting, lower hypoglycaemia risk, largely hepatically metabolised), glimepiride, glipizide, glibenclamide (**avoid in the elderly — long-acting, high hypoglycaemia risk**).
- **Adverse effects:** **HYPOGLYCAEMIA — the defining risk**, and it can be **prolonged and recurrent**, especially with renal impairment, the elderly, poor intake, alcohol, and long-acting agents; **weight gain**; rash and, rarely, haematological effects.

> [!danger] **SULFONYLUREA HYPOGLYCAEMIA REQUIRES ADMISSION AND PROLONGED OBSERVATION — dextrose alone perpetuates it.**
> Giving glucose stimulates further insulin release from the drug-bound beta cell, so the patient dips again hours later. **These patients are admitted, observed, and given a dextrose infusion; OCTREOTIDE is the specific measure** that suppresses the insulin release (see `NEW_Drugs_04_Antidotes_and_Antivenoms.md` 0.1.1). **Never discharge a patient home after correcting a sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia.**

- **Practical:** hold during fasting, prolonged illness or reduced intake; **check driving advice and the patient's hypoglycaemia awareness.**

### 0.2.3 Meglitinides
- **Key agents:** **repaglinide**, nateglinide.
- **Mechanism:** the same beta-cell potassium channel as sulfonylureas but a **different binding site, with rapid onset and short duration**.
- **Role:** **taken with meals** to target postprandial glucose — useful in erratic eating patterns and in those who skip meals (the dose is skipped with the meal). Less used now that DPP-4 inhibitors and GLP-1 agonists exist.
- **Adverse effects:** **hypoglycaemia (less than sulfonylureas, but real)**, weight gain. Repaglinide has significant **CYP interactions — gemfibrozil is contraindicated with it** (profound hypoglycaemia).

### 0.2.4 Thiazolidinediones
- **Key agent:** **pioglitazone** (rosiglitazone withdrawn from general use).
- **Mechanism:** **PPAR-γ agonists** — increase peripheral insulin sensitivity, particularly in adipose tissue. Durable glucose lowering; no hypoglycaemia alone; beneficial effects on hepatic steatosis.
- **Adverse effects that limit it:** **fluid retention and OEDEMA, and precipitation of HEART FAILURE — contraindicated in heart failure**; **weight gain**; **fractures**, particularly distal fractures in women; **macular oedema**; a debated bladder cancer signal; and hepatotoxicity (monitor LFTs).
- **Where it still has a place:** insulin-resistant patients, and **MASLD/NASH**, where it is one of the few agents with evidence.

### 0.2.5 Alpha-Glucosidase Inhibitors
- **Key agent:** **acarbose**.
- **Mechanism:** inhibits intestinal **α-glucosidase**, delaying carbohydrate digestion → blunts the **postprandial** glucose rise.
- **Role:** modest efficacy; rarely used in Australia because of tolerability.
- **Adverse effects:** **flatulence, bloating and diarrhoea** from unabsorbed carbohydrate reaching the colon — the reason patients stop.

> [!danger] **If a patient on acarbose has hypoglycaemia (from a co-prescribed sulfonylurea or insulin), treat with ORAL GLUCOSE (dextrose) — NOT sucrose, table sugar, juice or lollies.** Acarbose blocks the breakdown of disaccharides, so sucrose will not be absorbed and the patient will not improve. This is a small, specific, exam-favourite fact with real consequences.

### 0.2.6 DPP-4 Inhibitors (Gliptins)
- **Mechanism:** inhibit **dipeptidyl peptidase-4**, the enzyme that degrades endogenous **GLP-1 and GIP** → modest, **glucose-dependent** increase in insulin and decrease in glucagon.
- **Key agents:** **sitagliptin, linagliptin, vildagliptin, saxagliptin, alogliptin**.
- **Role:** **weight-neutral, very well tolerated, no hypoglycaemia alone**, oral, once daily — which makes them useful in the **elderly and frail** and where hypoglycaemia must be avoided. Modest HbA1c reduction, and **no cardiovascular or renal outcome benefit** (their main limitation against the SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 agonists).
- **Adverse effects:** generally minimal; **pancreatitis** (rare, class labelling); **arthralgia** (occasionally severe and reversible on stopping — worth remembering in unexplained joint pain); **bullous pemphigoid** — a recognised and easily missed association; **saxagliptin and alogliptin were associated with increased heart failure hospitalisation** — avoid in heart failure.
- **Practical:** **linagliptin needs no renal dose adjustment** (biliary excretion), which is convenient in significant renal impairment. **Do not combine a DPP-4 inhibitor with a GLP-1 receptor agonist** — same pathway, no added benefit.

### 0.2.7 GLP-1 Receptor Agonists
*(covers build-list classes: GLP-1 Receptor Agonists; Glucagon-like peptide-1 analogues)*
- **Mechanism:** agonists at the GLP-1 receptor → **glucose-dependent** insulin secretion, **glucagon suppression**, **delayed gastric emptying**, and **central appetite suppression**. Glucose dependence means **little hypoglycaemia unless combined with insulin or a sulfonylurea**.
- **Key agents:** **semaglutide** (weekly injection; oral formulation available), **dulaglutide** (weekly), **exenatide**, **liraglutide** (daily); **tirzepatide** is a dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist.
- **Indications:** type 2 diabetes — particularly with **obesity** and with **established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease**, where several agents have demonstrated cardiovascular and renal outcome benefit; and **obesity management** at specific doses. **PBS criteria and supply shortages in Australia have been a recurring practical issue — check current availability and criteria.**
- **Adverse effects:** **nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation** — very common, dose-related, and reduced by slow titration and smaller meals; **weight loss** (usually desired, but assess for sarcopenia and malnutrition in older patients); **gallstones and cholecystitis** with rapid weight loss; **injection site reactions**; **pancreatitis** — sources advise **stopping the GLP-1 and seeking urgent assessment if pancreatitis is suspected**; a thyroid **C-cell tumour signal in rodents** means they are **contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2**; and **diabetic retinopathy** can worsen transiently with rapid glucose improvement.

> [!danger] **DELAYED GASTRIC EMPTYING AND ANAESTHESIA: a genuinely new and important perioperative issue.** Retained gastric contents despite standard fasting have caused **pulmonary aspiration** during sedation and anaesthesia. **Tell the anaesthetist and the endoscopist that the patient is on a GLP-1 agonist**, and follow local guidance on withholding it before a procedure. **Also warn patients about severe nausea and vomiting**, which can cause dehydration and acute kidney injury, and about **not using these agents in gastroparesis**.

### 0.2.8 SGLT2 Inhibitors
- **Mechanism:** block the **sodium-glucose co-transporter 2** in the proximal tubule → **glycosuria**, with additional natriuresis, weight loss, blood pressure reduction and — the important part — **haemodynamic and metabolic effects on the kidney and heart that are largely independent of glucose lowering.**
- **Key agents:** **dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin**.
- **Indications:** type 2 diabetes; and, increasingly, as **cardiorenal drugs in their own right — heart failure (both reduced and preserved ejection fraction) and chronic kidney disease, WITH OR WITHOUT diabetes** (see `NEW_Drugs_06_Cardiovascular.md` 0.10.4).
- **Adverse effects:** **genital mycotic infection** (very common — warn about it, and about hygiene); urinary tract infection; **volume depletion and hypotension**, especially with diuretics and in the elderly; **an initial dip in eGFR that is expected and haemodynamic, not injury** — do not stop the drug for it; **Fournier gangrene** (rare but catastrophic — perineal pain, swelling and erythema is a surgical emergency); and **increased amputation and fracture signals** with some agents.

> [!danger] **EUGLYCAEMIC DIABETIC KETOACIDOSIS — the reason an intern must know this class.**
> **The patient is acidotic and ketotic with a NEAR-NORMAL blood glucose**, so the diagnosis is missed. **Check ketones and a blood gas in ANY unwell patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor**, regardless of the glucose reading. Precipitants are **fasting, surgery, acute illness, dehydration, low-carbohydrate diets and alcohol**.
> **Withhold SGLT2 inhibitors before surgery and during acute illness or fasting** — Australian perioperative guidance specifies a withholding period of several days before an elective procedure. **This is one of the highest-yield practical facts in current diabetes prescribing**, and the drug is now taken by many patients who do not have diabetes at all.

### 0.2.9 Insulins
*(covers build-list classes: Insulins (Rapid-Acting); Insulins (Short-Acting); Insulins (Intermediate-Acting); Insulins (Long-Acting))*
- **Rapid-acting analogues — aspart, lispro, glulisine** (and faster formulations). Onset in minutes, peak ~1 hour, duration a few hours. **Given with meals**, and used in pumps and in **variable-rate intravenous insulin infusions**.
- **Short-acting (neutral/regular human insulin — Actrapid, Humulin R).** Slower onset and longer duration than the analogues; still used **intravenously** (DKA, hyperkalaemia, infusions), where it is the standard.
- **Intermediate-acting — isophane/NPH.** Peaks at several hours; used once or twice daily, and the peak is the source of nocturnal hypoglycaemia.
- **Long-acting analogues — glargine, detemir, degludec.** Flat, peakless profiles over 24 hours or more; **less nocturnal hypoglycaemia** than NPH. **Degludec is ultra-long-acting**, which affects how quickly dose changes take effect and how long an overdose lasts.
- **Premixed preparations** combine a rapid or short component with an intermediate one in a fixed ratio.
- **Regimens:** **basal-bolus** (physiological, flexible, the standard in type 1 diabetes), **twice-daily premixed**, **basal-only** added to oral agents in type 2, and **pump therapy** (continuous subcutaneous infusion, increasingly with automated closed-loop glucose control).

> [!danger] **INSULIN IS ONE OF THE HIGHEST-RISK MEDICINES IN THE HOSPITAL. The errors are predictable and preventable.**
> - **NEVER abbreviate "units" as "U" or "IU"** — it is misread as a zero or a four and has caused ten-fold overdoses. **Write the word "units" in full.**
> - **Never use an intravenous syringe to draw up insulin** — use an **insulin syringe** or the pen device.
> - **Insulin names look and sound alike** (glargine/glulisine, Humalog/Humulin, NovoRapid/NovoMix). **Check the exact product, not the family.**
> - **NEVER omit basal insulin in a person with TYPE 1 DIABETES, even if they are fasting or nil by mouth** — they have no endogenous insulin, and omission causes **diabetic ketoacidosis**. This is a common and dangerous ward error. Adjust and monitor, do not stop.
> - **Prescribe and chart hypoglycaemia treatment alongside insulin.**
> - **Check that the patient's own regimen, devices and hypoglycaemia awareness are documented on admission**, and involve the diabetes team.
> - **Insulin requirements change with steroids (up), illness and infection (up), renal impairment (down — insulin is renally cleared, and a stable patient developing renal failure will start having hypos), and weight loss (down).**
> - **Hypoglycaemia: treat with fast-acting oral carbohydrate if conscious and able to swallow; intramuscular glucagon or intravenous glucose if not; then give longer-acting carbohydrate and look for the cause.** See 0.3.
> - See [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] and [[15_16b_Paeds_-_Diabetes_Mellitus__MODY__DKA]].

### 0.2.10 Other Drugs for Diabetes
- **Amylin analogue — pramlintide** (limited availability); **bromocriptine** and **colesevelam** have minor glucose-lowering indications overseas.
- **Drugs for diabetes complications:** ACE inhibitors and ARBs for **albuminuria** (renoprotective independent of blood pressure), **finerenone** in diabetic kidney disease, statins for cardiovascular risk, and agents for **neuropathic pain** (see `NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological.md` 0.6).
- **Non-drug management is the foundation:** structured education, dietetic input, physical activity, **glucose monitoring and continuous glucose monitoring** (subsidised in Australia for defined groups — check current NDSS eligibility), sick day plans, **driving advice**, foot care, and **annual screening for retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy**.
- **Vaccination** — influenza, pneumococcal and COVID-19 are specifically recommended in diabetes.

## 0.3 Drugs for Hypoglycaemia

### 0.3.1 Glucagon Receptor Agonists
- **Key agent:** **glucagon** — intramuscular, subcutaneous or intranasal; also given intravenously in specific settings.
- **Mechanism:** stimulates hepatic **glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis**, raising blood glucose. Also has **inotropic and chronotropic effects mediated independently of the β-receptor**.
- **Indications:** **severe hypoglycaemia where the patient cannot take oral carbohydrate and intravenous access is not available** — which is exactly the community and pre-hospital situation it exists for; **beta-blocker and calcium channel blocker overdose** (see `NEW_Drugs_04_Antidotes_and_Antivenoms.md` 0.1.1); and as a smooth muscle relaxant for radiological and endoscopic procedures.

> [!danger] **GLUCAGON DOES NOT WORK IF THERE IS NO GLYCOGEN TO MOBILISE.**
> It is **ineffective or unreliable in prolonged fasting or starvation, alcohol-related hypoglycaemia, liver failure, adrenal insufficiency and glycogen storage disease** — precisely some of the situations in which severe hypoglycaemia occurs. **In hospital, intravenous glucose is the treatment**; glucagon is for when access is not available.
> **After glucagon works, give oral carbohydrate** — the effect is short-lived and glycogen stores are now depleted, so the patient will drop again.
> **It is much less effective in sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycaemia** and can even worsen it by stimulating insulin release — those patients need dextrose, admission and octreotide (see 0.2.2).

- **Adverse effects:** nausea and vomiting (common — position the patient safely), hypokalaemia, rebound hyperglycaemia.
- **Practical:** **every person at risk of severe hypoglycaemia should have glucagon available and their family or carers trained to use it** — the intranasal formulation has made this considerably easier. **Prescribing insulin without addressing hypoglycaemia rescue is incomplete care.**
- **Other agents:** **diazoxide** (inhibits insulin release — congenital hyperinsulinism and insulinoma); **octreotide** (sulfonylurea overdose, insulinoma); **glucose gel and dextrose** in various concentrations.

## 0.4 Drugs for Thyroid Disorders

### 0.4.1 Thyroid Hormones
*(covers build-list classes: Thyroid hormones; Synthetic Thyroid Hormones)*
- **Key agents:** **levothyroxine (T4)** — the standard; **liothyronine (T3)** — rarely, in myxoedema coma and in specialist settings.
- **Indications:** **hypothyroidism** (primary, central and post-ablative), **congenital hypothyroidism** (detected on newborn screening — see `NEW_Investigations_Orthopaedics_Neurology_and_Other.md` 0.17, and **treatment is urgent** because delay causes irreversible intellectual disability), TSH suppression after thyroid cancer, and **myxoedema coma**.

> [!info] **Administration determines whether it works.** Sources describe levothyroxine as recommended **in a fasting state, 30–60 minutes before breakfast**, with **up to 80% absorbed when fasting**, and note that **food and several medications interfere with absorption**. Consistency matters more than the precise timing — bedtime dosing, at least 3 hours after food, is a validated alternative for people who cannot manage the morning routine.
- **Interactions that cause apparent treatment failure:** **calcium, iron, magnesium, antacids, proton pump inhibitors, colestyramine, sucralfate, soy and coffee** reduce absorption — **separate by at least 4 hours**. **Enzyme inducers (rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine) increase requirements**, as does **oestrogen** (raised thyroxine-binding globulin) and **pregnancy**.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **PREGNANCY: levothyroxine requirements INCREASE substantially and early.** Women on thyroxine planning or confirmed pregnant need **prompt dose increase and early TFT monitoring** — untreated maternal hypothyroidism harms fetal neurodevelopment. This is a standing action that is frequently missed.
> - **In the ELDERLY and in ISCHAEMIC HEART DISEASE, start LOW and go SLOW** — rapid replacement precipitates angina, arrhythmia and infarction.
> - **Exclude ADRENAL INSUFFICIENCY before starting thyroxine** in anyone in whom it is possible: giving thyroxine first raises metabolic demand and can **precipitate an adrenal crisis**. Treat the adrenal insufficiency first.
> - **Over-replacement is harmful, not benign** — atrial fibrillation and osteoporosis. **Check TSH about 6 weeks after any dose change**, and do not chase symptoms with dose increases in a patient with a suppressed TSH.
> - **Do not treat "subclinical" abnormalities reflexively** — repeat, look for non-thyroidal illness, and consider the patient's age and symptoms.

### 0.4.2 Antithyroid Drugs (Thioamides)
*(covers build-list classes: Antithyroid drugs; Thioamides)*
- **Key agents:** **carbimazole** (and its active metabolite methimazole) — first-line in Australia; **propylthiouracil (PTU)**.
- **Mechanism:** inhibit **thyroid peroxidase**, blocking iodination and coupling of thyroglobulin. **PTU additionally inhibits peripheral T4→T3 conversion**, which is why it is used in thyroid storm.
- **Indications:** **hyperthyroidism** — Graves disease (as a course aiming for remission, or as long-term therapy), toxic nodular disease, and preparation for surgery or radioiodine.

> [!danger] **AGRANULOCYTOSIS — the warning every patient must be given, in words they will remember.**
> Sources are explicit that the **earliest sign is a SORE THROAT, MOUTH ULCERS, or a FEVER/flu-like illness**, that it is a **medical emergency**, and that **stopping the drug immediately allows recovery**. **Tell every patient starting carbimazole or PTU: "If you get a sore throat, mouth ulcers or a fever, STOP the tablet and get a full blood count the same day."** Give it in writing. Routine monitoring FBCs do not reliably catch it, because onset is abrupt — **the patient's symptom is the monitoring**.
> **Any patient on a thioamide presenting with fever or sore throat gets an urgent FBC before anything else.**

- **Other adverse effects:** rash and urticaria (common, often manageable), arthralgia, **hepatotoxicity** — and here the two agents differ importantly: **PTU causes severe, sometimes fatal hepatic necrosis**, which is why **carbimazole is preferred**; **carbimazole causes a cholestatic picture**. **ANCA-associated vasculitis** is described with PTU.
- **Pregnancy — the one situation where PTU is preferred, and only in part of it:** **carbimazole/methimazole is associated with embryopathy (aplasia cutis, choanal and oesophageal atresia) in the FIRST trimester**, so **PTU is used in the first trimester and carbimazole thereafter** (because of PTU's hepatotoxicity). This switching is specialist-managed and is a classic examinable point.
- **Monitoring:** thyroid function (**TSH lags and stays suppressed for months — titrate on free T4/T3 early on**), FBC and LFTs at baseline and if symptomatic.

### 0.4.3 Other Drugs for Thyroid Disorders
- **Beta-blockers — propranolol** — control the adrenergic symptoms (tremor, palpitations, anxiety, heat intolerance) **immediately**, while thioamides take weeks; propranolol also **blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion** at higher doses.
- **Iodine (Lugol's solution, potassium iodide)** — the **Wolff-Chaikoff effect**: high-dose iodine acutely blocks hormone release. Used in **thyroid storm** and to reduce gland vascularity before surgery. **Must be given AFTER a thioamide**, not before — otherwise it provides substrate and worsens thyrotoxicosis.
- **Radioactive iodine (I-131)** — definitive treatment for Graves disease and toxic nodules. **Contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding**, requires radiation precautions, commonly causes **permanent hypothyroidism** (an accepted outcome), and can **worsen Graves ophthalmopathy** — corticosteroid cover is used in those at risk.
- **Corticosteroids** — thyroid storm (block T4→T3 conversion and treat any relative adrenal insufficiency), and **Graves ophthalmopathy**; **teprotumumab** and orbital radiotherapy in specialist ophthalmopathy care.
- **Cholestyramine and, in extremis, plasmapheresis** — thyroid storm.

> [!danger] **THYROID STORM and MYXOEDEMA COMA are endocrine emergencies with high mortality.** Storm: fever, tachyarrhythmia, agitation or coma, heart failure, gastrointestinal symptoms — treated with **beta-blocker, thioamide, iodine (after the thioamide), corticosteroid, cooling and treatment of the precipitant** in a critical care setting. Myxoedema coma: hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation, hyponatraemia and reduced consciousness — treated with **intravenous thyroid hormone plus corticosteroid (given first, until adrenal insufficiency is excluded)** and supportive care. **Both need immediate senior and endocrine involvement.** See [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]].

## 0.5 Drugs for Other Endocrine Disorders

### 0.5.1 Systemic Corticosteroids (Glucocorticoids)
*(covers build-list classes: Corticosteroids; Glucocorticoids (Systemic))*
- **Key agents:** **prednisolone** (the oral workhorse), **hydrocortisone** (short-acting, with mineralocorticoid activity — the replacement and emergency agent), **dexamethasone** (long-acting, minimal mineralocorticoid activity — cerebral oedema, croup, antiemesis, fetal lung maturation), **methylprednisolone** (pulsed intravenous).
- **Indications:** **replacement** in adrenal insufficiency, and **pharmacological** anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive use across almost every specialty.
- **Adverse effects — comprehensive, and largely predictable from physiology:** hyperglycaemia and precipitation of diabetes; **hypertension, fluid retention and hypokalaemia** (mineralocorticoid effect); **osteoporosis and avascular necrosis**; **infection and reactivation of TB, hepatitis B and Strongyloides**; **peptic ulceration**, particularly with NSAIDs; **myopathy**; **skin thinning, bruising and striae**; **cataract and glaucoma**; **weight gain and Cushingoid habitus**; and **psychiatric effects — insomnia, agitation, mania, depression and psychosis**, which are common, dose-related, and frequently unattributed.

> [!danger] **NEVER STOP LONG-TERM GLUCOCORTICOIDS ABRUPTLY, AND ALWAYS INCREASE THEM WHEN THE PATIENT IS ILL.**
> Sources describe the risk of HPA suppression as arising with **more than about 3–4 weeks of therapy at doses above roughly 15–25 mg hydrocortisone equivalent daily**, and note that **recovery of adrenal function varies greatly between individuals** and can take months.
> - **SICK DAY RULES:** sources describe **doubling the oral dose for fever above 38 °C and tripling above 39 °C**, while noting honestly that **evidence for the optimal dose and duration is lacking**. **The patient must be taught this and given it in writing.**
> - **If vomiting or unable to take oral medication, they need PARENTERAL hydrocortisone** — and should have an emergency injection kit and know how to use it.
> - **SURGERY:** sources describe **hydrocortisone 100 mg intravenously at induction followed by an infusion of 200 mg per 24 hours** until the patient can take double their usual oral dose — but **follow your local protocol**, and tell the anaesthetist.
>   - **THESE TWO FIGURES ARE ADULT DOSES. DO NOT USE THEM IN A CHILD.** Paediatric perioperative and stress glucocorticoid cover is dosed **by body weight or body surface area**, not as a fixed adult quantity; 100 mg at induction is a large multiple of a small child's requirement. Use the paediatric protocol and the treating endocrinology team — an absolute figure copied across from an adult is the specific error CLAUDE.md rule 5 exists to prevent.
> - **Every patient on long-term steroids should carry a STEROID CARD or equivalent alert.**
> - **ADRENAL CRISIS** — hypotension unresponsive to fluids, vomiting, abdominal pain, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia, fever, confusion. **Give hydrocortisone IMMEDIATELY on suspicion — do not wait for a cortisol result.** Take the blood, then treat.
> - **Adrenal suppression also occurs with INHALED, INTRANASAL, TOPICAL and INTRA-ARTICULAR steroids**, especially at high dose, in children, and with **ritonavir/cobicistat or azole** interactions.
- **Alongside any prolonged course:** consider **bone protection, gastroprotection, glucose monitoring, blood pressure, PJP prophylaxis at higher doses, vaccination before starting, and ophthalmology review.**

### 0.5.2 Mineralocorticoids
- **Key agent:** **fludrocortisone**.
- **Mechanism:** potent mineralocorticoid receptor agonist → sodium and water retention, potassium excretion.
- **Indications:** **primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease) — replacement alongside a glucocorticoid**; congenital adrenal hyperplasia; **orthostatic hypotension and autonomic failure** (see `NEW_Drugs_06_Cardiovascular.md` 0.11.5).
- **Not needed in SECONDARY adrenal insufficiency** — the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis is intact, and this distinction is frequently misunderstood.
- **Adverse effects:** **hypertension, oedema, heart failure, hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis.**
- **Monitoring:** blood pressure (lying and standing), **potassium, sodium, and plasma renin activity** as a guide to adequacy of replacement in Addison disease.

### 0.5.3 Dopamine Agonists (Ergot Derivatives)
- **Key agents:** **cabergoline** (preferred — twice weekly, better tolerated, more effective), **bromocriptine** (used where pregnancy is planned, given longer experience), quinagolide.
- **Mechanism:** **D₂ receptor agonists** on pituitary lactotrophs → **suppress prolactin secretion and shrink prolactinomas**.
- **Indications:** **hyperprolactinaemia and prolactinoma** — sources describe cabergoline as **the preferred agent because of high efficacy and tolerability**, and **medical therapy shrinks even macroadenomas, so surgery is usually not first-line** (a genuinely distinctive fact among pituitary tumours); acromegaly (adjunct); Parkinson disease (largely superseded); and **lactation suppression** (used selectively).
- **Adverse effects:** **nausea, postural hypotension and dizziness** (start low, take at night with food), headache, nasal congestion, constipation; **impulse control disorders — pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping and eating** — which are **under-recognised, devastating, and must be asked about directly at every review, including of the partner**; psychosis and hallucinations.
- **CARDIAC VALVULOPATHY:** sources describe **cabergoline-associated valvulopathy** as the echocardiographic triad of **moderate-to-severe regurgitation, valvular thickening and restricted motion**, affecting the **mitral, tricuspid and aortic** valves — a class effect of ergot-derived agonists at the 5-HT₂B receptor. It is largely a concern at the **high doses used in Parkinson disease**, and prospective studies at prolactinoma doses have been reassuring, but **echocardiographic surveillance is advised for prolonged or higher-dose therapy** — check current local practice.
- **Practical:** **before diagnosing a prolactinoma, exclude the common causes of a raised prolactin** — **antipsychotics and other dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, domperidone)**, pregnancy, hypothyroidism, renal failure, chest wall stimulation, and **macroprolactin** (a laboratory artefact). See `NEW_Investigations_Obstetrics_and_Gynaecology.md` 0.4.

### 0.5.4 Somatostatin Analogues
- **Key agents:** **octreotide** and **lanreotide** (long-acting depot formulations); pasireotide.
- **Mechanism:** synthetic somatostatin analogues acting mainly at **SSTR2** → inhibit growth hormone, insulin, glucagon, gastrin, VIP, serotonin and other hormone secretion, and reduce splanchnic blood flow.
- **Indications:** **acromegaly** — sources describe somatostatin analogues as **first-line medical treatment, mainly targeting SSTR2**, controlling symptoms and hormonal hypersecretion and often **shrinking the tumour**; **neuroendocrine tumours and carcinoid syndrome** (symptom control and antiproliferative effect); **variceal bleeding** (octreotide infusion); **sulfonylurea overdose** (see 0.2.2); high-output fistulas and refractory diarrhoea.
- **Adverse effects:** **gastrointestinal — abdominal pain, bloating, steatorrhoea and diarrhoea**; **GALLSTONES and biliary sludge** (from inhibited gallbladder contraction — common with long-term use); **hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia** (insulin and glucagon are both suppressed; **pasireotide causes significant hyperglycaemia**); bradycardia; injection site reactions; and **hypothyroidism** with long-term use.
- **Related:** **pegvisomant** is a GH receptor antagonist used when somatostatin analogues fail (it normalises IGF-1 but does not shrink the tumour, so imaging surveillance continues); **cabergoline** is an adjunct.

### 0.5.5 Growth Hormone
- **Key agent:** **somatropin** (recombinant human GH), and long-acting weekly formulations.
- **Indications:** **paediatric GH deficiency**; **Turner syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome, chronic kidney disease, SGA with failure of catch-up growth, and idiopathic short stature** in defined circumstances; **adult GH deficiency**; and **short bowel syndrome** and HIV lipodystrophy in specific settings. **Access in Australia is tightly criteria-based through the PBS Growth Hormone Program — check the current criteria.**
- **Adverse effects:** **fluid retention, arthralgia and myalgia, carpal tunnel syndrome**; **insulin resistance and hyperglycaemia**; **benign intracranial hypertension**; **slipped upper femoral epiphysis and scoliosis progression** in children; and, in Prader-Willi syndrome, caution with **severe obesity, sleep apnoea or respiratory impairment**, where deaths have been reported.
- **Contraindications:** **active malignancy**, active proliferative retinopathy, and acute critical illness (where GH increased mortality in trials).
- **Monitoring:** growth velocity and IGF-1 in children; IGF-1, glucose, lipids and body composition in adults. **Misuse for athletic performance and anti-ageing is common, unapproved and harmful** — worth recognising.

### 0.5.6 Vasopressin Receptor Antagonists
- **Key agent:** **tolvaptan** — an oral **V2 receptor antagonist** producing **aquaresis** (free water excretion without electrolyte loss); conivaptan is a parenteral V1a/V2 antagonist.
- **Indications:** **hypervolaemic and euvolaemic hyponatraemia, including SIADH**, where fluid restriction has failed; and at higher doses to **slow progression in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease**.
- **Adverse effects and the specific dangers:** **thirst and polyuria** (expected); **over-rapid correction of hyponatraemia with the risk of OSMOTIC DEMYELINATION** — so it is **started in hospital with frequent sodium monitoring, and fluid restriction is lifted at initiation**; and **HEPATOTOXICITY**, particularly at ADPKD doses, requiring a formal liver monitoring programme.
- See `NEW_Drugs_06_Cardiovascular.md` 0.11.4 and `NEW_Investigations_Orthopaedics_Neurology_and_Other.md` 0.2 for the wider hyponatraemia context.
