# NEW — Drug Classes: Dermatological (AMH section 8)

> [!warning] **Standalone build, not yet integrated.** No cross-references written into existing corpus files.

> [!danger] **Sourcing limitation applying to this whole file.** The **Australian Medicines Handbook and Therapeutic Guidelines are subscription-gated and egress-blocked** in this environment. Entries are **snippet-sourced**, and **no doses are stated anywhere in this file** — with one exception noted below where the **fingertip unit** is given, because it is a *quantity-of-application* rule rather than a drug dose and is the standard way topical quantity is communicated.

> [!info] **The principle that governs the whole section: in topical therapy, the VEHICLE and the QUANTITY matter as much as the active drug.**
> **Ointments** are greasier, more occlusive and more potent for the same steroid, and are better for dry, lichenified skin; **creams** are better tolerated and suit weeping or flexural areas; **lotions, gels and foams** suit hairy areas and the scalp. **Under-prescribing quantity is the commonest reason topical treatment "fails"** — a patient given a 15 g tube for widespread eczema cannot possibly apply enough.

## 0.1 Drugs for Acne

> [!info] **Match the treatment to the lesion type, and combine mechanisms.** **Comedonal** acne → a topical retinoid. **Inflammatory papulopustular** → benzoyl peroxide ± topical retinoid ± topical or oral antibiotic. **Nodulocystic or scarring** → **oral isotretinoin, referred early**. Combination therapy attacking different mechanisms outperforms monotherapy, and **treatment takes 6–8 weeks to show benefit** — telling the patient that prevents them abandoning it at week three.

### 0.1.1 Keratolytics
- **Mechanism:** loosen and dissolve the **stratum corneum** and the keratinous plug, reducing comedone formation and aiding penetration of other agents.
- **Key agents:** **benzoyl peroxide** (also directly antibacterial and oxidising), **salicylic acid**, azelaic acid, urea, lactic and glycolic acid, sulfur.
- **Indications:** acne (benzoyl peroxide, salicylic and azelaic acid); **hyperkeratotic conditions** — warts, corns and calluses (high-strength salicylic acid), keratosis pilaris, ichthyosis and xerosis (urea, lactic acid); seborrhoeic dermatitis (sulfur, salicylic acid).

> [!danger] **BENZOYL PEROXIDE IS THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT AGENT FOR PREVENTING ANTIBIOTIC RESISTANCE IN ACNE.**
> *Cutibacterium acnes* readily develops resistance to macrolides and tetracyclines. **Benzoyl peroxide has no resistance**, and **co-prescribing it with any topical or oral antibiotic markedly reduces the emergence of resistant organisms.** **Never prescribe a topical antibiotic for acne as monotherapy**, and never continue an oral antibiotic for acne indefinitely — cap the course and hand over to a maintenance topical retinoid.

- **Adverse effects:** irritation, dryness, peeling and erythema — **expected, dose-related, and the reason to start low-strength, alternate-night, and build up**. **Benzoyl peroxide BLEACHES fabric** — warn about towels, pillowcases, clothing and hair; contact allergy occurs.
- **Cautions:** **salicylic acid over large areas or in children risks systemic salicylism**; avoid keratolytics on broken or inflamed skin and near the eyes.

### 0.1.2 Topical Retinoids (RAR Agonists)
*(covers build-list classes: Retinoids (Topical RAR Agonists); Retinoids (skin))*
- **Mechanism:** bind **retinoic acid receptors**, normalising follicular keratinisation, **preventing microcomedone formation** — the precursor of every acne lesion — and exerting an anti-inflammatory effect.
- **Key agents:** **adapalene** (best tolerated, photostable, can be combined with benzoyl peroxide), **tretinoin**, **tazarotene**, isotretinoin gel; **trifarotene**.
- **Indications:** **comedonal and inflammatory acne — first-line, and the maintenance treatment after clearance**; photoageing; psoriasis (tazarotene); and, off-label, a range of keratinisation disorders.

> [!warning] **Retinoids cause an initial flare and irritation, and patients stop because nobody warned them.** Apply **at night, to dry skin, a pea-sized amount for the whole face, starting every second or third night**; expect dryness and peeling for the first weeks; use a non-comedogenic moisturiser; and **use sun protection** (photosensitivity, and Australian UV levels make this non-negotiable).
- **Contraindication:** **pregnancy.** Systemic absorption from topical use is low and the risk is much smaller than with oral retinoids, but **topical retinoids are avoided in pregnancy and when planning pregnancy** — use an alternative.

### 0.1.3 Systemic (Oral) Retinoids
*(covers build-list classes: Retinoids (Systemic); Retinoids (oral))*
- **Key agents:** **isotretinoin** (acne), **acitretin** (psoriasis and disorders of keratinisation — see 0.3.5), bexarotene (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma).
- **Isotretinoin — indications:** **severe nodulocystic or scarring acne**, acne unresponsive to adequate conventional therapy, and acne causing significant psychological distress. Sources note the Australian convention of a **cumulative total dose over roughly 5–8 months**. In Australia it is largely a **dermatologist-initiated** medicine.

> [!danger] **ISOTRETINOIN IS ONE OF THE MOST POTENT HUMAN TERATOGENS IN CLINICAL USE.**
> Sources state it is **highly teratogenic at all therapeutic doses**, with **malformations reported after a single dose in pregnancy** — producing **craniofacial, cardiac, thymic and central nervous system malformations**. Formal **pregnancy prevention programmes** (iPLEDGE in the US, the European PPP) require negative pregnancy tests before, during and after treatment and **two forms of contraception**; sources note candidly that even stringent programmes **do not eliminate fetal exposure**, which is an argument for taking the counselling seriously rather than treating it as paperwork.
> **Practical requirements:** exclude pregnancy before starting; **effective contraception from before treatment, throughout, and for at least a month after stopping**; monthly pregnancy testing; **no blood donation during or for a month after treatment**; and explicit documented counselling. **This applies to every patient who could become pregnant, including adolescents** — and the conversation must be had with them directly and privately.

- **Other adverse effects (sources name the systemic profile):** **hyperlipidaemia**, **hepatotoxicity**, **musculoskeletal pain**, and **psychiatric symptoms including depression and anxiety**; plus near-universal **cheilitis, dry eyes** (problematic for contact lens wearers), dry nose and epistaxis, photosensitivity, myalgia, and an **initial acne flare**. **Benign intracranial hypertension** occurs and is **markedly increased by concurrent tetracyclines — never combine isotretinoin with doxycycline or minocycline.**
- **Monitoring:** **pregnancy test, lipids and LFTs at baseline and during treatment**; sources note extra caution in **diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and mood disorder**. **Ask about mood at every review** and give the patient and family an explicit instruction to report change.
- **Caution:** avoid elective procedures causing dermal wounding (waxing, laser, dermabrasion) during and for a period after treatment.

### 0.1.4 Other Drugs for Acne
- **Topical antibiotics — clindamycin, erythromycin.** **Always with benzoyl peroxide, never alone** (see 0.1.1).
- **Oral antibiotics — doxycycline, minocycline, erythromycin.** For moderate-to-severe inflammatory acne, **for a limited course (conventionally around 3 months) with a topical retinoid continued as maintenance afterwards.** **Doxycycline: photosensitivity and oesophagitis** (see `NEW_Drugs_05_Anti_infectives.md` 0.2.13). **Minocycline is generally avoided** where alternatives exist — drug-induced lupus, hyperpigmentation, hypersensitivity and benign intracranial hypertension.
- **Combined oral contraceptives** — reduce free androgens; useful in women where acne is hormonally patterned or contraception is also wanted. Consider the VTE risk profile of the specific preparation.
- **Spironolactone** — an anti-androgen, genuinely effective for adult female acne, particularly jawline and hormonal patterns. Causes hyperkalaemia (usually not clinically significant in young healthy women, but check in renal impairment), menstrual irregularity and breast tenderness; **teratogenic — contraception required**.
- **Azelaic acid** — useful in pregnancy and in **post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation**, and in rosacea.
- **Topical dapsone**, **clascoterone** (a topical androgen receptor inhibitor), and intralesional corticosteroid for individual nodules.
- **Consider the underlying cause:** **PCOS** in a woman with acne, hirsutism and irregular cycles; drug causes (**corticosteroids, anabolic steroids, lithium, some antiepileptics, EGFR inhibitors**); and **acne fulminans** — an abrupt, ulcerating, systemically unwell presentation sometimes precipitated by starting isotretinoin, which needs corticosteroids and urgent specialist care. See [[09_03b_Dermatology_-_Acne_Vulgaris]].

## 0.2 Drugs for Eczema

### 0.2.1 Topical Corticosteroids
*(covers build-list classes: Corticosteroids (skin); Glucocorticoids (Topical))*
- **Mechanism:** bind the glucocorticoid receptor → anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, antiproliferative and vasoconstrictor effects in the skin.
- **Potency:** sources describe ranking into **four potency groups across seven classes**, from **mild** (hydrocortisone) through **moderate** (e.g. triamcinolone, betamethasone valerate 0.02%) and **potent** (betamethasone valerate 0.1%, mometasone, methylprednisolone aceponate) to **very potent** (betamethasone dipropionate in optimised vehicle, clobetasol). Sources note **efficacy and adverse effects are both greatest with the ultra-high-potency preparations, which should be used for limited periods (2–3 weeks)**.

> [!danger] **UNDER-TREATMENT IS THE COMMONER ERROR, AND "STEROID PHOBIA" IS A CLINICAL PROBLEM IN ITS OWN RIGHT.**
> Sources and the Australasian College of Dermatologists position are consistent: **topical corticosteroids are a safe and effective treatment for eczema**, and an umbrella review of their safety in atopic eczema is reassuring for appropriate use. Patients and parents routinely apply too little, too infrequently, for too short a time, because they fear the drug. **The consequences of under-treatment — persistent inflammation, sleep loss, infection, school and work absence, and impaired growth in severe childhood eczema — are real and common; the consequences of appropriate use are not.** **Address the fear explicitly, name the potency you are prescribing and why, and give a specific quantity and duration.**

> [!info] **The FINGERTIP UNIT (FTU) — how to prescribe quantity, and the one figure given in this file.**
> Sources define it as **the amount squeezed from the fingertip to the first crease ≈ 0.5 g, covering an area equal to two flat adult hands**. Adult body-area guides given: **face and neck 2.5 FTU · one arm 3 FTU · one leg 6 FTU · trunk front or back 7 FTU · one foot 2 FTU.** Use this to calculate a realistic tube size — and **prescribe enough**. (Paediatric FTU requirements are lower and are age-banded; use a paediatric chart rather than scaling an adult figure — **CLAUDE.md rule 5**.)

- **Adverse effects (local, and the reason potency is matched to site):** **skin atrophy, striae** (permanent), telangiectasia, purpura, hypopigmentation, **perioral dermatitis and steroid rosacea** on the face, hypertrichosis, delayed wound healing, and **masking or worsening of infection**. Sources note **atrophy risk rises with higher potency, occlusion, thinner skin and older age**, and that the **face, backs of the hands and intertriginous areas** are most susceptible; and that **side effects are rare with low-to-high potency used for three months or less, except on the face and neck, in flexures, and under occlusion.**
- **Systemic absorption and HPA suppression** are possible with very potent steroids over large areas, under occlusion, in infants (**high surface-area-to-weight ratio**) and in broken skin — a genuine paediatric consideration.

> [!info] **Tachyphylaxis.** Sources describe tolerance developing with chronic application — a loss of capillary vasoconstriction — with **capillaries regaining responsiveness after about 4 days**, which is the rationale for **pulsed or weekend ("proactive") regimens** rather than continuous daily application.

- **Practical rules:** **match potency to site and severity** — mild on the face, eyelids and flexures; potent for lichenified limb and trunk eczema; **avoid potent steroids on the face and in flexures other than briefly and deliberately**. **Apply to inflamed skin only, moisturise everywhere.** **Step down rather than stopping abruptly**, and consider proactive twice-weekly application to previously affected sites to prevent flares.
- **Emollients are the foundation, not an adjunct** — prescribed in large quantities (hundreds of grams a week for widespread disease), applied liberally and frequently, continued when the skin is clear, and used as soap substitutes. **Paraffin-based emollients are a fire hazard on clothing and bedding.**

> [!danger] **ECZEMA HERPETICUM is a dermatological emergency and topical corticosteroids will make it worse.** Suspect it when eczema deteriorates rapidly with **monomorphic punched-out erosions, vesicles, fever and pain** — it needs **systemic aciclovir**, ophthalmology review if periocular, and admission if unwell. **Bacterial superinfection** (weeping, crusting, pustules) is far commoner and needs antibiotics. See [[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]].

### 0.2.2 Topical Calcineurin Inhibitors
- **Key agents:** **tacrolimus ointment** and **pimecrolimus cream**.
- **Mechanism:** inhibit **calcineurin** in T cells, blocking cytokine transcription — an immunomodulatory rather than a corticosteroid mechanism.
- **The advantage that defines their place:** sources state they are **more selective, do NOT cause skin atrophy, and are not associated with significant systemic absorption** — so the **preferred sites are exactly those where corticosteroids cause trouble: the FACE, EYELIDS, NECK, FLEXURES and GENITAL AREA.** Sources describe tacrolimus for **moderate-to-severe** and pimecrolimus for **mild-to-moderate** disease.
- **Uses beyond eczema:** vitiligo, oral and genital lichen planus, lichen sclerosus, seborrhoeic dermatitis, and as **steroid-sparing maintenance** including proactive intermittent use.
- **Adverse effects:** **burning and stinging on application** — very common, worst in the first days, and the main reason patients stop; **warn them it settles**, and applying to dry skin or briefly refrigerating the product helps. **Alcohol causes facial flushing** with tacrolimus. Transient increase in folliculitis and herpetic infection.

> [!warning] **The malignancy "black box" warning is worth understanding rather than simply repeating.** A theoretical concern about lymphoma and skin cancer, extrapolated from systemic calcineurin inhibition in transplantation, prompted a regulatory boxed warning. **Long-term observational data have not confirmed an increased risk with topical use.** Nonetheless: use the lowest effective amount, avoid on clearly infected or premalignant skin, and **advise sun protection** — the latter being sensible in Australia regardless.

### 0.2.3 Topical PDE-4 Inhibitors
- **Key agent:** **crisaborole** (topical); **roflumilast** cream is used for psoriasis and seborrhoeic dermatitis in some markets.
- **Mechanism:** inhibit **phosphodiesterase-4**, raising intracellular cAMP and downregulating inflammatory cytokine production.
- **Role:** a **non-steroidal, non-calcineurin option** for mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis, including on the face and in children — its attraction is the absence of atrophy risk and the absence of the calcineurin-inhibitor boxed warning.
- **Adverse effects:** **application-site burning and stinging** (the dominant complaint, as with the calcineurin inhibitors).
- **Availability and PBS status in Australia vary — check before promising it to a patient.**

### 0.2.4 Other Drugs for Eczema
- **Emollients and soap substitutes** — the foundation of all eczema care (see 0.2.1).
- **Antihistamines** — **sedating antihistamines may help sleep during a severe flare, but they do not treat eczema itch**, which is not primarily histamine-mediated. **They are not recommended in children** (see `NEW_Drugs_01_Allergy_and_Anaphylaxis.md` 0.1.2).
- **Antiseptics and dilute bleach baths** — reduce *S. aureus* colonisation in recurrent infected eczema; preferred to repeated topical antibiotics, which drive resistance (see `NEW_Drugs_05_Anti_infectives.md` 0.2.20).
- **Wet wrap dressings** — very effective for severe flares, particularly in children, usually under specialist or nurse-led guidance.
- **Phototherapy (narrowband UVB)** — for extensive disease not controlled topically.
- **Systemic immunosuppression — ciclosporin, methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate** — specialist-initiated, with the monitoring burdens of each (see 0.3.4). **Check TPMT/NUDT15 before azathioprine** (see `NEW_Investigations_General_and_Preventive.md` 0.16).
- **Biologics and JAK inhibitors — dupilumab** (anti-IL-4Rα; adverse effect of note is **conjunctivitis**), tralokinumab, lebrikizumab; **oral JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, abrocitinib, baricitinib)** — rapid and effective but carrying class warnings for **infection including herpes zoster, VTE, major adverse cardiovascular events and malignancy**, requiring screening and monitoring. Specialist-initiated with PBS criteria.
- **Identify and treat the aggravators:** irritants and soaps, heat and sweat, house dust mite in sensitised patients, infection, and — in a minority — **allergic contact dermatitis**, which is why **patch testing** matters in refractory or unusually distributed eczema. See [[09_04_Dermatology_-_Eczema__Psoriasis__Rosacea]].

## 0.3 Drugs for Psoriasis

### 0.3.1 Topical Vitamin D Analogues
- **Key agents:** **calcipotriol** (calcipotriene), calcitriol, tacalcitol; commonly as a **fixed combination with betamethasone dipropionate**, which is more effective than either alone and is a mainstay of Australian practice.
- **Mechanism:** bind the vitamin D receptor → inhibit keratinocyte **proliferation**, promote differentiation, and modulate T-cell activity.
- **Role:** **first-line topical therapy for chronic plaque psoriasis**, alone or with a corticosteroid. Sources report **calcipotriol generally provides superior efficacy to betamethasone valerate or dithranol**.
- **Advantages:** no atrophy, no staining, cosmetically acceptable, and suitable for long-term maintenance — the properties that displaced tar and dithranol from routine use.
- **Adverse effects:** **local irritation**, especially on the face and in flexures (use an alternative there); **hypercalcaemia if applied to very large areas in excess** — respect the maximum weekly quantity; **calcipotriol is inactivated by salicylic acid** and is pH-sensitive, so do not mix or co-apply with acidic preparations.

### 0.3.2 Tars
- **Key agents:** **coal tar** preparations (creams, ointments, bath additives, shampoos), pine tar.
- **Mechanism:** incompletely understood — antiproliferative, anti-inflammatory, antipruritic and keratoplastic effects.
- **Role:** an **older but still useful** option for chronic plaque psoriasis and scalp psoriasis, and in seborrhoeic dermatitis; often combined with UVB phototherapy (the Goeckerman regimen).
- **Adverse effects and why use has declined:** **smell, staining of skin, hair, clothing and bedding, messiness**, folliculitis, irritation, and **photosensitivity**. Theoretical carcinogenicity from polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons has not been borne out in clinical use at therapeutic concentrations, but it contributes to caution.
- **Practical:** apply **in the direction of hair growth** to reduce folliculitis; warn about staining; **adherence is the limiting factor** — a treatment the patient will not use is not effective.

### 0.3.3 Topical Antimitotics
- **Key agent:** **dithranol (anthralin)**.
- **Mechanism:** inhibits keratinocyte proliferation (mitochondrial and DNA effects) and generates free radicals.
- **Role:** effective for **well-demarcated chronic plaques**, classically as **"short-contact therapy"** — applied for a limited period then washed off — and historically in day-treatment centres (the Ingram regimen with UVB).
- **Adverse effects:** **burns and severe irritation of surrounding NORMAL skin** — which is why it is applied **precisely to the plaque only**, sometimes with a protective barrier of soft paraffin on the margin; and **staining of skin (a temporary purple-brown), bath, clothing and linen (permanent)**.
- **Not used on the face, flexures or genitals.** Largely superseded by vitamin D analogues, but retained for resistant plaques.

### 0.3.4 Immunosuppressants for Psoriasis
- **Methotrexate** — a folate antagonist; **first-line systemic therapy** for extensive psoriasis and for **psoriatic arthritis**.

> [!danger] **METHOTREXATE IS DOSED ONCE WEEKLY. Daily dosing has repeatedly killed patients.**
> **Write the day of the week on the prescription and the chart**, dispense in a way that makes daily dosing difficult, and **check the frequency with every patient at every review.** **Co-prescribe folic acid** on non-methotrexate days. **Monitor FBC, LFTs and renal function** at baseline and regularly. **Avoid in pregnancy (teratogenic and abortifacient — contraception required in both sexes) and in significant renal or liver impairment.** **Interactions: trimethoprim/co-trimoxazole and NSAIDs raise toxicity** — the co-trimoxazole combination has caused fatal pancytopenia. Watch for **pneumonitis** (dry cough and dyspnoea) and **mucositis**, and consider **hepatic fibrosis** with cumulative dosing. See [[12_01_Rheum_-_Rheumatoid_Arthritis__Osteoarthritis__Psoriatic_Arthritis]].

- **Ciclosporin** — a calcineurin inhibitor; rapid and effective, used for **short courses and for severe flares** rather than long-term, because of **nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hyperkalaemia, hyperlipidaemia, gingival hyperplasia, hypertrichosis** and cumulative renal damage. **Extensive CYP3A4 interactions** — check every co-prescription.
- **Azathioprine and mycophenolate** — less used in psoriasis than in eczema and other dermatoses; **check TPMT/NUDT15 before azathioprine**, and remember the **azathioprine–allopurinol interaction** (see `NEW_Drugs_03_Analgesics.md` 0.1.1).
- **Apremilast** — an oral PDE-4 inhibitor; modest efficacy, no laboratory monitoring required, but causes **diarrhoea, nausea, weight loss and depression** (ask about mood).
- **Biologics** — TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, certolizumab), IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, brodalumab), IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab), IL-12/23 (ustekinumab), and **oral JAK/TYK2 inhibitors (deucravacitinib)**. Highly effective; **PBS authority criteria in Australia are specific and change — check them.** See 0.3.5 and `NEW_Drugs_14_Immunomodulators_and_Antineoplastics.md` 0.5.

> [!danger] **BEFORE ANY SYSTEMIC IMMUNOSUPPRESSANT OR BIOLOGIC: screen for latent TUBERCULOSIS, HEPATITIS B and C, and HIV; check the vaccination history and give indicated vaccines BEFORE starting (live vaccines are contraindicated afterwards); and consider STRONGYLOIDES serology in anyone with relevant exposure.** Hepatitis B reactivation and TB reactivation are preventable and are missed regularly. See `NEW_Drugs_05_Anti_infectives.md` 0.6.3 and 0.1.2.

### 0.3.5 Other Drugs for Psoriasis
- **Topical corticosteroids** — widely used, usually with a vitamin D analogue; **avoid abrupt withdrawal of potent steroids from extensive psoriasis**, which can precipitate a **pustular or erythrodermic flare**.
- **Acitretin** — an oral retinoid, particularly useful in **pustular and palmoplantar psoriasis** and as an adjunct to phototherapy; **not immunosuppressive**, which is an advantage in some patients.

> [!danger] **ACITRETIN IS TERATOGENIC AND ITS PREGNANCY EXCLUSION PERIOD IS MEASURED IN YEARS, NOT WEEKS.**
> Acitretin is converted to **etretinate** — which has a very long half-life and is stored in fat — **particularly in the presence of ALCOHOL**. **Pregnancy must be avoided during treatment and for a prolonged period afterwards (conventionally three years in Australian practice), alcohol must be avoided during and after treatment, and blood donation is prohibited for the same period.** This is qualitatively different from isotretinoin's one-month rule and is a classic and consequential confusion.
> Other effects: cheilitis and mucocutaneous dryness, hyperlipidaemia, hepatotoxicity, hair loss, skeletal hyperostosis with long-term use, and benign intracranial hypertension (avoid with tetracyclines).

- **Salicylic acid** — descaling agent, particularly for scalp and thick plaques, improving penetration of other agents (**but it inactivates calcipotriol**).
- **Phototherapy — narrowband UVB** (first-line for extensive plaque disease) and **PUVA** (more effective but with a documented long-term skin cancer risk that matters especially in Australia).
- **Address the whole patient:** **psoriatic arthritis** (ask about joint pain and morning stiffness — early treatment prevents erosive damage), **cardiovascular and metabolic risk** (psoriasis is an independent risk factor — screen for hypertension, lipids, diabetes, obesity), **depression**, alcohol and smoking, and the **very substantial quality-of-life burden**.
- **Recognise the triggers and the emergencies:** **streptococcal throat infection precipitating guttate psoriasis**; **drugs that provoke psoriasis — lithium, beta-blockers, antimalarials, NSAIDs, and steroid withdrawal**; and **generalised pustular and erythrodermic psoriasis**, which are **dermatological emergencies** with fluid, thermoregulatory and infective risk requiring admission. See [[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]].

## 0.4 Drugs for Skin Infections

### 0.4.1 Topical Azoles and Antifungals for the Skin
- **Key agents:** **clotrimazole, miconazole, ketoconazole, econazole, bifonazole**; and the non-azoles **terbinafine (topical)**, ciclopirox, amorolfine (nail lacquer), nystatin (candida only), Whitfield ointment.
- **Indications:** **dermatophyte infections** — tinea corporis, cruris and pedis; **candidal intertrigo and napkin dermatitis**; **pityriasis versicolor**; **seborrhoeic dermatitis** (ketoconazole shampoo and cream — the yeast *Malassezia* is central to it).

> [!danger] **Confirm the diagnosis before treating nails or scalp, and know when topical treatment is NOT enough.**
> **Tinea capitis and onychomycosis require ORAL therapy** — topical agents do not penetrate the hair shaft or the nail plate adequately (amorolfine lacquer is the partial exception for very limited distal nail disease). **Take scrapings or clippings for microscopy and culture BEFORE starting**, because treatment reduces the yield and oral courses run for months (see `NEW_Investigations_Orthopaedics_Neurology_and_Other.md` 0.14 and `NEW_Drugs_05_Anti_infectives.md` 0.3.3).

> [!danger] **TINEA INCOGNITO: applying a topical corticosteroid to a fungal infection removes the scaly advancing edge, lets it spread, and makes it look like eczema.**
> Combined antifungal–corticosteroid preparations are widely used and widely misused for exactly this reason. **A "rash" that keeps coming back or worsens on a steroid cream should be scraped**, not escalated to a stronger steroid.

- **Other topical anti-infectives:** **mupirocin and fusidic acid** for localised impetigo and nasal decolonisation — **short courses only**, because resistance rises quickly; **antiseptics (chlorhexidine, povidone-iodine, dilute bleach baths)** preferred where an antibacterial effect is wanted without an antibiotic; **topical aciclovir and penciclovir** for cold sores (marginal benefit); **imiquimod and podophyllotoxin** for anogenital warts (see 0.6). All are covered in `NEW_Drugs_05_Anti_infectives.md` 0.2.20 and 0.6.6.
- **Adverse effects:** local irritation and burning; **contact allergic dermatitis to the topical agent itself**, which mimics treatment failure.

## 0.5 Scabicides and Pediculicides

- **Key agents:** **permethrin 5% cream** — sources describe it as the **first-choice topical treatment in Australia and available without prescription**, and as **the most effective topical agent compared with benzyl benzoate, crotamiton, lindane and sulfur**; **benzyl benzoate** (a source notes one recent comparison favouring 25% benzyl benzoate over 5% permethrin — the evidence is not entirely settled, and local resistance patterns matter); **oral ivermectin**; crotamiton (antipruritic, weakly scabicidal); sulfur ointment (used in infants and pregnancy); and for **head lice**, permethrin, pyrethrins, malathion, and **dimeticone/isopropyl myristate physical agents**, which are increasingly preferred because lice cannot develop resistance to suffocation.

> [!info] **How to actually treat scabies — the details determine whether it works**
> - **Apply to the WHOLE body from the neck down**, including between fingers and toes, under nails, genitals, and the umbilicus — **and include the scalp, face and ears in infants, the elderly and the immunosuppressed.**
> - **Leave on for the specified time** (usually overnight) and **reapply after any handwashing**.
> - **Treat ALL household members and close/sexual contacts SIMULTANEOUSLY**, whether or not they itch — asymptomatic contacts are the reservoir and reinfestation is otherwise guaranteed.
> - **Repeat the treatment after about a week** to kill mites hatched from eggs that survive the first application.
> - **Wash clothing, bedding and towels in hot water and dry hot**, or seal in a bag for several days.
> - **Itch persists for 2–4 weeks after successful treatment** — this is **post-scabetic itch**, not treatment failure. **Warn the patient explicitly**, and treat with emollients, a mild-to-moderate topical corticosteroid and antihistamines. Repeated scabicide application for persistent itch causes irritant dermatitis and is a common error.

> [!danger] **CRUSTED (NORWEGIAN) SCABIES is a different disease and is a public health emergency, with particular importance in remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities in Australia.**
> Hyperinfestation with thousands to millions of mites, in the immunosuppressed, elderly, and those with HIV/HTLV-1 or neurological impairment. It is **extremely contagious**, presents with **thick hyperkeratotic crusts and often little or no itch**, and is regularly misdiagnosed as psoriasis or eczema. Sources describe treatment as **repeated topical permethrin or benzyl benzoate PLUS multiple doses of oral ivermectin**, with sources also describing combination therapy for nursing-home outbreaks as **topical permethrin plus two doses of ivermectin a week apart**. It requires **keratolytics to allow penetration, contact tracing, environmental measures, infection control precautions and public health involvement.**
> **Crusted scabies is a driver of recurrent Group A streptococcal skin infection, and therefore of acute rheumatic fever, acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and sepsis** — which is why it matters far beyond the skin. See [[09_05_Dermatology_-_Bacterial_Infections_and_Infestations]].

- **Adverse effects:** local irritation and burning (**benzyl benzoate stings considerably, especially in children — dilution is used**); **lindane is neurotoxic and is not used**; **ivermectin** is generally well tolerated but is avoided in very young children and low body weight, and in pregnancy the safety data are limited (**permethrin is preferred in pregnancy and breastfeeding**).
- **Head lice practical points:** **diagnosis requires finding a live louse** (nits alone do not indicate active infestation and do not justify treatment or school exclusion); **conditioner-and-comb detection and removal** is effective and cheap; **resistance to neurotoxic pediculicides is widespread**, which favours the physical/dimeticone agents; **repeat treatment after 7 days**; and **treat only those with live lice**.

## 0.6 Drugs for Warts

- **Key agents:** **salicylic acid** (with or without lactic acid) — the mainstay; **cryotherapy** with liquid nitrogen (a procedure rather than a drug, but the usual comparator); **imiquimod** (an immune response modifier, principally for anogenital warts); **podophyllotoxin** (anogenital warts); **5-fluorouracil**; **cantharidin**; **formaldehyde and glutaraldehyde** soaks for mosaic plantar warts; and **intralesional agents** (bleomycin, candida antigen) for recalcitrant lesions.
- **Indications:** **common, plantar and plane viral warts**, and **anogenital warts** (a distinct clinical situation).

> [!info] **The counselling point that matters most: most cutaneous warts resolve spontaneously**, particularly in children — often within one to two years. **Treatment is for symptoms, cosmetic concern or interference with function, and is not urgent.** Salicylic acid requires **weeks to months of daily application with paring of the surface between applications and occlusion** — patients abandon it because nobody told them how long it takes.
- **Adverse effects:** irritation, blistering, pain, and **hypopigmentation and scarring with cryotherapy** (relevant on the face and in darker skin, where **post-inflammatory pigmentary change is common and prolonged**).
- **Cautions:** **avoid salicylic acid and cryotherapy over large areas in diabetes and peripheral vascular disease** — ulceration on an ischaemic or neuropathic foot can be limb-threatening. **Podophyllotoxin is contraindicated in pregnancy**; **imiquimod causes deliberate, sometimes marked, local inflammation** which should be explained or the patient will stop.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Anogenital warts in a CHILD require careful assessment for sexual abuse**, alongside the recognised routes of vertical and innocent transmission — this is a considered, multidisciplinary assessment, not an accusation and not a dismissal. See [[NEW_Safeguarding_and_Forensic]].
> - **Anogenital warts in adults warrant a full STI screen** and a discussion of **HPV vaccination** and of **cervical screening** (which is unaffected by wart status — see `NEW_Investigations_Obstetrics_and_Gynaecology.md` 0.1).
> - **A solitary, growing, bleeding or pigmented "wart" — particularly on the face, in an older person, or one that fails to respond — is not necessarily a wart.** Consider **squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, amelanotic melanoma and keratoacanthoma**, and biopsy. In Australia this is a high-prevalence differential and the commonest reason to stop treating and start investigating. See [[09_03a_Dermatology_-_Non-Melanoma_Skin_Cancer]].

## 0.7 Other Dermatological Drugs

- **Emollients, humectants and barrier preparations** — the highest-volume dermatological prescribing there is, and the most under-prescribed by quantity.
- **Antipruritics** — menthol in aqueous cream, crotamiton, doxepin cream; and the systemic options where itch is neuropathic or cholestatic (**gabapentinoids, sertraline, naltrexone, cholestyramine, rifampicin**). **Chronic generalised itch without a rash demands a work-up** — iron deficiency, renal and hepatic disease, thyroid disease, lymphoma and other malignancy, HIV, and drugs.
- **Drugs for hyperhidrosis** — **aluminium chloride hexahydrate** topically, oral anticholinergics (oxybutynin, glycopyrronium), **botulinum toxin** injection, and iontophoresis.
- **Drugs for rosacea** — topical **metronidazole, azelaic acid, ivermectin**, and **brimonidine** (for erythema, with the caveat of rebound flushing); oral **doxycycline** (often at sub-antimicrobial dose) and, in severe or phymatous disease, isotretinoin. Ocular rosacea needs recognition and ophthalmology input.
- **Drugs for alopecia** — **topical minoxidil**, **oral finasteride** (androgenetic alopecia in men; **teratogenic — women of childbearing potential must not handle crushed tablets**, and it causes sexual adverse effects that must be disclosed), intralesional and topical corticosteroids and **JAK inhibitors** for alopecia areata.
- **Drugs for pigmentary disorders** — **hydroquinone**, azelaic acid, topical retinoids and rigorous **sun protection** for melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation; topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, phototherapy and **JAK inhibitors** for vitiligo.
- **Photoprotection** — **sunscreen is a drug in this context and, in Australia, arguably the most important dermatological prescription there is.** Broad-spectrum SPF50+, applied in adequate quantity and reapplied, alongside clothing, hats and shade. **Relevant to every patient on a photosensitising drug** — doxycycline, thiazides, amiodarone, voriconazole, retinoids, methotrexate, St John's wort — and to every immunosuppressed patient, in whom **skin cancer surveillance** is mandatory.
- **Drugs for hidradenitis suppurativa** — topical clindamycin, oral tetracyclines, clindamycin–rifampicin combination, metformin, hormonal therapy, **adalimumab and secukinumab**, and surgery. See [[09_07_Dermatology_-_Chickenpox__Shingles__Pityriasis_Rosea__Hidradenitis_Suppurativa]].
- **Wound care products, keratolytics for corns and calluses, and drugs for pressure injury** — largely dressings and pressure relief rather than pharmacology.

> [!danger] **The dermatological drug reactions an intern must recognise, because they are drug-caused and life-threatening**
> **STEVENS-JOHNSON SYNDROME and TOXIC EPIDERMAL NECROLYSIS** — mucosal involvement (eyes, mouth, genitals), painful skin, Nikolsky sign, in the first weeks of a new drug (**allopurinol, lamotrigine, carbamazepine, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, nevirapine**). **DRESS** — rash, fever, eosinophilia, lymphadenopathy and organ involvement, characteristically **2–8 weeks** after starting the drug. **Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP)**. **STOP THE CULPRIT DRUG IMMEDIATELY, involve dermatology and, for SJS/TEN, a burns or intensive care unit, and document the reaction prominently so it is never re-prescribed.** See [[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]].
