## Acute Sore Throat

- **DDx:**
  - **Emergencies that present as "sore throat" and must be excluded first:** **epiglottitis/supraglottitis** (rapid onset, severe odynophagia out of proportion to visible findings, **drooling, muffled "hot potato" voice, tripod posture, stridor** — and often a near-normal-looking oropharynx, which is the trap); **peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)** (unilateral severe pain, trismus, uvular deviation, muffled voice); **parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal abscess** (neck stiffness, neck swelling, systemic toxicity — commoner in children); **Ludwig's angina** (bilateral submandibular swelling with a raised, firm floor of mouth and tongue elevation, usually of dental origin — a genuine airway emergency); **Lemierre's syndrome** (persisting sore throat with rigors and unilateral neck tenderness from internal jugular thrombophlebitis).
  - **Common infective causes:** viral pharyngitis (much the commonest), **group A streptococcal pharyngitis**, infectious mononucleosis (EBV — marked lymphadenopathy, fatigue, splenomegaly, and **a rash if given amoxicillin**), herpangina and hand-foot-and-mouth in children, herpes simplex gingivostomatitis, **acute HIV seroconversion**, gonococcal pharyngitis, candidiasis (consider immunosuppression or inhaled steroid use), diphtheria (in the unimmunised).
  - **Non-infective:** gastro-oesophageal reflux, post-nasal drip, allergy, smoking and irritants, vocal overuse, foreign body (**fish bone**), chemical or thermal injury, **agranulocytosis from a drug — clozapine, carbimazole, methimazole, sulfasalazine, chemotherapy** (a sore throat in a patient on any of these is a haematological emergency until the neutrophil count is known), malignancy, Kawasaki disease and other systemic inflammatory conditions in children.
- **Focused Hx:** duration and speed of onset; **fever**; **difficulty swallowing, swallowing saliva, or breathing** — the airway questions; **voice change and trismus**; unilateral versus bilateral pain; ear pain; **neck swelling and stiffness**; rash; fatigue and its duration (mononucleosis); cough and coryza (**their presence points away from streptococcal infection**); **contacts and recent streptococcal illness**; **immunisation status**; **medications, specifically clozapine, carbimazole and any recent chemotherapy**; immunosuppression; sexual history where gonococcal or HIV causes are plausible; smoking and alcohol; **and — in Australia specifically — whether the patient identifies as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander, and whether they live in a rural or remote community**, because that changes the management threshold (see the box below).
- **Examination:** **assess the airway before the throat.** Look for stridor, drooling, tripoding, respiratory distress and inability to swallow saliva — **if any of these are present, do not attempt to examine the throat with a tongue depressor, and call for senior and anaesthetic/ENT help immediately**, particularly in a child. Otherwise: vital signs; oropharyngeal inspection for tonsillar enlargement, exudate, unilateral swelling, **uvular deviation**, palatal petechiae, ulcers, membrane; **trismus**; **floor of mouth and tongue elevation**; **cervical lymphadenopathy — site and tenderness**; **abdominal examination for splenomegaly** where mononucleosis is possible (and the advice to avoid contact sport that follows from it); dentition; skin for rash; and a general septic screen.
- **First-line Ix:** **most acute sore throat needs no investigation.** Where indicated: **throat swab for culture** (and, in high-risk settings, for group A *Streptococcus*); **FBC with differential — urgently if the patient is on a drug that causes agranulocytosis**; **CRP**; **EBV serology or monospot** (noting it can be falsely negative in the first week and in young children); **HIV testing** where seroconversion is a possibility; **blood cultures if septic**; **lateral soft-tissue neck X-ray or CT neck** where a deep neck space infection or epiglottitis is suspected — **imaging must never delay airway management**; **ultrasound or CT** for suspected abscess.

> [!danger] **The Australian equity point, which changes practice rather than being background.** Acute rheumatic fever following group A streptococcal infection is **strongly concentrated among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, particularly children living in rural and remote communities**, where rates are among the highest reported anywhere in the world and vastly exceed those in the non-Indigenous population. The consequence at the bedside is that **the threshold for treating a sore throat with antibiotics is lower in a patient from a high-risk group** than the "most sore throats are viral" rule would otherwise suggest — this is a recognised primary-prevention strategy, and the relevant Australian guidance is RHDAustralia's.
> **The specific antibiotic, dose and duration are deliberately omitted** — the Australian guidance has been updated to expand the oral options for high-risk patients, and neither the current regimen nor the exact risk-group definitions could be read first-hand under the egress limitation. **Check the current RHDAustralia guideline and your local pathway.**
> **A specific incidence figure was retrieved and deliberately rejected.** One source gave ARF incidence as ">300 per 1,000 person-years" in Indigenous Australians against "<5 per 1,000" — a figure that is internally implausible (it would mean roughly a third of the population developing ARF each year) and almost certainly a units error in the source for a per-100,000 rate. **Under the three-source rule it does not qualify, and under this project's rule about absolute figures it fails a sanity check, so the disparity is stated in words rather than numbers.** Do not "restore" a number here without a primary source.

## Acute Ear Pain

- **DDx:**
  - **Primary otological:** **acute otitis media** (commonest in children — pain, fever, bulging red tympanic membrane; pain often relieved suddenly by perforation with discharge), **otitis externa** (pain on tragal pressure or pinna traction, canal oedema and discharge; consider swimming, hearing aids, cotton buds), **furunculosis**, **bullous myringitis**, **barotrauma**, **foreign body**, **trauma with tympanic perforation**, **herpes zoster oticus (Ramsay Hunt)** — vesicles in the canal with facial palsy and vertigo, **mastoiditis** (post-auricular swelling, tenderness and erythema with a **protruding pinna** — a complication needing urgent admission), **necrotising (malignant) otitis externa** — severe unremitting pain in an **older person with diabetes or immunosuppression**, with granulation tissue in the canal and risk of skull base osteomyelitis and cranial nerve palsies.
  - **Referred otalgia — and this is the group that matters, because the ear looks completely normal:** **dental disease** (much the commonest referred cause), **temporomandibular joint dysfunction**, **tonsillitis and peritonsillar abscess**, **pharyngeal or laryngeal malignancy** (via cranial nerves IX and X — **unilateral otalgia with a normal ear examination in an adult who smokes or drinks is head and neck cancer until proven otherwise**), cervical spine disease, **giant cell arteritis**, glossopharyngeal or trigeminal neuralgia, parotid disease, thyroiditis, angina.
- **Focused Hx:** unilateral or bilateral; onset and severity; **discharge — and whether the pain eased when it started**; hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo; **facial weakness**; fever; upper respiratory symptoms; **swimming, water exposure, cotton buds, hearing aids or earplugs**; trauma, air travel or diving; **dental symptoms, jaw clicking, bruxism**; **sore throat, dysphagia, hoarseness, neck lump, weight loss** — the head and neck cancer screen; **diabetes and immunosuppression**; smoking and alcohol; and in a child, immunisation and recurrence history.
- **Examination:** **otoscopy of both ears** — canal (oedema, discharge, debris, foreign body, granulation, vesicles), tympanic membrane (colour, position, perforation, fluid level, mobility if available); **tragal tenderness and pinna traction**; **post-auricular area — swelling, tenderness and the position of the pinna**; **facial nerve function**; hearing assessment including **Rinne and Weber**; **cervical lymph nodes**; and **when the ear is normal, examine the referral sites: teeth and TMJ (palpate with the jaw moving), oral cavity, oropharynx, tongue base, neck and thyroid**, and consider nasendoscopy referral.
- **First-line Ix:** usually **clinical**. Where indicated: **ear swab for culture** in refractory or severe otitis externa or in a discharging ear; **FBC, CRP, glucose/HbA1c** — the last specifically in suspected necrotising otitis externa; **blood cultures if systemically unwell**; **CT temporal bones** for suspected mastoiditis, cholesteatoma or necrotising otitis externa; **MRI** for skull base involvement; **audiometry**; **urgent ENT referral with nasendoscopy** for unexplained unilateral otalgia with a normal ear in an at-risk adult; **ESR and CRP** where giant cell arteritis is possible.

> [!warning] **Two patterns not to dismiss.** **(1) A normal ear does not mean a benign cause** — persistent unilateral otalgia with a normal examination in an adult with smoking or alcohol history requires visualisation of the pharynx and larynx, not reassurance. **(2) Severe, unremitting, night-waking ear pain in an older person with diabetes** suggests necrotising otitis externa, which is an osteomyelitis rather than a skin infection and needs urgent specialist involvement; treating it as simple otitis externa loses time.

## Acute Epistaxis

- **DDx and anatomy — the distinction that drives management:**
  - **Anterior bleeding (about 90%)** — from **Little's area (Kiesselbach's plexus)** on the anterior septum. Visible, usually controllable with first aid and local measures.
  - **Posterior bleeding** — from the sphenopalatine artery territory. **Bleeding from both nostrils, blood running down the throat, difficulty localising the source, and haemodynamic compromise** suggest it; it is commoner in older patients and is far more likely to need specialist packing or intervention.
  - **Local causes:** digital trauma (much the commonest in children), nasal trauma or fracture, dry air and mucosal crusting, **intranasal corticosteroid technique** (a genuinely common and correctable cause — spraying toward the septum rather than away from it), foreign body (**consider in a child with unilateral offensive discharge**), rhinitis and infection, septal perforation or deviation, **cocaine and other intranasal drug use**, **nasal or sinus tumour, and juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma** in an adolescent male with recurrent unilateral epistaxis.
  - **Systemic causes:** **anticoagulants and antiplatelets** (the most important in practice), **hypertension** (its role in causing epistaxis is debated, but it makes control harder), **thrombocytopenia and platelet dysfunction**, **haemophilia and von Willebrand disease**, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, **hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia** (recurrent epistaxis with mucocutaneous telangiectasia and a family history).
- **Focused Hx:** **which side it started on** and whether both sides are involved; duration and estimated volume, including **blood swallowed or vomited** (which is usually underestimated); **frequency and previous episodes and how they were controlled**; trauma or nose picking; **anticoagulants, antiplatelets and NSAIDs — with the last dose**; known bleeding disorder, liver or kidney disease; **family history of bleeding or telangiectasia**; hypertension; intranasal sprays and their technique; drug use; recent surgery; and **symptoms of hypovolaemia — dizziness, syncope, breathlessness**.
- **Examination:** **A–E first if bleeding is heavy — this can be a haemorrhagic emergency, particularly in an anticoagulated older patient.** Vital signs including postural blood pressure; pallor and perfusion. Then, with the patient **sitting up and leaning forward**, suction and inspect with a nasal speculum and light for an anterior bleeding point; examine the **oropharynx for blood tracking posteriorly** — the finding that suggests a posterior bleed; look for telangiectasia on the lips, tongue and fingers; check for signs of chronic liver disease and for bruising or petechiae elsewhere.
- **First-line Ix:** **none in a straightforward, self-limiting anterior bleed in a well patient.** Otherwise: **FBC** (haemoglobin and **platelet count**), **coagulation profile and INR** — essential in anyone anticoagulated, **group and hold or crossmatch** if bleeding is significant, **UEC and LFTs**; **β-hCG** where relevant; **nasendoscopy** by ENT for posterior or recurrent bleeding; **CT or MRI** where a tumour is suspected — particularly for **unilateral recurrent epistaxis with obstruction**.
- **Immediate management at intern level:** **sit the patient up and forward, apply firm continuous pressure to the soft cartilaginous part of the nose for a sustained period without releasing to check**, and ask them to spit out rather than swallow blood. Escalate for cautery or packing if this fails. **Reverse or withhold anticoagulation only after discussing with a senior**, weighing the indication for it. **Specific topical agents, packing products and tranexamic acid regimens are deliberately omitted** — practice varies between Australian services and the guidelines are egress-blocked.

## Acute Hoarseness

- **D:** a recent change in voice quality. Most acute hoarseness is benign and self-limiting; **the clinical task is to identify the minority that is not, and to know the duration at which "wait and see" stops being safe.**
- **DDx:**
  - **Acute and usually benign:** **acute viral laryngitis** (much the commonest), **vocal overuse or abuse**, acute allergic reaction, reflux laryngitis, inhaled corticosteroid use (candidiasis or local myopathy), smoking and irritant exposure, post-intubation or post-operative.
  - **Airway emergencies presenting with voice change:** **epiglottitis/supraglottitis**, **angioedema**, **inhalation or burn injury**, **anaphylaxis**, deep neck space infection — all of which come with other airway signs.
  - **Neurological:** **vocal cord palsy from recurrent laryngeal nerve injury** — and the causes of that are the important part: **thyroid or other neck surgery, lung apex or mediastinal malignancy, aortic arch pathology including aneurysm, oesophageal malignancy, and stroke**. A **left** recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy has a long intrathoracic course and so raises chest pathology specifically.
  - **Structural and neoplastic:** **laryngeal or hypopharyngeal carcinoma**, vocal cord nodules, polyps or cysts, papillomatosis, granuloma, **thyroid mass**.
- **Focused Hx:** **duration — the single most important question**; onset and progression; **voice use and occupation** (teachers, singers, call-centre workers); preceding upper respiratory infection; **smoking and alcohol — quantified**; reflux symptoms; **recent intubation or neck, thyroid or chest surgery**; inhaled corticosteroids; **the red flags: stridor or breathing difficulty, dysphagia or odynophagia, a neck lump, unilateral otalgia with a normal ear, haemoptysis, unexplained weight loss**; and other neurological symptoms.
- **Examination:** **listen to the voice and characterise it** — breathy suggests glottic incompetence or cord palsy; harsh or rough suggests a mass lesion; a **muffled "hot potato" voice suggests supraglottic pathology and an airway concern**. Then: **airway assessment for stridor and distress**; oral cavity and oropharynx; **neck palpation for masses, thyroid and lymphadenopathy**; **cranial nerve examination**; chest examination and assessment for a **Horner's syndrome** or clubbing where an apical lung lesion is possible; ear examination in a patient with referred otalgia.
- **First-line Ix:** in acute hoarseness with a clear viral cause, a well patient, and no red flags, **observation with voice rest is appropriate**. Otherwise:
  - **Laryngoscopy — direct visualisation of the vocal cords is the definitive investigation** and is the referral being made.
  - **Chest X-ray** in any patient with a suspected vocal cord palsy — looking for an apical or mediastinal lesion; **CT neck and chest from skull base to diaphragm** where a palsy is confirmed, to trace the whole course of the recurrent laryngeal nerve.
  - **TFTs and thyroid ultrasound** where a thyroid cause is suspected; **FBC, CRP**; **HbA1c**.

> [!danger] **The referral rule, and it is the reason this topic is Tier 2 rather than Tier 3.** **Hoarseness persisting beyond about three weeks without an obvious resolving cause requires urgent ENT referral for laryngoscopy**, and sources agree on this threshold (some state two to three weeks). The threshold falls further — refer sooner — in the presence of **smoking or alcohol history, a neck lump, dysphagia, odynophagia, unilateral otalgia with a normal ear, haemoptysis, stridor or weight loss**.
> **The specific documented failure mode is attributing hoarseness in a smoker to the smoking itself** rather than treating smoking as the reason to look at the larynx sooner. That single error accounts for a substantial share of delayed laryngeal cancer diagnoses.
