# NEW — Drug Classes: Psychotropic (AMH section 17)

> [!warning] **Standalone build, not yet integrated.** No cross-references written into existing corpus files.

> [!danger] **Sourcing limitation applying to this whole file.** The **Australian Medicines Handbook and Therapeutic Guidelines are subscription-gated and egress-blocked** in this environment. Entries are **snippet-sourced**, and **no doses are stated anywhere in this file** — with **one exception**, the **lithium therapeutic range (0.4.1)**, which is a monitoring target agreed across sources and is inseparable from safe use of the drug.

> [!info] **Principles for the whole section.** **Psychotropics work slowly and are stopped too quickly.** Most antidepressants take 2–4 weeks for onset and 6–8 weeks for full effect; antipsychotics similar. **Start low, titrate, and give an adequate trial at an adequate dose before switching.** Conversely, **do not continue indefinitely without review** — and **never stop abruptly**. **Ask about suicidal ideation directly** at initiation and at every review; asking does not increase risk, and not asking loses the information.

## 0.1 Antidepressants

> [!danger] **THREE THINGS THAT APPLY TO EVERY ANTIDEPRESSANT**
> **1. Discontinuation syndrome.** Sources describe a **range of somatic and psychiatric symptoms on abrupt discontinuation** — dizziness, "brain zaps", paraesthesia, nausea, flu-like symptoms, insomnia, vivid dreams, irritability and anxiety. **It is worst with short-half-life agents (paroxetine, venlafaxine) and least with fluoxetine.** **Taper slowly, sometimes over months**, and **warn the patient at the time of prescribing**, because otherwise they interpret it as relapse — or as evidence of "addiction".
> **2. Suicidality in young people.** There is an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviour in those **under 25** in the early weeks. **This is a reason for close early review, not for withholding treatment** — untreated depression is far more dangerous. **Arrange follow-up within 1–2 weeks of starting.**
> **3. Serotonin syndrome.** **Agitation, tremor, clonus (especially inducible ankle clonus), hyperreflexia, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, diarrhoea and autonomic instability**, usually within hours of a dose increase or an added serotonergic drug. **The culprits are cumulative:** SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, MAOIs, **tramadol, pethidine, fentanyl, triptans, linezolid, methylthioninium chloride, St John's wort, lithium and metoclopramide.** **Stop all serotonergic agents, cool, sedate with benzodiazepines, and involve critical care in severe cases.** It is distinguished from neuroleptic malignant syndrome by its **rapid onset, clonus and hyperreflexia** (NMS causes rigidity and evolves over days).

### 0.1.1 SSRIs
*(covers build-list classes: SSRIs; Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors)*
- **Key agents:** **sertraline, escitalopram, citalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine, fluvoxamine**.
- **Indications:** **depression, generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, OCD, PTSD, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, bulimia**; and **vasomotor symptoms of menopause** (see `NEW_Drugs_16_Obstetric_and_Gynaecological.md` 0.2.1).
- **Role:** **first-line** for most depressive and anxiety disorders — because of tolerability and safety in overdose relative to tricyclics, not because of superior efficacy.
- **Adverse effects:** **nausea and GI upset** (usually settles), **anxiety or agitation in the first days** (start low, warn), **SEXUAL DYSFUNCTION — very common, frequently the reason patients stop, and routinely not asked about**; insomnia or somnolence; weight change; **bruising and gastrointestinal bleeding** (antiplatelet effect — the risk is **additive with NSAIDs and anticoagulants, and a PPI should be considered**); and **bone loss with long-term use in older adults**.

> [!danger] **HYPONATRAEMIA (SIADH) IN OLDER PEOPLE — an under-recognised and consequential SSRI effect.**
> Sources report that **the majority of symptomatic cases occur in elderly patients (79% over 70) and in women (74%)**, and advise that **all SSRIs be used with care in the elderly with careful monitoring of water and electrolyte balance.** Severe cases cause **confusion, seizures and coma**. **Check sodium before starting and within the first weeks in an older patient**, and consider it in any older patient who becomes confused or falls after a new antidepressant.

> [!warning] **QT prolongation — citalopram and escitalopram specifically.** Sources state that **citalopram should be discontinued where the QTc persistently exceeds 500 ms**, and that **electrolytes should be monitored in patients at risk of hypokalaemia or hypomagnesaemia.** Dose limits apply in the elderly and in hepatic impairment.
- **Interactions:** **fluoxetine and paroxetine are potent CYP2D6 inhibitors** — clinically important with **tamoxifen** (reducing its activation — **use venlafaxine or escitalopram instead in women on tamoxifen**), codeine and tramadol (blocking activation), metoprolol and antipsychotics; **fluvoxamine inhibits CYP1A2** (clozapine, theophylline). **Sertraline and escitalopram have the fewest interactions** and are often the pragmatic choice in polypharmacy.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** generally usable, with **sertraline** commonly preferred; **paroxetine is usually avoided** (cardiac malformation signal). **Untreated perinatal depression carries substantial risk to mother and infant** — the decision is a balance, not an automatic cessation.

### 0.1.2 SNRIs
*(covers build-list classes: SNRIs; Serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors)*
- **Key agents:** **venlafaxine, desvenlafaxine, duloxetine**.
- **Indications:** depression and anxiety disorders; **duloxetine additionally for diabetic and other neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia and stress urinary incontinence** — which makes it a useful choice where pain and depression coexist.
- **Adverse effects:** the SSRI profile, plus **dose-related HYPERTENSION and tachycardia (check the blood pressure)**, sweating, and **a notably severe discontinuation syndrome with venlafaxine** because of its short half-life — missing a single dose can produce symptoms.
- **Cautions:** venlafaxine is **more toxic in overdose** than SSRIs (seizures, cardiac effects) — relevant when prescribing to someone at risk; duloxetine is avoided in significant hepatic impairment and heavy alcohol use.

### 0.1.3 Tricyclic Antidepressants
*(covers build-list classes: Tricyclic Antidepressants; Tricyclic antidepressants)*
- **Key agents:** **amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine, clomipramine, dosulepin, doxepin**.
- **Mechanism:** serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake inhibition, **plus antagonism at muscarinic, histaminic and α₁-adrenergic receptors** — which is where all the adverse effects come from.
- **Current role:** largely **second-line for depression**, but retained for **neuropathic pain, migraine prophylaxis, nocturnal enuresis, and OCD (clomipramine)**, usually at doses lower than antidepressant doses.
- **Adverse effects:** **anticholinergic** (dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, urinary retention, **confusion and delirium in the elderly**), **sedation and weight gain** (histaminic), **postural hypotension and falls** (α₁), and **cardiac conduction delay**.

> [!danger] **TRICYCLICS ARE LETHAL IN OVERDOSE, AND THIS DRIVES THE PRESCRIBING DECISION.**
> Overdose causes **QRS widening, arrhythmia, seizures, hypotension and coma** — **sodium bicarbonate is the key treatment for QRS widening and arrhythmia.** **Prescribe with great caution, in limited quantities, to anyone at risk of self-harm**, and consider whether an SSRI would do the same job. **Dosulepin in particular is now discouraged** because of its toxicity.
> **Avoid tricyclics in the elderly** where possible — they appear on every potentially-inappropriate-medication list, and the anticholinergic and falls burden is substantial. **Nortriptyline is the least anticholinergic** if one is needed.

### 0.1.4 Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors
*(covers build-list classes: MAOIs (Irreversible Non-selective); Monoamine oxidase inhibitors)*
- **Key agents:** **irreversible non-selective — phenelzine, tranylcypromine**; **reversible selective (RIMA) — moclobemide**; **selegiline** (MAO-B, transdermal at antidepressant doses).
- **Role:** **rarely used, and specialist-initiated** — but genuinely effective in **treatment-resistant and atypical depression**, which is why they persist.

> [!danger] **THE TWO MAOI DANGERS**
> **1. HYPERTENSIVE ("CHEESE") CRISIS with tyramine.** Irreversible non-selective MAOIs prevent breakdown of dietary tyramine → a hypertensive crisis with severe headache, and risk of intracranial haemorrhage. **A strict tyramine-restricted diet is required — aged cheeses, cured and fermented meats, soy products, tap beer, broad beans, yeast extracts (Vegemite) — and must be maintained for about two weeks after stopping.** **Indirect sympathomimetics (pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine, amfetamines) are also contraindicated.** **Moclobemide (reversible) carries far less dietary risk**, which is why it is the one an intern may actually encounter.
> **2. SEROTONIN SYNDROME.** MAOIs with **SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, tramadol, pethidine, fentanyl, triptans, linezolid or methylthioninium chloride** can be fatal. **WASHOUT PERIODS ARE MANDATORY when switching — two weeks in most directions, and FIVE WEEKS when switching from fluoxetine to an MAOI** because of fluoxetine's long half-life. **Pethidine is absolutely contraindicated.**
> **Any patient on an MAOI needs this flagged prominently in the record and communicated at every handover.**

### 0.1.5 NDRIs and Other Antidepressants
*(covers build-list classes: NDRIs; Other antidepressants)*
- **Bupropion (NDRI)** — noradrenaline and dopamine reuptake inhibition. **No sexual dysfunction and no weight gain** (often weight loss), which makes it useful where those are limiting. **Also licensed for smoking cessation** (0.7). **Lowers the seizure threshold — contraindicated in epilepsy, eating disorders and alcohol withdrawal.** Inhibits CYP2D6.
- **Mirtazapine** — α₂-antagonist with 5-HT₂/5-HT₃ blockade. **Sedating and appetite-stimulating at low doses** — genuinely useful in depression with insomnia and weight loss, and in the elderly and palliative settings. **No sexual dysfunction.** Adverse effects: weight gain, sedation, and rarely agranulocytosis.
- **Agomelatine** — melatonergic agonist and 5-HT₂C antagonist; sleep benefit, no sexual dysfunction, but **requires regular LFT monitoring** because of hepatotoxicity.
- **Vortioxetine** — multimodal serotonergic agent with claimed cognitive benefits.
- **Reboxetine** (NRI), **trazodone** (sedating, and causes **priapism** — a urological emergency the patient must be warned about), **St John's wort** (effective in mild depression but a **potent CYP3A4 and P-gp INDUCER** — it reduces contraceptives, warfarin, DOACs, immunosuppressants, antiretrovirals and many others, and patients do not report it as a medicine — **ask**).
- **Ketamine and esketamine** — for treatment-resistant depression, in supervised settings, with rapid onset; and **psilocybin and MDMA-assisted therapy**, for which **Australia introduced a regulatory pathway for authorised prescribers**, still a highly specialised area.
- **Non-drug treatments are not optional add-ons:** **psychological therapy is at least as effective as medication in mild-to-moderate depression**, exercise has good evidence, and **ECT remains the most effective treatment for severe, psychotic or catatonic depression and where a rapid response is needed** (see [[14_05d_Psych_-_Electroconvulsive_Therapy]]).

## 0.2 Antipsychotics

- **Mechanism:** **D₂ receptor antagonism** (or partial agonism) is common to all; **"atypical" (second-generation)** agents additionally have **5-HT₂A** antagonism and a lower rate of extrapyramidal effects, at the cost of greater metabolic burden.
- **Key agents:** **first-generation — haloperidol, chlorpromazine, zuclopenthixol, flupentixol**; **second-generation — olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride, aripiprazole and brexpiprazole (partial agonists), ziprasidone, lurasidone, asenapine, clozapine**; and **long-acting injectable ("depot")** formulations of several, which substantially improve adherence and reduce relapse.
- **Indications:** **schizophrenia and related psychoses; bipolar mania and maintenance; psychotic depression; adjunct in treatment-resistant depression; delirium (short-term, at low dose, where non-drug measures have failed); severe agitation; Tourette syndrome; and nausea (see `NEW_Drugs_12_Gastrointestinal.md` 0.2.2).**

> [!danger] **THE ADVERSE EFFECTS THAT MATTER, GROUPED BY MECHANISM**
> **Extrapyramidal (D₂ blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway):**
> - **Acute dystonia** — hours to days; oculogyric crisis, torticollis, trismus, laryngospasm. **Frightening, often misdiagnosed, and treated rapidly with benztropine or another anticholinergic.**
> - **Akathisia** — an inner restlessness and inability to keep still. **Profoundly distressing and ASSOCIATED WITH SUICIDE. It is frequently mistaken for worsening psychosis or anxiety, and the dose is increased, making it worse.** Ask about it explicitly; treat by reducing the dose, switching, or with propranolol or a benzodiazepine.
> - **Parkinsonism** — tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia; often misattributed to idiopathic Parkinson disease.
> - **Tardive dyskinesia** — late, involuntary orofacial and limb movements, **often irreversible**; risk rises with duration, age and first-generation agents.
> **Metabolic (especially olanzapine and clozapine):** **weight gain, dyslipidaemia, type 2 diabetes** — and **people with severe mental illness die 15–20 years earlier than the general population, largely from cardiovascular disease.** **Baseline and regular weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, lipids and glucose/HbA1c are part of prescribing an antipsychotic, not an optional extra.**
> **Hyperprolactinaemia (risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride, first-generation):** galactorrhoea, gynaecomastia, amenorrhoea, sexual dysfunction, and long-term bone loss. **Aripiprazole is prolactin-sparing.**
> **Cardiac:** **QT prolongation** (dose-related; worst with some agents), postural hypotension, and myocarditis with clozapine (0.2.1).
> **Anticholinergic and sedative:** particularly chlorpromazine, olanzapine and quetiapine.
> **Lowered seizure threshold**, and **temperature dysregulation**.

> [!danger] **NEUROLEPTIC MALIGNANT SYNDROME — recognise it and stop the drug.**
> **Fever, "lead-pipe" rigidity, altered conscious state, autonomic instability, raised CK and leucocytosis**, evolving over days. **Stop the antipsychotic, cool, resuscitate, and treat with supportive care ± dantrolene or bromocriptine.** It also occurs on **abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic drugs in Parkinson disease** (see `NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological.md` 0.2). **Distinguish from serotonin syndrome** (faster onset, clonus and hyperreflexia rather than rigidity).

> [!danger] **ANTIPSYCHOTICS IN OLDER PEOPLE WITH DEMENTIA INCREASE MORTALITY AND STROKE**, and are **contraindicated in Lewy body dementia and Parkinson disease dementia** because of severe neuroleptic sensitivity. See `NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological.md` 0.3.3.

### 0.2.1 Clozapine — a separate safety case
- **Role:** **the only agent with proven superiority in TREATMENT-RESISTANT schizophrenia**, and it also reduces suicidality. **It is under-used**, often because of monitoring burden.
- **Agranulocytosis:** **mandatory, protocolised full blood count monitoring** — frequent initially, then continuing for the life of the treatment, with defined thresholds for interruption and cessation. **In Australia, treatment is delivered through a registered clozapine monitoring program, and dispensing depends on the blood count being current.**

> [!danger] **CLOZAPINE-INDUCED MYOCARDITIS — commonest in the FIRST MONTH and with mortality up to 50% when undetected.**
> Sources describe **weekly monitoring of troponin and CRP (with CK) for the first 6–8 weeks**, and note that **combining symptom review with troponin and CRP gives 100% sensitivity for symptomatic myocarditis.** **Any fever, tachycardia, chest pain, dyspnoea or flu-like illness in the first weeks of clozapine must be investigated as myocarditis — not dismissed as an infection.** Australian units led much of this monitoring work.

> [!danger] **CLOZAPINE-INDUCED CONSTIPATION KILLS MORE PEOPLE THAN THE AGRANULOCYTOSIS DOES.**
> Sources are explicit: clozapine **impairs bowel function in the majority of patients**, ranging from constipation to **complete obstruction, paralytic ileus and intestinal ischaemia or infarction**, and the **case-fatality rate of clozapine-related lower gastrointestinal hypomotility is about SEVEN TIMES that of clozapine-related agranulocytosis.** The FDA has strengthened its warning accordingly. Risk rises with **higher doses and with co-prescribed anticholinergics or other constipating drugs.**
> **Practical: ask about bowels at every review, prescribe prophylactic laxatives proactively, avoid adding anticholinergics, and treat abdominal pain, distension or absent bowel motions in a clozapine patient as a potential surgical emergency.**
> **Other clozapine points:** **seizures** (dose-related), **hypersalivation** (paradoxical and socially disabling), sedation, weight gain and diabetes, myocarditis and cardiomyopathy, and **a rise in clozapine levels when SMOKING STOPS** (tobacco smoke induces CYP1A2 — so a patient admitted to a smoke-free hospital can become toxic within days). **Never abruptly stop and restart clozapine without re-titration** — restarting at the previous dose after more than about 48 hours off is dangerous.

## 0.3 Drugs for Anxiety and Sleep Disorders

### 0.3.1 Benzodiazepines
- **Key agents:** **diazepam, oxazepam, temazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam, clonazepam, nitrazepam**.
- **Mechanism:** positive allosteric modulation at **GABA-A**.
- **Legitimate indications:** **short-term severe anxiety or insomnia**, **alcohol withdrawal** (0.6), **status epilepticus** (see `NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological.md` 0.1.2), **acute agitation**, **procedural sedation**, muscle spasm, and **palliative care**.
- **Adverse effects:** sedation, impaired concentration and memory, **falls and fractures in older people**, **respiratory depression — profoundly potentiated by opioids**, **tolerance, dependence and withdrawal**, paradoxical disinhibition, and driving impairment.

> [!danger] **THE RULE: SHORT COURSES, SMALL QUANTITIES, AND AN EXIT PLAN AT THE TIME OF PRESCRIBING.**
> **Dependence develops within weeks.** **Withdrawal causes anxiety, insomnia, tremor, perceptual disturbance and SEIZURES, and can be life-threatening** — so long-term benzodiazepines are **tapered very slowly**, often over months, frequently after converting to a longer-acting agent.
> **In Australia, alprazolam was rescheduled to Schedule 8 (controlled drug) in 2014** because of misuse and overdose harm — worth knowing as an indicator of how the class is regarded.
> **The opioid–benzodiazepine combination is a leading cause of overdose death** — check the whole medication list, and consider whether take-home naloxone is warranted.
> **In older people, prefer the shorter-acting agents without active metabolites (oxazepam, temazepam)** and, better, avoid them; **benzodiazepines are on every deprescribing list.**

### 0.3.2 Barbiturates
- **Key agents:** **phenobarbital**, primidone, thiopentone (see `NEW_Drugs_02_Anaesthetics.md` 0.2.1).
- **Mechanism:** GABA-A modulation, but — unlike benzodiazepines — they **directly open the chloride channel at higher doses**, which is why they have **no ceiling effect and are lethal in overdose.**
- **Role:** **obsolete for anxiety and insomnia.** Retained for **epilepsy** (especially neonatal seizures and in resource-limited settings), **status epilepticus** as a later-line agent, and in palliative sedation.
- **Adverse effects:** profound sedation and respiratory depression, **potent enzyme induction** (extensive interactions), **dependence with a dangerous withdrawal syndrome**, and precipitation of **porphyria**.

### 0.3.3 Other Drugs for Anxiety and Sleep Disorders
- **For anxiety — the actual first-line treatments are SSRIs/SNRIs and psychological therapy (CBT)**, not benzodiazepines. Also **buspirone** (slow onset, no dependence), **pregabalin** (effective in generalised anxiety, but with misuse potential and sedation), **propranolol** (for the physical symptoms of performance anxiety), and **quetiapine at low dose** (widely used, with a metabolic cost that is often not acknowledged).
- **For insomnia — CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia) is the FIRST-LINE treatment and is more effective and more durable than any drug.** It is under-provided and under-referred.
- **Z-drugs — zolpidem, zopiclone.** Similar mechanism to benzodiazepines and **similar risks of tolerance, dependence, falls and next-day impairment** — the perception that they are meaningfully safer is not supported. **Zolpidem carries an Australian boxed warning for complex sleep behaviours — sleep-walking, sleep-driving and sleep-eating with amnesia.**
- **Melatonin** — modest benefit; prolonged-release melatonin is approved for insomnia in adults over 55, and it is used in circadian rhythm disorders, jet lag, and in children with neurodevelopmental conditions.
- **Sedating antihistamines** — widely bought over the counter; **anticholinergic, causing next-day impairment, and inappropriate in older people.**
- **Orexin receptor antagonists (suvorexant, lemborexant)** — a newer class for insomnia.
- **And always: look for the cause.** **Depression, anxiety, pain, obstructive sleep apnoea, restless legs, nocturia, alcohol, caffeine, shift work, and drugs** (steroids, stimulants, some antidepressants). **Treating "insomnia" with a hypnotic while missing sleep apnoea is common and harmful.**

## 0.4 Drugs for Bipolar Disorder

### 0.4.1 Lithium
- **Role:** **the most effective mood stabiliser for bipolar disorder**, with the strongest evidence for **preventing relapse of both mania and depression**, and — uniquely — **an anti-suicide effect independent of mood stabilisation**. It remains a first-line agent despite its monitoring burden.
- **Therapeutic monitoring — the one numeric range stated in this file, because safe use is inseparable from it:** sources give a **therapeutic range of approximately 0.5–1.2 mmol/L**, with levels taken **12 hours after the last dose**, and note **significant risk of intoxication above about 1.5 mmol/L**. Levels are checked after initiation and each dose change, then regularly; **thyroid, renal function, calcium and weight are monitored throughout** because lithium causes **hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism with hypercalcaemia, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and chronic kidney disease.**

> [!danger] **LITHIUM TOXICITY IS COMMON, PREVENTABLE, AND OFTEN CAUSED BY ANOTHER DRUG OR BY DEHYDRATION.**
> **Anything that reduces renal lithium clearance raises the level.** Sources name the key interactions: **NSAIDs**, **ACE inhibitors and ARBs** (with advice to monitor levels more frequently when starting, stopping or changing the dose), and **thiazide diuretics**, which sources describe as having the greatest potential — **increasing lithium concentrations by 25–40%.** **Dehydration, vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, a low-salt diet and acute kidney injury do the same.**
> **Recognise toxicity: coarse tremor (as opposed to the fine tremor of therapeutic use), vomiting and diarrhoea, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion, myoclonus, seizures, arrhythmia and coma.** **Severe toxicity requires haemodialysis.**
> **SICK DAY ADVICE IS ESSENTIAL AND MUST BE GIVEN AT PRESCRIBING: maintain fluid and salt intake, and withhold lithium and seek review during any illness causing dehydration, vomiting or diarrhoea.**
> **Never prescribe an NSAID to a patient on lithium without thinking about it** — patients buy them over the counter, so tell them.
> **Pregnancy:** lithium is associated with **cardiac malformations (Ebstein anomaly)** in the first trimester; the decision is a specialist balance, since relapse in pregnancy and postpartum is also dangerous. **Levels change markedly through pregnancy and immediately postpartum** and need close monitoring.

### 0.4.2 Other Mood Stabilisers
- **Sodium valproate** — effective in acute mania and maintenance. **But see the teratogenicity warning in `NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological.md` 0.1.1 — this applies with even greater force in bipolar disorder, where alternatives exist. Do not use valproate in anyone who could become pregnant unless there is genuinely no alternative.**
- **Lamotrigine** — particularly effective for **bipolar DEPRESSION and its prevention**; **slow titration is mandatory** because of the rash, and **the dose must be halved if valproate is co-prescribed** (see `NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological.md` 0.1.1).
- **Carbamazepine** — second-line; extensive enzyme induction.
- **Atypical antipsychotics** — **quetiapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole, risperidone, lurasidone and cariprazine** are all used, in acute mania, bipolar depression and maintenance, alone or with lithium or valproate.
- **Antidepressants in bipolar disorder** — used cautiously and generally **only with a mood stabiliser**, because of the risk of **switching into mania and of rapid cycling.** **Always screen for a history of mania before prescribing an antidepressant for depression** — this is a routinely missed step that causes harm.
- **ECT** — highly effective in severe mania, bipolar depression and where a rapid response is needed.

## 0.5 Drugs for Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder

### 0.5.1 Psychostimulants
- **Key agents:** **methylphenidate** (immediate and modified-release), **dexamfetamine**, **lisdexamfetamine** (a prodrug with lower misuse potential).
- **Mechanism:** increase synaptic **dopamine and noradrenaline** in prefrontal circuits.
- **Efficacy:** **large and rapid** — stimulants are among the most effective treatments in psychiatry, and treatment reduces accidents, injuries and substance misuse.
- **Adverse effects:** **reduced appetite and weight loss** (monitor growth in children), **insomnia** (dose timing matters), headache, **tachycardia and raised blood pressure**, anxiety and irritability, **tics** (usually manageable), and a **rebound "crash"** as the dose wears off.
- **Cautions:** cardiovascular history and family history of sudden death should be assessed before starting; caution in psychosis, bipolar disorder and substance use disorder.

> [!warning] **Australian regulatory context: stimulants are Schedule 8 controlled drugs, and prescribing generally requires a state authority and is usually restricted to paediatricians and psychiatrists** (with shared-care arrangements varying by jurisdiction, and several states expanding GP roles). **Real-time prescription monitoring applies.** **Diversion and misuse are real** — as study aids, for weight loss, and recreationally — and prescriptions should be quantity-limited with a single prescriber.

### 0.5.2 Non-Amphetamine Psychostimulants
- **Key agent:** **modafinil** (and armodafinil) — a wakefulness-promoting agent with a distinct mechanism.
- **Indications:** **narcolepsy and excessive daytime sleepiness** in defined conditions; used off-label in ADHD, fatigue and shift work disorder.
- **Adverse effects:** headache, nausea, anxiety, insomnia, and — importantly — **serious skin reactions including Stevens-Johnson syndrome**, and **it INDUCES CYP3A4, reducing the efficacy of hormonal contraception** (which must be discussed with anyone of reproductive potential).

### 0.5.3 Other Drugs for ADHD
- **Atomoxetine** — a **selective noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor**; non-stimulant, **not a controlled drug**, and useful where there is tic disorder, anxiety, or a substance misuse concern. **Takes weeks to work** (unlike stimulants), and carries warnings for **suicidal ideation in young people** and **hepatotoxicity**.
- **Alpha-2 agonists — guanfacine and clonidine** (extended-release) — useful for hyperactivity, impulsivity, tics and sleep; cause sedation, hypotension and bradycardia, and **must not be stopped abruptly** (rebound hypertension).
- **Non-drug management matters and is not optional:** behavioural and parenting interventions, educational support and accommodations, sleep, exercise, and treatment of the very common comorbidities — **anxiety, depression, learning disorders, autism and substance use.** See [[14_07_Psych_-_Attention_Deficit_Hyperactivity_Disorder]].

## 0.6 Drugs for Alcohol Dependence

- **Withdrawal management:** **benzodiazepines are the mainstay** — usually **diazepam**, symptom-triggered against a validated withdrawal scale, or **oxazepam** where there is significant liver impairment (no active metabolites).

> [!danger] **THIAMINE BEFORE GLUCOSE, AND PARENTERAL THIAMINE FOR ANYONE AT RISK.**
> **Give high-dose parenteral thiamine before or with any glucose-containing fluid** — a carbohydrate load in a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate **Wernicke encephalopathy**, which becomes irreversible Korsakoff syndrome. **Oral thiamine is inadequate for treatment.** **Do not delay glucose in a hypoglycaemic patient** — give both. See `NEW_Drugs_07_Blood_and_Electrolytes.md` 0.4.2.
> **Also: severe withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium tremens, which has a real mortality** — assess severity, monitor, and treat adequately rather than cautiously. **Check and replace magnesium, potassium and phosphate**, and watch for refeeding syndrome.

- **Relapse prevention — three agents, and they are under-prescribed:**
  - **Naltrexone** — an opioid antagonist reducing craving and heavy drinking. **Contraindicated with opioid use (precipitates withdrawal) and in acute hepatitis or liver failure.**
  - **Acamprosate** — modulates glutamate/GABA; supports abstinence. Renally cleared; well tolerated; requires frequent dosing.
  - **Disulfiram** — an aldehyde dehydrogenase inhibitor causing an intensely unpleasant reaction with alcohol (flushing, vomiting, tachycardia, hypotension). **Requires supervision and full informed consent; contraindicated in cardiovascular disease and psychosis.** **Warn about hidden alcohol in mouthwashes, foods and medicines.**
- **And the rest of care:** **brief interventions work**, psychosocial support and structured programs, treatment of comorbid depression and anxiety, hepatitis B vaccination and blood-borne virus screening, and attention to nutrition and social circumstances.

## 0.7 Drugs for Nicotine Dependence

- **Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT)** — patch (for background craving) **plus** a rapid-acting form (gum, lozenge, inhalator, mouth spray) for breakthrough craving. **COMBINATION NRT is more effective than a single form and is under-used** — and patients routinely under-dose. **Available over the counter and, for some products, on the PBS in Australia.**
- **Varenicline** — a **partial α4β2 nicotinic agonist**: it reduces craving and blunts the reward of smoking. **The most effective single agent.** Adverse effects: **nausea (common), vivid or abnormal dreams, insomnia, and headache**; earlier neuropsychiatric concerns have been substantially allayed by subsequent trial evidence, but **mood should still be monitored**, particularly in those with psychiatric illness. **Australian supply has been intermittent — check availability.**
- **Bupropion** — an NDRI (see 0.1.5), moderately effective; **contraindicated in epilepsy and eating disorders.**
- **Nicotine vaping products** — in Australia, **regulated as therapeutic goods with a prescription-and-pharmacy model**, positioned as a **second-line aid for smokers who have failed other approaches**, and **not for people who do not already smoke, and not for adolescents**. **The policy position has changed repeatedly — check the current regulatory arrangements rather than relying on this note.**

> [!danger] **SMOKING CESSATION IS THE HIGHEST-VALUE INTERVENTION IN MEDICINE, AND IT IS A DRUG-INTERACTION EVENT.**
> **Tobacco smoke (not nicotine) induces CYP1A2.** **When a patient stops smoking — including on admission to a smoke-free hospital — levels of CLOZAPINE, OLANZAPINE, THEOPHYLLINE and some others RISE, and toxicity can follow within days.** **Ask about smoking status changes and review these drugs.**
> **Every admission is an opportunity: offer NRT to every smoker on admission** — it treats withdrawal, improves the admission, and doubles quit rates. **Brief advice plus pharmacotherapy plus referral to Quitline is the evidence-based package.**

## 0.8 Other Psychotropic Drugs

- **Drugs for opioid dependence** — methadone, buprenorphine (including long-acting injectable) and naltrexone; see `NEW_Drugs_03_Analgesics.md` 0.3, including the standing rules about never omitting a patient's opioid agonist dose and providing analgesia in addition to it.
- **Drugs for methamphetamine and stimulant use disorder** — no approved pharmacotherapy; management is psychosocial, with symptomatic treatment of agitation (**benzodiazepines first-line, not antipsychotics**) and of complications.
- **Cognitive enhancers and drugs for dementia** — see `NEW_Drugs_15_Neurological.md` 0.3.
- **Melatonin, and drugs for circadian rhythm disorders** — 0.3.3.
- **Drugs used in eating disorders** — **fluoxetine** in bulimia nervosa; **there is no effective pharmacotherapy for the core features of anorexia nervosa**, where **refeeding with careful monitoring for refeeding syndrome** and psychological therapy are the treatment. **Beware QT prolongation and electrolyte disturbance** in this group. See [[14_05a_Psych_-_Eating_Disorders]].
- **Rapid tranquillisation for acute severe agitation** — **de-escalation and environmental measures first**; then oral medication offered before parenteral; typically a **benzodiazepine and/or an antipsychotic** to local protocol, with **monitoring of consciousness, airway, oxygen saturation, blood pressure and temperature afterwards.** **This is a high-risk intervention** — deaths have occurred — and it must be documented, justified, debriefed with the patient afterwards, and conducted within the relevant **mental health legislation**. See [[14_06b_Psych_-_Mental_Health_Act_and_Sectioning]].
- **Physical health in severe mental illness** — the single largest unmet need in this section. **Metabolic monitoring, cardiovascular risk management, cancer screening, dental care and smoking cessation** are all systematically under-delivered to people with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, and are a direct cause of the mortality gap.
