## Acute Chest Pain

- **DDx:** structure this as **"the six that kill" first, then the rest** — the intern's task is not to reach the diagnosis but to exclude the lethal causes before accepting a benign one.
  - **Immediately life-threatening:** **acute coronary syndrome**; **pulmonary embolism**; **aortic dissection**; **tension pneumothorax**; **oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave)**; **cardiac tamponade**.
  - **Cardiac, other:** myocarditis, pericarditis, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, takotsubo cardiomyopathy.
  - **Respiratory:** pneumonia with pleurisy, simple pneumothorax, pleural effusion, malignancy.
  - **GI:** gastro-oesophageal reflux, oesophageal spasm, peptic ulcer, biliary colic, pancreatitis.
  - **Musculoskeletal/neuro:** costochondritis, rib fracture, muscular strain, **herpes zoster** (pain precedes the rash — a recognised early misdiagnosis), cervical radiculopathy.
  - **Psychological:** panic disorder — a diagnosis of exclusion in the acute setting, never a first-pass explanation in a patient with risk factors.
- **Focused Hx:** **SOCRATES**, with the discriminating features: **onset** (instantaneous and maximal at onset suggests dissection or PE; crescendo over minutes suggests ACS); **character** (tearing → dissection; heavy/crushing/band-like → ischaemia; sharp and worse on inspiration → pleuritic; burning → reflux, though this overlaps with ischaemia far more than students expect); **radiation** (jaw, neck, either arm → ischaemia; interscapular/back → dissection); **exertional relationship**; **relief by rest or nitrates** (unreliable — GTN relieves oesophageal spasm too, and this is a classic false-reassurance trap); **positional and respiratory variation** (relieved by sitting forward → pericarditis). Then: **cardiovascular risk factors** (age, sex, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, family history of premature CAD, previous events and interventions); **PE risk** (immobility, recent surgery, malignancy, previous VTE, pregnancy or postpartum, oestrogen, long-haul travel, leg symptoms); **dissection risk** (hypertension, Marfan or other connective tissue disease, bicuspid aortic valve, pregnancy, cocaine); **cocaine and amphetamine use**, asked directly; associated **dyspnoea, syncope, sweating, nausea, vomiting**.
- **Examination:** **A–E, with vital signs including bilateral blood pressures and SpO₂.** Look for: pallor, sweating, distress; **blood pressure difference between arms and unequal or absent pulses** (dissection); **JVP elevation with hypotension and muffled heart sounds** (tamponade, Beck's triad); **tracheal deviation with absent breath sounds and hyperresonance** (tension pneumothorax — a clinical diagnosis, decompressed before imaging); pericardial rub; murmurs, particularly a new early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation (dissection into the root); **crackles and a third heart sound** (failure); unilateral leg swelling or calf tenderness; chest wall tenderness that **faithfully reproduces the presenting pain** (helpful but never sufficient to exclude ACS on its own); dermatomal vesicles.
- **First-line Ix:** **12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of arrival — this is the single time-critical action** and should precede everything except resuscitation. Repeat it if pain recurs or the first is non-diagnostic, and compare with any previous ECG.
  - **Troponin**, on the local pathway, with the timing of the repeat sample determined by the assay in use.
  - **Chest X-ray** — pneumothorax, consolidation, effusion, a widened mediastinum (insensitive for dissection; a normal film does not exclude it).
  - **Bloods:** FBC, UEC, glucose, LFTs, lipase if GI cause is possible, coagulation profile.
  - **D-dimer only within a validated risk-stratification pathway** — ordering it in a high-probability patient delays definitive imaging, and ordering it indiscriminately generates unnecessary CTPA. This is a common intern error in both directions.
  - **CT pulmonary angiogram** for suspected PE; **CT aortogram** for suspected dissection; **bedside echocardiography** for tamponade or regional wall motion abnormality.
  - **β-hCG** in any woman of reproductive age before imaging.

> [!danger] **The two errors that cause real harm here.** First, **anchoring on a benign cause because the patient is young or the pain is atypical** — women, people with diabetes and older patients present atypically with ACS, and "atypical" is a description of the pain, not evidence against the diagnosis. Second, **giving anticoagulation or thrombolysis before considering dissection** — the treatment for one lethal cause is catastrophic in another, which is why bilateral blood pressures and a dissection-focused history are part of the *initial* assessment, not a later refinement.

## Chronic Chest Pain

- **DDx:** stable angina; **refractory or microvascular angina**; oesophageal disease (reflux, spasm, oesophagitis); musculoskeletal chest wall pain (much the commonest in primary care); anxiety and panic disorder; pericardial disease; valvular disease, particularly aortic stenosis; pulmonary hypertension; malignancy involving chest wall, pleura or mediastinum; chronic pancreatitis or biliary disease referred to the chest; post-herpetic neuralgia; **chronic pain syndrome after a previous cardiac event or surgery**.
- **Focused Hx:** the discriminator is **reproducibility with exertion and relief with rest** — the defining pair for stable angina. Quantify **exercise tolerance in functional terms** (flights of stairs, metres on the flat, whether it has changed and over what timescale), because a *falling* threshold converts stable angina into a crescendo pattern requiring urgent assessment. Ask about relationship to meals, posture, swallowing, and emotional stress; nocturnal symptoms; associated dyspnoea, syncope or presyncope on exertion (**exertional syncope with a murmur is aortic stenosis until proven otherwise and warrants urgent, not routine, assessment**); full cardiovascular risk profile; previous investigations and their results; psychosocial context and what the patient believes is happening.
- **Examination:** cardiovascular examination with attention to **murmurs (aortic stenosis), signs of heart failure, blood pressure, peripheral pulses and bruits**; BMI; chest wall palpation for reproducible tenderness; respiratory examination; abdominal examination for epigastric tenderness; signs of hyperlipidaemia (xanthelasma, tendon xanthomata, corneal arcus in a younger patient).
- **First-line Ix:** **resting 12-lead ECG** (often normal in stable angina — a normal ECG does not exclude it); **FBC** (anaemia as a precipitant), **UEC**, **fasting lipids**, **HbA1c or fasting glucose**, **TFTs**; **chest X-ray** where a respiratory or structural cause is possible; **absolute cardiovascular risk calculation** using an Australian risk calculator; then **functional or anatomical cardiac testing** — exercise stress testing, stress echocardiography, myocardial perfusion imaging, or CT coronary angiography — selected with the local pathway rather than by the intern alone. **Trial of a proton pump inhibitor** where reflux is genuinely the leading hypothesis, remembering that a response does not exclude ischaemia.

## Chest Tightness

- **DDx:** the term is used by patients for both **cardiac pain** and **bronchoconstriction**, and the first job is to work out which. Consider: ACS and stable angina; **asthma and COPD** (tightness is a recognised asthma symptom and may be the only one); anxiety and panic with hyperventilation; pulmonary embolism; heart failure; anaemia; reflux; vocal cord dysfunction.
- **Focused Hx:** ask the patient to **describe it in their own words and point to it with one hand or the flat of the hand** — a clenched fist over the sternum (Levine's sign) suggests ischaemia, a finger point suggests a non-cardiac focal cause. Relationship to **exertion versus to allergen, cold air, night-time or a respiratory infection**; wheeze; cough; personal or family history of atopy; inhaler use and response; cardiovascular risk factors; anxiety symptoms and precipitants; caffeine and stimulant use.
- **Examination:** vital signs including **SpO₂ and respiratory rate**; work of breathing and ability to speak in full sentences; auscultation for wheeze (and **a silent chest as the ominous finding**, not a reassuring one); cardiovascular examination; peak expiratory flow where asthma is plausible.
- **First-line Ix:** **ECG**; **peak flow or spirometry** (with reversibility testing where asthma is suspected and the patient is stable); **chest X-ray**; **FBC**; troponin if the history admits an ischaemic cause. Where both cardiac and respiratory causes are genuinely possible, **investigate both in parallel rather than sequentially** — the common error is committing to one system early.

## Pleuritic Chest Pain

- **DDx:** **pulmonary embolism** (the one to exclude first); pneumonia; pneumothorax; pleural effusion and empyema; **pericarditis** (pleuritic and positional together); rib fracture or chest wall injury; costochondritis; malignancy involving the pleura, including mesothelioma; **herpes zoster**; connective tissue disease serositis (SLE, rheumatoid); subdiaphragmatic causes irritating the diaphragm (subphrenic abscess, cholecystitis, pancreatitis).
- **Focused Hx:** confirm the pain is genuinely **worse on inspiration, coughing or movement**; onset and progression; fever, cough, sputum, haemoptysis; dyspnoea; **VTE risk factors in full** (immobility, surgery within weeks, malignancy and its treatment, previous VTE, pregnancy or postpartum, oestrogen-containing contraception or HRT, long-haul travel, thrombophilia); leg swelling or pain; trauma, including minor trauma or vigorous coughing in an older or osteoporotic patient; recent viral illness; connective tissue disease symptoms; occupational asbestos exposure where mesothelioma is a consideration.
- **Examination:** vital signs with **respiratory rate and SpO₂**, and **heart rate** (tachycardia is the commonest abnormal sign in PE); chest expansion; percussion note; auscultation for **pleural rub**, reduced breath sounds, bronchial breathing, crackles; chest wall palpation for focal bony tenderness; **calf examination**; JVP and signs of right heart strain; **pericardial rub with the patient sitting forward**; skin for a dermatomal rash.
- **First-line Ix:** **ECG** (sinus tachycardia commonest in PE; look for right heart strain and for the widespread saddle-shaped ST elevation with PR depression of pericarditis); **chest X-ray**; **FBC, CRP, UEC**; **arterial or venous blood gas** if hypoxic or unwell; **D-dimer only within a validated pretest-probability pathway**; **CTPA** where PE probability is high or the pathway directs it; **bedside ultrasound or formal imaging** for effusion; **β-hCG** where relevant, since it changes imaging choice in pregnancy.

## Bradycardia and Symptomatic Bradycardia

> Built as one entry because the assessment is identical and the *only* thing that separates them is the presence of symptoms and adverse signs — which is exactly the judgement being taught. Both list topics are covered here.

- **D:** heart rate **below 60 beats per minute**. **Bradycardia is a rate, not a diagnosis, and does not by itself require treatment** — an athlete at 45 bpm and a patient in complete heart block at 38 bpm with syncope are entirely different problems.
- **DDx:**
  - **Physiological:** athletic training, sleep, high vagal tone.
  - **Conduction disease:** sinus node dysfunction/sick sinus syndrome, first-, second- (Mobitz I and II) and third-degree AV block, atrial fibrillation with slow ventricular response.
  - **Drugs — check the chart before anything else:** **beta-blockers** (including eye drops, which are genuinely missed), **non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem)**, **digoxin**, amiodarone and other antiarrhythmics, ivabradine, clonidine, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, opioids, sedatives.
  - **Metabolic/systemic:** **hyperkalaemia** (a rapidly lethal and reversible cause — check it early), hypothyroidism/myxoedema, hypothermia, hypoxia, **raised intracranial pressure with hypertension and irregular breathing (Cushing's reflex)**, obstructive sleep apnoea.
  - **Cardiac:** **inferior myocardial infarction** (the right coronary artery usually supplies the SA and AV nodes — bradycardia with inferior ST elevation is a recognised pairing), myocarditis, infiltrative disease, post-cardiac-surgery, endocarditis with aortic root abscess.
  - **Infective:** Lyme carditis (relevant in returned travellers, not acquired in Australia).
- **Focused Hx:** **symptoms are the whole question** — syncope, presyncope, dizziness, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, dyspnoea, chest pain, confusion. Establish whether symptoms **correlate in time with the slow rate**. Then: full drug history including recent changes and doses, overdose or deliberate self-poisoning, comorbidity (thyroid, renal, cardiac), previous ECGs and any known conduction disease, pacemaker or ICD in situ.
- **Examination:** **the assessment is for adverse features**, which convert bradycardia from an observation into an emergency: **shock** (hypotension, pallor, sweating, cold peripheries, confusion), **syncope**, **myocardial ischaemia**, **heart failure**. Also: rate and rhythm regularity, blood pressure, **cannon A waves in the JVP and variable-intensity first heart sound in complete heart block**, signs of hypothyroidism, temperature, conscious state, pacemaker scar.
- **First-line Ix:** **12-lead ECG** — the diagnostic test, identifying the block and its level; **continuous cardiac monitoring** for any symptomatic or high-grade block; **UEC with potassium**, **calcium and magnesium**, **TFTs**, **troponin**, **digoxin level** where relevant, **FBC**, **glucose**; **chest X-ray**; **blood gas** if unwell. **Withhold the culprit drug** as an early, reversible intervention — often the most useful thing an intern does here.

> [!warning] **The escalation point.** Bradycardia **with any adverse feature** (shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia, heart failure) or with **high-grade block carrying a risk of asystole** — Mobitz II, complete heart block, a recent asystolic pause, or a ventricular pause of significant length — requires **immediate senior help and cardiac monitoring**, not a routine referral. **Specific drug doses and pacing thresholds are deliberately not stated here**: they belong to the ANZCOR/local resuscitation algorithm, which is egress-blocked in this environment and must not be reproduced from memory for a time-critical intervention.

## Tachycardia

- **D:** heart rate **above 100 beats per minute**. As with bradycardia, the rate is the finding; the questions are **regular or irregular, narrow or broad, and is the patient stable**.
- **DDx:** organise by the ECG, which is how it is actually done:
  - **Narrow-complex regular:** sinus tachycardia (**overwhelmingly the commonest, and always secondary — find the cause**), atrial flutter with fixed block, AVNRT, AVRT, atrial tachycardia.
  - **Narrow-complex irregular:** **atrial fibrillation**, atrial flutter with variable block, **multifocal atrial tachycardia** (below).
  - **Broad-complex regular:** **ventricular tachycardia — assume VT until proven otherwise in any patient with structural heart disease or ischaemic history**, SVT with aberrancy or bundle branch block, antidromic AVRT, paced rhythm.
  - **Broad-complex irregular:** AF with aberrancy, **AF with pre-excitation (WPW)**, **polymorphic VT/torsades de pointes**.
  - **Secondary causes driving sinus tachycardia — the list an intern must run through:** pain, anxiety, fever and **sepsis**, hypovolaemia and haemorrhage, **hypoxia**, **pulmonary embolism**, anaemia, **thyrotoxicosis**, hypoglycaemia, alcohol withdrawal, stimulants (caffeine, cocaine, amphetamines, salbutamol, pseudoephedrine), anticholinergic and sympathomimetic toxidromes, pregnancy, phaeochromocytoma.
- **Focused Hx:** onset (**abrupt start and stop suggests a re-entrant SVT; gradual suggests sinus**), duration, previous episodes and what terminated them, palpitations, chest pain, dyspnoea, syncope or presyncope, fever and infective symptoms, blood loss, fluid intake and output, drugs including stimulants and thyroid medication, alcohol and withdrawal, caffeine, known cardiac disease, family history of sudden cardiac death or inherited arrhythmia syndromes.
- **Examination:** **A–E with a focus on haemodynamic stability** — blood pressure, conscious state, perfusion, evidence of heart failure or ischaemia. Rhythm regularity at the pulse; temperature; volume status (JVP, mucous membranes, postural BP); signs of sepsis, thyrotoxicosis (tremor, goitre, lid lag, warm moist skin), anaemia, or blood loss including a rectal examination where GI bleeding is possible.
- **First-line Ix:** **12-lead ECG during the tachycardia** — capture it before terminating the rhythm wherever safe, since a rhythm strip taken only after cardioversion loses the diagnosis; **continuous monitoring**; **FBC**, **UEC with potassium and magnesium**, **calcium**, **TFTs**, **glucose**, **troponin**, **CRP and cultures** if septic, **β-hCG**; **chest X-ray**; **blood gas with lactate**. Treat the **cause** in sinus tachycardia — rate-controlling a compensatory sinus tachycardia in a hypovolaemic or septic patient removes the compensation and is actively dangerous.

## Multifocal Atrial Tachycardia

- **D:** an **irregularly irregular narrow-complex tachycardia** arising from multiple atrial foci. It is worth knowing specifically because it is **routinely misdiagnosed as atrial fibrillation**, and the management differs — MAT is treated by treating the underlying illness, not by rate-controlling and anticoagulating on an AF pathway.
- **ECG criteria (three independent sources agreed):** atrial rate **>100 bpm**; **at least 3 distinct P-wave morphologies in the same lead**; **irregular P–P, P–R and R–R intervals**; and an **isoelectric baseline between P waves** — the feature that separates it from atrial flutter, and the presence of discrete P waves at all is what separates it from AF.
- **DDx/associations:** **severe COPD is the classic setting**, and MAT often signals a decompensation; also congestive heart failure, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, hypoxia of any cause, theophylline toxicity, and electrolyte disturbance — particularly **hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia**.
- **Focused Hx:** respiratory history and current exacerbation, oxygen and bronchodilator use, **theophylline** if prescribed, cardiac history, palpitations, symptoms of PE or sepsis, diuretic use (driving potassium and magnesium loss).
- **Examination:** irregularly irregular pulse; respiratory examination for evidence of COPD exacerbation (wheeze, prolonged expiration, accessory muscle use, hyperinflation); **SpO₂ and work of breathing**; signs of right heart failure and cor pulmonale; volume status; signs of sepsis.
- **First-line Ix:** **12-lead ECG** (and a long rhythm strip — three P morphologies may not be visible on a single short lead); **arterial or venous blood gas** for hypoxia and CO₂ retention; **UEC, magnesium, calcium**; **theophylline level** if prescribed; **FBC, CRP**; **chest X-ray**; **TFTs**; investigation for PE if the clinical picture suggests it. **The treatment is of the underlying respiratory or systemic illness** — correcting hypoxia and electrolytes — rather than of the rhythm itself.

## Presyncope

- **D:** the sensation of impending loss of consciousness without actually losing it. **It carries the same differential and the same risk stratification as syncope** — treating it as a lesser complaint is the error, because the underlying cause may be identical and the patient simply did not progress to collapse on that occasion.
- **DDx:**
  - **Cardiac — the group that must be excluded:** arrhythmia (brady- or tachyarrhythmia), **structural obstruction (aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pulmonary embolism, cardiac tamponade)**, myocardial ischaemia.
  - **Orthostatic:** hypovolaemia, haemorrhage (including occult GI bleeding), **drug-induced** (antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers, nitrates, antidepressants, antipsychotics, opioids), autonomic failure (Parkinson's disease, diabetic autonomic neuropathy), adrenal insufficiency, prolonged bed rest, dehydration.
  - **Reflex/neurally mediated:** vasovagal (prolonged standing, heat, pain, emotion, venepuncture), situational (micturition, defaecation, cough, swallow), carotid sinus hypersensitivity.
  - **Non-syncopal mimics:** hypoglycaemia, anaemia, vestibular disorders, anxiety and hyperventilation, medication effect, posterior circulation ischaemia (rarely, and essentially never in isolation).
- **Focused Hx:** **the circumstances are the diagnosis.** Position at onset (**during exertion or lying flat points to a cardiac cause; on standing points to orthostatic; standing still in a hot room points to vasovagal**); prodrome (nausea, warmth, sweating, tunnel vision → vasovagal; **none at all, or palpitations immediately before → cardiac**); duration; what aborted it; **exertional symptoms specifically asked about**; associated chest pain or dyspnoea; frequency and pattern; drug history with attention to recent changes and to QT-prolonging agents; **family history of sudden cardiac death, drowning, or unexplained death under 50**; alcohol; oral intake and losses; melaena or haematemesis; pregnancy.
- **Examination:** **lying and standing blood pressure, measured properly** — after lying for several minutes, then at intervals after standing; heart rate and rhythm; **murmurs, particularly the ejection systolic murmur of aortic stenosis and the dynamic murmur of HCM**; signs of heart failure; volume status; pallor; **neurological examination**; **rectal examination for melaena where anaemia or bleeding is possible**; capillary glucose.
- **First-line Ix:** **12-lead ECG in every patient** — looking for conduction disease, pre-excitation, **long or short QT**, Brugada pattern, ventricular hypertrophy, prior infarction and arrhythmogenic right ventricular changes; **FBC** (anaemia); **UEC, calcium, magnesium, glucose**; **β-hCG**; **troponin** where ischaemia is possible; **echocardiography** where a murmur, abnormal ECG or exertional symptoms are present; **ambulatory ECG monitoring** chosen to match symptom frequency; **postural blood pressure recorded as an observation**, not just performed. **Faecal occult blood or direct testing for GI loss** where iron-deficiency anaemia is found.

> [!danger] **Red flags making this cardiac until proven otherwise:** presyncope or syncope **on exertion** or **when supine**; **no prodrome**; **preceding palpitations**; **known structural heart disease or previous myocardial infarction**; **an abnormal ECG**; **a family history of sudden cardiac death**. Any of these warrants urgent assessment rather than reassurance and discharge.

## Hypotension

- **D:** a blood pressure low **for that patient** — the absolute number matters less than the trend, the perfusion, and the patient's baseline. A systolic of 95 mmHg is normal in a young woman and shock in a chronic hypertensive.
- **DDx — by shock phenotype**, which is the framework that makes this manageable at the bedside:
  - **Hypovolaemic:** haemorrhage (trauma, GI bleeding, ruptured AAA, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, retroperitoneal bleed on anticoagulation), and non-haemorrhagic fluid loss (vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, DKA/HHS, third-spacing in pancreatitis or obstruction).
  - **Distributive:** **sepsis** (commonest), **anaphylaxis**, neurogenic shock from spinal cord injury, adrenal crisis, liver failure, drug overdose with vasodilatation.
  - **Cardiogenic:** myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, acute valvular failure, myocarditis, decompensated cardiomyopathy, drug toxicity (beta-blocker, calcium channel blocker).
  - **Obstructive:** **tension pneumothorax**, **cardiac tamponade**, **massive pulmonary embolism**, dynamic hyperinflation in a ventilated asthmatic.
  - **Non-shock hypotension:** drugs (antihypertensives, diuretics, opioids, sedatives, anaesthetic agents), autonomic dysfunction, prolonged immobility, endocrine causes.
- **Focused Hx:** speed of onset; **bleeding from any site — haematemesis, melaena, PV bleeding, trauma, recent procedure**; fever and infective symptoms; **allergen exposure with rash, wheeze or angioedema**; chest pain; dyspnoea; drug and fluid chart review including **recent doses of antihypertensives, opioids and sedatives**; anticoagulation; comorbidity, particularly cardiac and adrenal; **steroid use, current or recently stopped** (adrenal crisis is missed precisely because the history is not taken); last oral intake and output; pregnancy — **a woman of reproductive age with hypotension and abdominal pain has a ruptured ectopic until excluded**.
- **Examination:** **A–E**, with **perfusion assessed rather than only the number**: conscious state, capillary refill, peripheral temperature and mottling, urine output. Then the phenotype-defining signs — **JVP (low in hypovolaemia and distributive; raised in cardiogenic, tamponade, tension pneumothorax and massive PE)**; **warm peripheries and bounding pulses in distributive shock versus cold, clamped peripheries in cardiogenic and hypovolaemic**; chest auscultation and percussion; heart sounds and murmurs; abdominal examination including a **pulsatile expansile mass**; rectal examination; skin for rash, urticaria, purpura or mottling; temperature; and a **full drug chart review as part of the examination, not after it**.
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose** and **ECG** immediately; **venous or arterial blood gas with lactate** — the fastest useful marker of tissue hypoperfusion; **FBC, UEC, LFTs, coagulation profile, calcium and magnesium**; **group and hold or crossmatch** if bleeding is possible; **blood cultures before antibiotics** in suspected sepsis, without delaying them; **troponin**; **β-hCG**; **cortisol** before steroids if adrenal crisis is genuinely suspected; **chest X-ray**; **bedside echocardiography and ultrasound** for tamponade, right heart strain, free fluid and aortic diameter; **CT** as directed once stabilised. **Urinary catheter and hourly urine output** where shock is established.

> [!info] **The single most useful bedside discriminator** is the combination of **JVP and peripheral temperature**: low JVP with cold peripheries → hypovolaemic; low JVP with warm peripheries → distributive; raised JVP with cold peripheries → cardiogenic or obstructive. It does not replace investigation, but it directs the first ten minutes correctly, which is what matters.

## Elevated Blood Pressure

- **D:** a raised reading, **which is not the same as a diagnosis of hypertension** — this entry is deliberately about the *reading*, because acting on a single office measurement is a common and consequential intern error.
- **DDx — why this reading is high:**
  - **Measurement artefact:** **cuff too small for the arm** (the commonest technical cause of spurious elevation), arm unsupported or below heart level, patient talking, legs crossed, recent caffeine, nicotine or exercise, full bladder, insufficient rest before measurement.
  - **White-coat effect** — genuinely common and only identifiable with out-of-office measurement.
  - **Acute reactive elevation:** **pain**, anxiety, urinary retention, alcohol withdrawal, hypoxia, hypercapnia, **raised intracranial pressure**, ischaemic stroke (where the elevation is often protective and lowering it can be harmful).
  - **Essential hypertension** — the diagnosis in the large majority once the above are excluded.
  - **Secondary hypertension — worth actively considering when the patient is young, the elevation is severe or abrupt, or it is resistant to treatment:** renal parenchymal disease, **renal artery stenosis**, **primary hyperaldosteronism** (increasingly recognised as underdiagnosed), phaeochromocytoma, Cushing's syndrome, thyroid disease, **obstructive sleep apnoea**, coarctation of the aorta, and **drugs — NSAIDs, corticosteroids, oestrogens, sympathomimetics and decongestants, venlafaxine, ciclosporin, liquorice, cocaine and amphetamines, and alcohol**.
- **Focused Hx:** previous readings and any prior diagnosis; symptoms of end-organ damage (**chest pain, dyspnoea, headache, visual change, focal neurology, haematuria, oedema**); symptoms suggesting a secondary cause (**episodic headache with sweating and palpitations → phaeochromocytoma; muscle weakness and polyuria → hyperaldosteronism with hypokalaemia; snoring, witnessed apnoeas and daytime somnolence → OSA**); **full drug history including over-the-counter and complementary medicines**; alcohol, salt, and stimulant intake; cardiovascular risk factors and family history; pregnancy status — **hypertension in pregnancy is a separate and urgent pathway**.
- **Examination:** **repeat the measurement yourself, with correct technique and an appropriately sized cuff**, after the patient has rested; measure **both arms** at first assessment; **lying and standing** where symptoms suggest it. Then: BMI and waist circumference; **fundoscopy for hypertensive retinopathy** where the elevation is severe; cardiovascular examination for apex displacement, added heart sounds, murmurs and **radio-femoral delay (coarctation)**; **abdominal examination for renal bruits and masses**; peripheral pulses; signs of Cushing's syndrome; thyroid examination; neurological examination if any focal symptom.
- **First-line Ix:** **out-of-office measurement — ambulatory or validated home monitoring — is the key investigation**, and is what distinguishes sustained hypertension from white-coat effect. Then, for end-organ assessment and secondary screening: **UEC and eGFR**, **urinalysis for blood and protein with urine albumin:creatinine ratio**, **fasting lipids**, **HbA1c or fasting glucose**, **FBC**, **TFTs**, **ECG** for left ventricular hypertrophy and prior ischaemia, and **absolute cardiovascular risk calculation** using an Australian calculator. Add **aldosterone:renin ratio** (with attention to interfering drugs), **plasma or urinary metanephrines**, renal imaging, and sleep studies where the history points that way.

## Hypertensive Urgency

- **D:** **severe blood pressure elevation *without* acute hypertension-mediated organ damage** — as distinct from a **hypertensive emergency**, which is severe elevation **with** acute organ damage. **The distinction is made by the presence or absence of organ damage, not by the height of the number**, and this is the entire clinical point of the entry.
- **The threshold:** severe elevation is commonly defined as **systolic >180 mmHg or diastolic >120 mmHg**. Three independent sources agreed on this figure. **However, no Australian primary source could be read to confirm Australian practice uses the same cut-off** — the figure derives from international material, and the reviewer should confirm it against the Heart Foundation's Australian hypertension guideline before treating it as the local standard.
- **DDx — establish which of the two you are dealing with, by looking for organ damage:**
  - **Hypertensive emergency (any of these present):** **hypertensive encephalopathy** (headache, confusion, seizures, visual loss), **acute stroke or intracranial haemorrhage**, **acute coronary syndrome**, **acute pulmonary oedema**, **aortic dissection**, **acute kidney injury**, **retinal haemorrhages, exudates or papilloedema**, **eclampsia or pre-eclampsia with severe features**, microangiopathic haemolysis.
  - **Hypertensive urgency:** none of the above, and the elevation is often chronic, under-treated or the result of non-adherence.
  - **Precipitants to identify:** **non-adherence or abrupt withdrawal of antihypertensives — particularly clonidine and beta-blockers, which cause rebound**; pain; anxiety; urinary retention; **stimulant use (cocaine, amphetamines)**; NSAIDs and corticosteroids; untreated OSA; phaeochromocytoma.
- **Focused Hx:** **direct questioning for each organ system:** headache, visual disturbance, confusion, seizure, focal weakness; chest pain; dyspnoea and orthopnoea; **tearing interscapular pain**; reduced urine output; nausea and vomiting; in pregnancy or postpartum, **epigastric pain and visual symptoms**. Then: **adherence — asked non-judgementally and specifically, including when the last dose was actually taken**; recent medication changes; drug and stimulant use; previous BP readings and known hypertension; pregnancy.
- **Examination:** **repeat the BP in both arms with correct technique**; **fundoscopy — this is the examination that most often distinguishes urgency from emergency at the bedside** and an intern should be able to perform it; full **neurological examination**; cardiovascular examination for heart failure, murmurs and **pulse deficit or inter-arm difference**; respiratory examination for pulmonary oedema; abdominal examination for bruits, masses and a **palpable bladder**; assessment of pain and distress as reversible drivers.
- **First-line Ix:** **ECG**; **UEC and eGFR** (comparing against previous results to identify *acute* kidney injury rather than chronic disease); **FBC with film if microangiopathy is suspected**; **urinalysis for blood and protein**; **troponin**; **chest X-ray**; **β-hCG**; **CT brain** where there are neurological symptoms; **CT aortogram** where dissection is possible; **fundoscopy documented as a finding**.

> [!warning] **The management principle, because getting it wrong causes harm:** in **hypertensive urgency**, blood pressure is lowered **gradually, usually with oral therapy over days**, in the outpatient or ward setting — **rapid lowering risks cerebral, coronary and renal hypoperfusion**, and giving a short-acting agent to "bring the number down" before discharge is a recognised cause of iatrogenic ischaemic injury. In a **hypertensive emergency**, controlled reduction with **intravenous therapy in a monitored setting** is required, with the target and rate depending on the specific organ involved — and notably, **acute ischaemic stroke is an exception where permissive hypertension is usually appropriate**. **Specific agents, targets and rates of reduction are deliberately omitted**: they are organ-specific, they differ between Australian and international guidance, and no primary source was reachable to confirm them.

## Paroxysmal Hypertension

- **D:** **episodic**, often abrupt, elevations of blood pressure returning toward baseline between episodes — as opposed to sustained hypertension. The reason to know it as a distinct presentation is that it carries a **specific and dangerous differential** which sustained hypertension does not.
- **DDx:**
  - **Phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma** — the classic and the one that must be considered, though it accounts for a small minority of cases. The **triad of episodic headache, sweating and palpitations** with hypertension is the taught association.
  - **Drugs and withdrawal:** **cocaine, amphetamines, other sympathomimetics**; **abrupt clonidine or beta-blocker withdrawal**; **MAOI–tyramine interaction**; decongestants; **alcohol withdrawal**; serotonin syndrome.
  - **Autonomic:** **autonomic dysreflexia in spinal cord injury above roughly the mid-thoracic level** — triggered by bladder distension or constipation, and a genuine emergency that an intern should recognise, because the treatment is to remove the trigger.
  - **Neurological:** raised intracranial pressure, seizures, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome.
  - **Endocrine:** thyrotoxicosis, carcinoid, hyperaldosteronism (usually sustained), hypoglycaemia with sympathetic response.
  - **Anxiety and panic disorder**, and **"pseudophaeochromocytoma"** — genuine paroxysmal hypertension with symptoms but no catecholamine-secreting tumour, which is more common than phaeochromocytoma itself.
  - **Baroreflex failure**; obstructive sleep apnoea with nocturnal surges; **white-coat and measurement artefact**.
- **Focused Hx:** **characterise the episode**: frequency, duration, abruptness of onset and offset, triggers (posture, exertion, micturition, palpation of the abdomen, certain foods, drug doses); the **symptom triad — headache, sweating, palpitations** — asked about specifically; pallor or flushing during episodes; anxiety and its temporal relationship to the BP rise (**which comes first matters**); tremor, weight loss, heat intolerance; **spinal cord injury and bowel/bladder history**; full drug and substance history including recently ceased medications; family history of phaeochromocytoma, **MEN2, von Hippel–Lindau or neurofibromatosis type 1**.
- **Examination:** **BP during an episode if at all possible** — the observation that makes the diagnosis; heart rate and rhythm; **postural BP**; pallor, flushing, sweating, tremor; thyroid examination; abdominal examination for a mass, and for **bladder distension or faecal loading in a patient with spinal injury**; neurocutaneous stigmata (**café-au-lait macules and neurofibromas**); fundoscopy; signs of anxiety.
- **First-line Ix:** **plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines** — the screening test for phaeochromocytoma, and one where **collection conditions and interfering drugs (tricyclics, SNRIs, labetalol, paracetamol, and caffeine among others) materially affect the result**, so check the local laboratory's requirements before sending it. Then **ambulatory BP monitoring** to characterise the pattern objectively; **UEC and potassium**; **TFTs**; **glucose**; **ECG**; **urine drug screen** where stimulant use is plausible; imaging of the adrenals and abdomen **only after biochemical confirmation**, not as the first step — incidental adrenal nodules are common and imaging first generates confusion rather than diagnosis.

## Orthopnoea and Paroxysmal Nocturnal Dyspnoea

> Built as one entry: both are **positional dyspnoea symptoms elicited in the same history**, they share a differential, and separating them would duplicate the same content twice. Both list topics are covered here.

- **D:** **Orthopnoea** — breathlessness on lying flat, relieved by sitting up; quantified by **the number of pillows needed, and whether that number has changed**. **Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (PND)** — waking from sleep, typically **1–2 hours after lying down**, with severe breathlessness that forces the patient upright or to a window, resolving over some minutes. PND is the more specific of the two for heart failure.
- **A/P:** lying flat redistributes blood from the splanchnic and lower limb circulation into the thorax, raising pulmonary venous pressure; in a failing left ventricle unable to accommodate the increased preload, pulmonary congestion follows. The **delay before PND** reflects the time taken for interstitial fluid to accumulate. Understanding this is what makes the symptoms interpretable rather than memorised.
- **DDx:**
  - **Cardiac:** **left ventricular failure of any cause** — the principal diagnosis; mitral stenosis or regurgitation; constrictive pericarditis; large pericardial effusion.
  - **Respiratory:** **COPD and asthma** (nocturnal symptoms common; asthma classically worse in the early hours), **obstructive sleep apnoea** (choking arousals genuinely mimic PND and are frequently misattributed), large pleural effusions, diaphragmatic weakness or **bilateral phrenic nerve palsy** (orthopnoea that is severe and out of proportion), massive ascites, morbid obesity and obesity hypoventilation, large goitre or upper airway obstruction.
  - **Other:** gastro-oesophageal reflux with nocturnal aspiration, anxiety and nocturnal panic attacks, chronic kidney disease with fluid overload.
- **Focused Hx:** **how many pillows, and how many previously** — the change is the useful datum; how long the patient can lie flat before symptoms; **the timing after lying down and the time to relief for PND**; whether relief requires sitting, standing or opening a window; **associated symptoms: ankle swelling, weight gain, reduced exercise tolerance quantified in functional terms, chest pain, palpitations, cough (and whether frothy or pink-tinged sputum), wheeze**; **snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence** to catch OSA; reflux symptoms; cardiac history including previous infarction, valve disease and cardiomyopathy; risk factors; **medication adherence and recent changes, salt and fluid intake** — the commonest precipitants of decompensation and the ones an intern can act on; alcohol; renal disease.
- **Examination:** **volume status is the core** — **JVP**, peripheral and sacral oedema, weight compared with the recorded dry weight, hepatomegaly and hepatojugular reflux. Then **cardiovascular**: displaced apex, **third heart sound**, murmurs, blood pressure, heart rate and rhythm; **respiratory**: respiratory rate, SpO₂, bibasal crackles, wheeze ("cardiac asthma"), reduced air entry and stony dullness of effusions; **abdominal** for ascites; assessment of body habitus and neck circumference where OSA is suspected; and observe the patient's **actual posture and tolerance of lying flat during the examination**, which is often more informative than the history.
- **First-line Ix:** **ECG** (rarely normal in significant heart failure — a completely normal ECG makes it less likely); **chest X-ray** for cardiomegaly, upper lobe diversion, Kerley B lines, effusions and alveolar oedema; **BNP or NT-proBNP** where available, used to **rule out** rather than to confirm, and interpreted with awareness that obesity lowers it and age, renal impairment and AF raise it; **FBC** (anaemia as a precipitant), **UEC** (renal function and electrolytes before and during diuresis), **LFTs** (congestive hepatopathy), **TFTs**, **glucose/HbA1c**, **iron studies** (deficiency is common and treatable in heart failure), **troponin** if an ischaemic precipitant is possible; **echocardiography** — the definitive structural investigation, and the test that determines whether ejection fraction is reduced or preserved; **arterial or venous blood gas** if unwell or hypercapnia is suspected; **spirometry** and **sleep study** where the picture is respiratory rather than cardiac; **daily weights and a fluid balance chart**, which are investigations in their own right on the ward.

## Claudication

- **D:** **reproducible** muscle pain, cramping or fatigue brought on by **a consistent amount of exercise** and **relieved by rest within minutes without needing to change position** — the classic pattern of peripheral arterial disease. The site of pain indicates the level of disease: **buttock and hip → aorto-iliac; thigh → common femoral or aorto-iliac; calf (much the commonest) → superficial femoral or popliteal; foot → tibial or peroneal**.
- **DDx:**
  - **Vascular:** atherosclerotic PAD (overwhelmingly commonest); **acute limb ischaemia** if onset is sudden and pain is present at rest — a surgical emergency, not claudication; **popliteal artery entrapment** or **cystic adventitial disease** in a young patient with no risk factors, which is the situation where the diagnosis is most often missed; Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans) in a young heavy smoker; **abdominal aortic aneurysm** with embolic disease; vasculitis; **chronic venous disease** (aching, worse on standing, relieved by elevation — the opposite pattern).
  - **Neurogenic — the key mimic:** **lumbar spinal stenosis ("neurogenic claudication")**, where pain is **relieved by sitting or leaning forward rather than simply by stopping**, is often worse walking downhill and better walking uphill or leaning on a trolley, and where symptoms are less reproducible at a fixed distance. Also lumbar radiculopathy and peripheral neuropathy.
  - **Musculoskeletal:** osteoarthritis of hip or knee, muscular strain, **chronic exertional compartment syndrome** (young, athletic, tightness rather than cramp, slow resolution after exercise), medial tibial stress syndrome.
- **Focused Hx:** **claudication distance, and whether it is stable or shortening** — a shortening distance signals progression; whether it is genuinely reproducible at the same distance; **time to relief on stopping, and whether the patient must sit or bend forward** (the neurogenic discriminator); **rest pain — particularly pain in the forefoot at night relieved by hanging the leg out of bed**, which indicates critical limb ischaemia and changes urgency completely; **non-healing ulcers or gangrene**; erectile dysfunction (Leriche syndrome with aorto-iliac disease); **full cardiovascular risk profile — smoking above all, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, CKD, family history**; established atherosclerosis elsewhere (angina, previous MI, stroke or TIA); back and leg symptoms; and the **functional impact** — what the patient can no longer do.
- **Examination:** **inspect** both legs and **between the toes and on the heels** for ulceration, gangrene, pallor, dependent rubor, loss of hair, shiny atrophic skin, and thickened nails; **temperature** compared side to side; **capillary refill**; **palpate all pulses — femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis — and compare sides**, plus radial and carotid; **auscultate for femoral, iliac and carotid bruits**; **palpate the abdomen for an aneurysm**; **Buerger's test** (pallor on elevation with a low angle indicating severe disease, and dependent rubor on lowering); assess **sensation and motor function** — a cold, pale, pulseless, painful, paraesthetic, paralysed limb is **acute limb ischaemia** requiring immediate vascular referral; examine the **spine and hips** to test the mimics, including straight leg raise and hip range of movement.
- **First-line Ix:** **ankle–brachial index (ABI)** is the first-line bedside test. Three independent sources agreed on the interpretation: **normal 1.0–1.4; borderline 0.91–1.0; ≤0.9 diagnostic of peripheral arterial disease**. A value **above 1.4 indicates non-compressible, calcified vessels** — common in diabetes and CKD — and is **not reassuring**: it invalidates the test rather than excluding disease, and toe–brachial index or duplex should be used instead. **Exercise ABI** is useful where resting ABI is normal but the history is convincing.
  - Then: **duplex ultrasound** of the arterial tree; **CT or MR angiography** where intervention is contemplated; **FBC, UEC, HbA1c, fasting lipids, TFTs**; **ECG**; **absolute cardiovascular risk calculation**.
  - **Screen for atherosclerosis elsewhere** — the presence of PAD is a marker of systemic disease and of substantially increased cardiovascular mortality, and **risk-factor modification and secondary prevention are the main treatment**, not the leg symptoms alone. Roughly one in ten patients with a low ABI has classic claudication; most are asymptomatic or have atypical symptoms, so a normal history does not exclude the disease in a high-risk patient.
