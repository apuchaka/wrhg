## Acute Visual Loss

- **D:** loss of vision developing over seconds to days. The first three questions do almost all the diagnostic work: **is it painful or painless? one eye or both? and is the loss transient or persistent?**
- **DDx:**
  - **Sudden, painless, monocular — the classic emergency group:**
    - **Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO)** — sudden profound loss, a pale retina with a **cherry-red spot** at the macula, and a **relative afferent pupillary defect**. Treated as a stroke of the eye: it requires urgent ophthalmology involvement *and* urgent stroke work-up, because it carries the same embolic risk as a cerebral TIA.
    - **Giant cell arteritis with arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy** — **the diagnosis that must not be missed**, because untreated it blinds the other eye. Suspect in anyone over 50 with visual loss plus headache, scalp tenderness, **jaw claudication** (the most specific symptom), temporal artery tenderness, polymyalgic symptoms, fever or weight loss.
    - **Central retinal vein occlusion** — less abrupt, "blood and thunder" fundus with widespread haemorrhages.
    - **Retinal detachment** — preceded by **flashes and floaters**, then a curtain or shadow advancing across the field.
    - **Vitreous haemorrhage** — sudden floaters and haze, commonly in proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
    - **Non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy**; **occipital (cortical) stroke** — which causes a **homonymous field defect** and is often described by the patient as loss in one eye, so **always test the fields**.
  - **Sudden, painful:**
    - **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** — painful red eye, haloes around lights, nausea and vomiting, a hazy cornea and a fixed mid-dilated pupil; the eye feels hard.
    - **Optic neuritis** — pain on eye movement, reduced colour vision (**red desaturation**), a relative afferent pupillary defect, typically in a younger patient; associated with multiple sclerosis.
    - **Keratitis or corneal ulcer** (especially in contact lens wearers), **anterior uveitis**, **endophthalmitis** after intraocular surgery or injection, **chemical injury** or trauma.
  - **Transient:** **amaurosis fugax** (embolic — a TIA of the eye requiring the same urgency and work-up as any TIA), migraine with aura, papilloedema with transient visual obscurations, hypoperfusion.
  - **Gradual but noticed suddenly:** cataract, macular degeneration, chronic glaucoma, diabetic maculopathy, compressive lesion — commonly "discovered" when the good eye is covered.
- **Focused Hx:** **exact time and speed of onset**; **one eye or both — and whether the patient has actually covered each eye to check**; painful or painless; **pain on eye movement**; **flashes, floaters, or a curtain/shadow**; haloes around lights; **transient episodes preceding this one**; **headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, shoulder and hip girdle stiffness, fever, weight loss** — the GCA screen, asked in every patient over 50; **contact lens wear**; trauma or chemical exposure; recent eye surgery or intravitreal injection; **diabetes and its control, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, smoking, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke or TIA, known vasculitis**; medications including anticoagulants and **amiodarone, ethambutol and hydroxychloroquine**; neurological symptoms; and previous ophthalmic history.
- **Examination:**
  - **Visual acuity in each eye separately, with the patient's glasses or through a pinhole** — this is the vital sign of the eye and must be recorded, not estimated.
  - **Pupils, specifically testing for a relative afferent pupillary defect with the swinging-flashlight test** — an RAPD indicates optic nerve or extensive retinal disease and is the single most informative bedside sign here.
  - **Visual fields to confrontation in each eye** — to identify a homonymous defect indicating a retrochiasmal (cerebral) cause.
  - **Colour vision** (red desaturation) where optic neuritis is suspected.
  - **Eye movements** and whether they provoke pain.
  - **External examination** — redness and its pattern (ciliary flush), corneal clarity, discharge, proptosis, lid position.
  - **Fundoscopy**, dilated where safe and appropriate — **but do not dilate if angle-closure glaucoma is suspected**.
  - **Palpate the globe** for hardness and **palpate the temporal arteries** for tenderness, thickening or absent pulsation.
  - **Cardiovascular examination** — pulse for atrial fibrillation, blood pressure, carotid bruits.
  - **Fluorescein staining** where a corneal cause is possible; **intraocular pressure measurement** where glaucoma is suspected.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **ESR and CRP urgently, plus FBC and platelets**, in any patient over 50 with visual loss — **the GCA screen. Inflammatory markers can be normal in biopsy-proven GCA, so a normal result does not exclude it**, and treatment should not be withheld on that basis alone.
  - **Glucose/HbA1c, lipids, UEC, coagulation profile**; **ECG** for atrial fibrillation.
  - **Temporal artery ultrasound and/or temporal artery biopsy** where GCA is suspected — arranged urgently, **but never allowed to delay treatment**.
  - **Urgent CT or MRI brain** where a cortical cause, a compressive lesion or optic neuritis is suspected; **MRI with contrast** for optic neuritis and demyelination.
  - **Carotid duplex and echocardiography** for embolic causes, as for a TIA.
  - **Intraocular pressure**; **B-scan ultrasound** where the fundal view is obscured by vitreous haemorrhage.
  - **Same-day ophthalmology review** is itself the key "investigation" in most of this differential — formal slit-lamp examination, dilated fundoscopy and optical coherence tomography are not intern-level procedures, and the correct action is referral with an accurate acuity, pupil and field assessment already documented.

> [!danger] **Two things that cause avoidable permanent blindness in this presentation.** First, **not asking about jaw claudication and scalp tenderness in an older patient**, and not starting treatment while awaiting biopsy — in suspected GCA, **high-dose corticosteroid is started immediately on clinical suspicion, before biopsy, because the fellow eye is at risk within days**. *(The specific corticosteroid dose and route are deliberately omitted — they differ between guidelines and the Australian sources are egress-blocked. Escalate immediately; do not delay the discussion while looking up a dose.)* Second, **failing to check visual acuity and pupils** and referring an eye without them, which removes the information the ophthalmologist needs to triage.

## Acute Eye Pain

- **DDx — separate by whether the eye is red, and whether vision is affected:**
  - **Painful red eye with reduced vision — the sight-threatening group:** **acute angle-closure glaucoma** (severe pain, haloes, nausea and vomiting, hazy cornea, fixed mid-dilated pupil, hard globe), **keratitis and corneal ulcer** (especially **contact lens wearers — and any contact lens wearer with a red painful eye needs same-day ophthalmology review**), **anterior uveitis** (photophobia, ciliary flush, small irregular pupil, often with a systemic association such as ankylosing spondylitis or inflammatory bowel disease), **endophthalmitis** (after intraocular surgery or injection — an emergency), **scleritis** (severe boring pain that wakes the patient, often with connective tissue disease), **chemical injury** (**the one condition where treatment — copious irrigation — precedes assessment entirely**), **penetrating trauma**.
  - **Painful red eye with normal vision:** conjunctivitis (usually gritty rather than truly painful), episcleritis, subconjunctival haemorrhage (painless), blepharitis, foreign body, corneal abrasion, dry eye, **herpes zoster ophthalmicus** (dermatomal rash; **involvement of the nose tip — Hutchinson's sign — predicts ocular involvement**).
  - **Pain with a white eye:** **optic neuritis** (pain on eye movement), **giant cell arteritis**, migraine, cluster headache, sinusitis, trigeminal neuralgia, raised intracranial pressure, **orbital cellulitis** (pain on eye movement with proptosis, lid swelling, fever, and restricted movements — distinguishing it from preseptal cellulitis is the key clinical task, and it is an admission).
- **Focused Hx:** onset and severity; **vision change**; photophobia; discharge and its character; **contact lens wear, including sleeping in lenses and water exposure**; **trauma and chemical exposure, with the substance identified**; **recent eye surgery or intravitreal injection**; haloes; headache, nausea and vomiting; rash; systemic symptoms and known inflammatory or autoimmune disease; **jaw claudication and scalp tenderness** in the over-50s; previous episodes.
- **Examination:** **visual acuity each eye separately — first, and always**; pupils and **RAPD**; **pattern of redness** (diffuse conjunctival versus **ciliary flush** around the limbus, which points to intraocular inflammation); corneal clarity; **fluorescein staining under blue light** for abrasions, ulcers and **the dendritic ulcer of herpes simplex keratitis**; eye movements and whether they hurt; **proptosis and lid assessment**; globe tension by gentle palpation; **facial and scalp inspection for a zoster rash including the nose tip**; temporal artery palpation; and fundoscopy where safe.
- **First-line Ix:** largely **clinical and by slit lamp**. **Intraocular pressure measurement** where angle closure is suspected; **corneal scrape for microscopy and culture** in suspected microbial keratitis (an ophthalmology procedure); **ESR, CRP and FBC** in the over-50s where GCA is possible; **CT orbits** for suspected orbital cellulitis, orbital fracture or intraocular foreign body; **FBC, CRP, blood cultures** in orbital cellulitis; **HLA-B27, autoimmune screen and chest imaging** where recurrent uveitis prompts a systemic search — arranged, not urgent.

> [!danger] **Two actions that precede everything else.** **(1) Chemical injury: irrigate immediately, copiously and for a prolonged period, and check pH — do not delay to take a history or measure acuity.** Alkali injuries penetrate and are worse than acid. **(2) Do not dilate the pupil if acute angle-closure glaucoma is a possibility**, and do not pad an eye that may have an infected corneal ulcer. Contact-lens-related red eye is assumed to be microbial keratitis until an ophthalmologist says otherwise.

## Acute Visual Disturbance

> Distinct from **Acute Visual Loss** (Tier 1, above): this entry covers **distorted, double, obscured or transiently altered vision without necessarily losing it**, where the differential is broader and includes several neurological causes.

- **DDx:**
  - **Binocular diplopia (disappears when either eye is covered) — a cranial nerve or brainstem problem until proven otherwise:** **third nerve palsy — and a painful third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil is a posterior communicating artery aneurysm until excluded**; fourth and sixth nerve palsies (sixth is a false localising sign in raised intracranial pressure); **myasthenia gravis** (fatigable, variable, with ptosis); **thyroid eye disease**; orbital mass or fracture with muscle entrapment; **brainstem stroke**; multiple sclerosis; **Wernicke's encephalopathy**; cavernous sinus pathology.
  - **Monocular diplopia (persists with the other eye covered)** — almost always **ocular**: cataract, refractive error, corneal irregularity, dislocated lens, iris defect. Reassuring in that it is not neurological.
  - **Distortion and positive phenomena:** **metamorphopsia** (straight lines bent — macular pathology, especially **wet age-related macular degeneration**, which is time-critical for treatment), **photopsia** (flashes — vitreous traction, retinal tear or detachment), **floaters**, **scintillating scotoma with a marching edge** (migraine aura, typically evolving over 20–30 minutes and resolving), haloes (**angle closure or corneal oedema**).
  - **Field disturbance:** **homonymous hemianopia** (retrochiasmal — stroke or tumour), bitemporal hemianopia (**chiasmal compression — pituitary lesion**), altitudinal defect (ischaemic optic neuropathy or retinal vascular occlusion), arcuate defects (glaucoma).
  - **Transient:** **amaurosis fugax** (embolic — treat as a TIA), transient visual obscurations with papilloedema, migraine, hypoperfusion, **hypoglycaemia**.
  - **Toxic and metabolic:** **ethambutol, hydroxychloroquine, amiodarone, digoxin (xanthopsia), methanol**, vitamin A deficiency, hyperglycaemia causing refractive shift.
- **Focused Hx:** **one eye or both — and confirmed by the patient covering each eye**, because this single answer splits the differential; **constant or intermittent, and duration of episodes**; **diplopia: horizontal, vertical or oblique, and worse in which direction of gaze**; **fatigability and diurnal variation** (myasthenia); pain, especially **on eye movement**; **headache**; positive phenomena (flashes, floaters, zigzags) and their evolution over minutes; field loss and its shape; associated neurological symptoms; **vascular risk factors and AF**; medications; diabetes and recent glycaemic swings; alcohol and nutrition.
- **Examination:** **acuity each eye, pupils with the swinging-flashlight test, and visual fields to confrontation** — the same three that anchor every ophthalmic assessment; **colour vision**; **full eye movements in all directions, looking for the pattern of restriction, and for ptosis and fatigability on sustained upgaze**; **cover/uncover and alternate cover testing** where strabismus or skew is possible; **fundoscopy for papilloedema, macular changes and retinal vascular signs**; **cranial nerve examination**; blood pressure; and **an Amsler grid** where metamorphopsia is described.
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose**; **FBC, UEC, ESR and CRP** (GCA in the over-50s); **HbA1c and lipids**; **ECG** for AF; **urgent CT or CT angiography** for a painful third nerve palsy with pupil involvement, or for suspected stroke; **MRI brain and orbits** for suspected demyelination, chiasmal compression or brainstem lesion; **acetylcholine receptor antibodies and thyroid function** where myasthenia or thyroid eye disease is suspected; **carotid duplex and echocardiography** for embolic phenomena; **urgent ophthalmology and optical coherence tomography** where macular pathology is suspected.

## Acute Floaters

- **D:** the sudden appearance or increase of drifting shapes in the visual field. **Chronic, stable floaters are common and benign; a sudden change is the presentation that matters**, because it can signal a retinal tear before detachment occurs — the point at which treatment is simple and vision is preserved.
- **DDx:**
  - **Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD)** — the commonest cause, and usually benign; **but it is also the event that causes retinal tears**, so a first presentation needs examination rather than reassurance.
  - **Retinal tear and retinal detachment** — **the sight-threatening diagnosis**; suggested by **a sudden shower of floaters, flashes of light (photopsia), and a curtain, shadow or veil advancing across the field**, and by loss of central vision once the macula is involved.
  - **Vitreous haemorrhage** — sudden floaters, cobwebs or a red haze, sometimes with dense visual loss; commonly from **proliferative diabetic retinopathy**, retinal tear, or retinal vein occlusion.
  - **Posterior uveitis or vitritis** — floaters with pain, photophobia or systemic inflammatory disease; consider infection in the immunosuppressed (**toxoplasmosis, CMV retinitis**).
  - **Trauma**; **intraocular lymphoma** (rare, in older patients with persistent vitritis).
- **Focused Hx:** **onset — sudden or gradual**; **number and pattern: a few new floaters versus a sudden shower or "soot"**; **flashes of light, and whether they occur in the dark or on eye movement**; **any curtain, shadow, or field loss, and from which direction it advanced**; **central vision** (macular involvement changes urgency and prognosis); pain and photophobia; **myopia — a significant risk factor for retinal detachment**; previous retinal detachment in either eye or in family; **cataract or other intraocular surgery**; **diabetes and its retinopathy status**; trauma; anticoagulation; immunosuppression.
- **Examination:** **visual acuity each eye**; **visual fields to confrontation — a field defect corresponding to the reported curtain supports detachment**; **pupils and RAPD** (an RAPD suggests a large detachment or other significant retinal or optic nerve pathology); **red reflex** — reduced or absent in vitreous haemorrhage; **dilated fundoscopy** where the skill and equipment allow, looking for the detached retina (grey, mobile, with folds), pigment or haemorrhage in the vitreous, and retinal tears — recognising that **peripheral tears are frequently not visible on direct ophthalmoscopy, so a normal view does not exclude one**; assessment for signs of uveitis.
- **First-line Ix:** **the investigation is a dilated slit-lamp examination with indirect ophthalmoscopy and scleral indentation by an ophthalmologist** — this is a referral, not a test an intern performs. **B-scan ultrasound** where the fundal view is obscured by vitreous haemorrhage or dense cataract. **Glucose/HbA1c** where diabetic retinopathy is the likely source; **FBC, CRP and inflammatory or infective serology** where uveitis is suspected.

> [!danger] **The triage rule.** **New floaters with flashes, or with any field loss, curtain or shadow, need same-day ophthalmology assessment** — a retinal tear treated before detachment is a straightforward laser procedure, whereas a macula-off detachment often leaves permanent visual loss even after successful surgery. **New floaters without flashes or field loss** still need review, but the timeframe is set by local pathways. **Do not reassure a patient with new floaters on the basis of normal acuity** — acuity is preserved until the macula detaches, which is exactly the window in which intervention works.
